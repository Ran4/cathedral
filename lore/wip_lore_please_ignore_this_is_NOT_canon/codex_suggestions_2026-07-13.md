# Ten Lore Engines for Ombreval

*Suggestions only — 2026-07-13. None of this is canon yet. AGENT: DO NOT use it in any way, shape or form!*

The Second Sun works as a model because it is not merely a strange object with a backstory. It changes doctrine, law, slang, childhood games, architecture, factions, daily behavior, and what the player can actually do. The suggestions below aim for the same quality: each is a subject around which a very large lore corpus could eventually be built, and each could produce gameplay rather than only explanatory text.

The established names in `lore/second_sun` remain useful here. These proposals may use Ombreval, the Lanthorn, the Serle and the Cut, the five squares, the Candor, Saint Maren's church, the Ilvane Chapel, the Gradine, and other existing places without requiring every detail of the Second Sun plot. The proposals are deliberately compatible with one another. They are also deliberately unfinished. Questions, tensions, and possible document families are identified; the hundreds of pages that would answer them are not written here.

## 2. The Commonwealth of Bells

### The central idea

Ombreval is governed audibly. Bells announce hours, fires, floods, deaths, curfew, market openings, legal assemblies, arrivals, victories, public shame, and calls for help. Each bell has a name, a recognizable voice, an owner, a jurisdiction, and a history. Citizens can often tell what is happening without seeing it because the towers speak across roofs and down streets.

The existing names — Great Ambrelle called Gravemouth, Maren Smallvoice, the Orphan, the Scold — already suggest a much larger body of lore. The important expansion would be to treat bells not as background ambience but as civic instruments. The right to ring is a form of power. A monastery, ward, guild, or watch tower may own bronze yet lack permission to sound particular patterns. One peal can legally open a market; another can close the gates; a third obliges able-bodied citizens to bring hooks and buckets. A bell rung falsely can cause a crush, conceal a burglary, ruin a day's trade, or amount to treason.

Every bell is also a material biography. It was commissioned for a reason, cast from identifiable bronze, blessed under a particular name, cracked or recast, moved between towers, and heard during events that survive in family memory. Some contain metal donated by hundreds of households. Some are hated because they rang during an occupation or plague. A bell can be politically disputed without being magical: two institutions may each claim that the bronze of an older communal bell belongs to them.

### Why it can sustain a large lore corpus

Bells join timekeeping, geography, authority, music, metallurgy, labor, religion, and oral history. A full corpus could give every major bell a life, motto, pitch, reach, customary patterns, and conflicting popular reputation. Bellfounding could have its own dynasties, secret recipes, occupational injuries, patron saints, and vocabulary. Ringers would form a distinct social world of tower access, rope discipline, inherited rhythm, and intimate knowledge of the city's acoustics.

There is room for law as well as folklore. Who may command the Ruin? Must a death knell be paid for, and who pays when the dead are poor? Can a ward refuse to ring for a ruler it considers illegitimate? How are conflicting signals resolved when fire and curfew coincide? Does hearing a bell create a legal obligation even if the hearer misunderstood it? Records could include peal books, casting contracts, tower inspection reports, fines for false ringing, musical notation, witness statements, and letters complaining that a rival parish's new bell drowns the market bell.

Folk culture would be enormous: children imitating peals, proverbs about particular bells, claims about how far Gravemouth can be heard, funeral customs, weather predictions, ringer superstitions, and memories fixed to sounds. Citizens might date an event not by year but as “the winter before the Orphan cracked.” People who grew up in different quarters would hear the same city differently.

### Gameplay possibilities

The first gameplay layer is navigation without a minimap. Each square and church can have an acoustic signature. A player emerging from a covered passage can orient by locating the Tallage bell, the water, and the Lanthorn's ring. Directions become diegetic: “keep Smallvoice behind your left shoulder until you hear the Yard bell clear of the roofs.” This supports sighted play and could become the spine of a serious audio-accessibility mode.

The second layer is schedule. Bells cause actual state changes: stalls open, gates close, workers move, clergy assemble, debt comes due, a funeral route begins, or a curfew patrol leaves the Bellstand. NPCs should respond because they heard a signal, not because an invisible global timer changed. An actor indoors or too far away may miss it and act on old information. That produces believable mistakes and opportunities.

The third layer is interpretation and intervention. The player can learn peals, climb towers, assist ringers, carry authorization, or discover that a signal was wrong. A short sequence heard through rain might be ambiguous between flood and fire. Witnesses in different parts of the city heard different subsets because roofs and distance masked strokes. Reconstructing a disputed ringing could become an investigation based on position and sound rather than highlighted clues.

Eventually, allowing the player to pull a rope gives the system teeth. Ringing should not be a free musical toy with no consequences. Even one unauthorized stroke of Gravemouth would be a city event. Smaller practice bells or handbells can provide safe experimentation; tower bells require access, knowledge, physical timing, and acceptance of what the city will infer.

### A possible full expansion

- A bell canon listing every instrument, acoustic fact, owner, legal signal, and historical change.
- Aural maps for several weather states and listening positions.
- The ringers' occupational bible, bellfounding lore, and named living crews.
- A civic code of peals plus edge cases, disputed precedents, and famous false alarms.
- Sound assets for bells, ropes, frames, tower interiors, practice strokes, and distant reflections.
- Diegetic peal books, casting accounts, memorial inscriptions, and trial depositions.
- Scheduled scenarios: funeral, market opening, fire drill, cracked bell, unauthorized ringing.

The boundary is clarity. The system can be culturally intricate without making every peal an opaque puzzle. Citizens have lived with this language all their lives; the world should teach it through repetition, behavior, and context.

## 3. The Serle Remembers Its Old Course

### The central idea

The Serle has been straightened into the Cut, embanked, bridged, dredged, taxed, and forced through Ombreval — but the old river still determines the city. Former channels survive beneath streets and courtyards. Cellars flood along vanished bends. Property boundaries follow banks that no longer exist. Wells taste different on opposite sides of buried water. During exceptional floods the river briefly redraws its older map.

“The river remembers” can remain a boatman's phrase rather than an established supernatural fact. Engineers use it to mean that water finds old weakness. Lawyers use it because ancient rights were granted relative to channels that have moved. Families use it when a drowned object or body appears far from where it entered. Priests at Saint Maren's use it in sermons. Children hear a simpler warning: the Cut is what the city calls the river; the Serle is what the river calls itself.

This creates a broad lore subject covering the whole watershed without needing the game to leave Ombreval. Upstream wool, downstream salt, tolls, mills, fisheries, waste, drinking water, ferries, bridges, embankments, and flood defenses all meet inside the walls. The canal is not scenery at the city's edge. It is a contested working system on which food, heat, sanitation, and trade depend.

### Why it can sustain a large lore corpus

Water generates institutions. There can be a Cut authority, dredgers, lock keepers, polemen, boat families, fishers, laundresses, tanners, mill owners, customs officers, bridge wardens, and parish clergy who all claim different expertise. Their interests conflict cleanly. Dredging helps barges but may undermine old foundations. Closing a sluice protects the Tallage while flooding Maren's Green. Clean-water rules help brewers and harm upstream trades. A new bridge can enrich one square and destroy another landing.

The legal history could be gloriously difficult: lost islands whose ownership still affects rent; houses with rights to a “river door” now opening into a cellar wall; mills owing payment in measures of water; an old ferry privilege that may prohibit a modern bridge; and a boundary stone recovered from the river that changes which ward taxes a row of shops. Hydrological facts can anchor political stories without one official interpretation of justice.

There is also material and folk history. Flood marks on walls form a public chronology. Bridge names preserve collapses, saints, toll scandals, suicides, duels, and old crossings. Boatmen possess a vocabulary for currents that townspeople cannot hear. Saint Maren's flooded crypt can sit at the center of customs for the drowned. Work songs, launch blessings, river curses, children's games, eel recipes, salvage law, and warnings about winter ice all belong here.

A full expansion might cover the Serle from the Combs to Salorge, but always through objects and people arriving in the city: cargo marks, foreign mud on a hull, news brought upstream, a wool bale wet at a known ford, or salt prices changed by a storm nobody in Ombreval saw.

### Gameplay possibilities

The smallest implementation makes the river an information and commerce route. Barges arrive on schedules carrying named goods and rumors. Market stock and NPC goals change with delayed or damaged cargo. Players can follow a particular bale, letter, passenger, or stolen object from landing to warehouse. Bridges and towpaths become active places rather than scenic connectors.

Seasonal and weather-driven water levels can change exploration. A low Cut exposes steps, mooring rings, culverts, and an old arch. High water closes a cellar route, floods Saint Maren's crypt, moves boats closer to upper landings, and makes some bridges dangerous. These changes should be few and legible; even a couple of alternate states could make the city feel responsive.

The old course can support investigations. Damp lines, subsidence, old maps, property claims, and local testimony let the player reconstruct where water once ran. The answer may open a physical route through linked cellars or reveal why a building keeps failing. This is a way to make procedural urban layers genuinely meaningful: the generator can create an earlier river path first, then build the present city over it.

Social gameplay follows naturally. During a threatened flood, every ward argues for a different sluice decision. The player can carry warnings, help move goods, persuade a stubborn household, expose a false alarm intended to manipulate grain prices, or simply choose where to be when the water rises. NPCs remember whether the player saved a person, a reliquary, a ledger, or an expensive cargo — and judge the priority.

### A possible full expansion

- A hydrological canon: present Cut, old channels, drains, wells, flood levels, and what the simulation enforces.
- A river gazetteer of bridges, landings, sluices, mills, cellars, and vanished crossings.
- Boat families, water trades, ward authorities, and rival economic interests.
- Flood annals, salvage cases, toll books, cargo manifests, bridge charters, and boatmen's oral culture.
- A seasonal trade table linking arrivals to markets and NPC routines.
- Two or three explorable water states, each with route and schedule consequences.
- A bounded flood storyline with no morally perfect engineering answer.

The boundary is that the river should feel physical before it feels mythic. If every coincidence proves that the Serle is literally conscious, the legal, economic, and environmental richness collapses into one magical answer.

## 4. Five Cities Inside One Wall

### The central idea

Ombreval calls itself one free city, but daily life is organized through several smaller civic worlds. The Wickmarket, Coswald's Yard, the Tallage, Maren's Green, and the Bellstand are not merely differently dressed plazas. Each anchors a ward with its own trades, institutions, patronage, routes, food, sounds, grudges, standards of respectability, and account of what Ombreval fundamentally is.

To the Wickmarket, the city may be a chain of household flames supplied and tended. To Coswald's Yard, it is stone, work, and things built to outlast talk. To the Tallage, it is a set of weights, contracts, arrivals, and prices. To Maren's Green, it is water, hunger, weather, and mutual obligation. To the Bellstand, it is law proclaimed in public and obeyed within hearing. These are tendencies, not rigid personality types. A ward becomes credible through internal disagreement: old money and day laborers, respectable guild officers and illicit practitioners, natives and newcomers, pious customs and profane jokes.

The citywide conflict is productive. Every ward believes its labor supports the others and is insufficiently honored. Processions argue over precedence. Tax burdens are compared. A bridge closure helps one market and hurts another. Saints, public fountains, watch patrols, fire equipment, and festival money become subjects of civic competition. In crisis, the five wards may act like rival towns — until an outsider insults Ombreval and discovers their unity.

### Why it can sustain a large lore corpus

This is perhaps the most directly expandable proposal because every district can receive its own small encyclopedia. A ward bible can define its physical boundaries, demographic mixtures, principal trades, important households, daily schedule, smells and sounds, building forms, religious sites, food, clothing, insults, hospitality customs, oral history, and relationships to every other ward. Street-by-street gazetteers can then grow beneath it without losing coherence.

Faction design becomes more grounded. Not every conflict needs to be Church versus heresy or rich versus poor. A Tallage customs clerk and a Tallage smuggler may share a fierce ward loyalty. Masons in Coswald's Yard disagree about wages while agreeing that glaziers receive too much glory. A prosperous fish merchant at Maren's Green may be economically powerful but still excluded from Bellstand respectability. These overlapping identities make LLM actors less likely to reduce themselves to one faction label.

This subject also supports language. Directions, metaphors, accents, preferred oaths, jokes, and terms for outsiders can vary by ward. So can common knowledge: everyone knows a bridge collapsed, but only one quarter remembers who opened their doors that night. Contradictory ward chronicles let history be political without making basic facts unusable.

The corpus could include ward charters, tax rolls, guild lists, neighborhood petitions, procession disputes, market regulations, tavern songs, recipes, street-name etymologies, watch reports, and household maps. Much of it would be exceptional NPC prompt fodder because it answers a practical question: what does this particular person assume about the few streets they know best?

### Gameplay possibilities

Procedural generation can use ward briefs to make culture visible. Street width, façade projection, materials, shopfront density, courtyards, bridge frequency, lantern coverage, and ambient occupation can vary according to district history rather than a generic random seed. The player learns where they are through architecture and activity before seeing a sign.

Socially, reputation should propagate through actual ward networks instead of a displayed five-bar meter. Help a household at Maren's Green and their relatives, neighbors, creditors, and parish contacts know. That does not mean the whole district instantly approves. Different actors interpret the same act through their interests. The useful game state is who heard what and which relationships carried it, with ward identity shaping the retelling.

Ward events can move populations through the city: market days, a saint's procession, a guild vote, a brawl over precedence, a water shortage, a public punishment, or a contest for festival honors. These events give the five squares changing purposes and allow the player to encounter the same NPC in different social contexts.

This structure is also ideal for a player learning the city. Early errands stay within one ward and teach its landmarks. Later tasks cross boundaries and expose incompatible assumptions. A supposedly simple delivery becomes difficult because the Tallage recipient expects a written tally while a Yard sender considers his spoken mark sufficient. The player gains competence by learning cultures, not by raising an abstract diplomacy statistic.

### A possible full expansion

- One citywide canon defining ward boundaries, institutions, shared facts, and major dependencies.
- Five ward bibles of equal depth, each written from inside rather than as a tourist guide.
- Relationship maps among households, trades, parishes, and civic officers.
- Ward-specific common-knowledge and rumor pools for NPC injection.
- Architectural and ambient-generation briefs tied to each district's history.
- Competing chronicles of several citywide events.
- A year of ward events and three conflicts whose consequences cross boundaries.

The boundary is caricature. The five wards should produce different priors, not five monocultures. People marry across them, move, conceal their origins, work in one and sleep in another, and belong to institutions whose boundaries do not match the map.

## 5. The Law of Earshot

### The central idea

In Ombreval, much of the law is spoken. A promise can become a debt when uttered in the proper form, at the proper place or hour, before people competent to hear it. Market bargains, apprenticeship vows, marriage settlements, guild admissions, confessions, testimony, and public challenges all depend on words and witnesses. Writing matters, but a document is often a record that words were spoken rather than a substitute for them.

This turns the game's voice and hearing systems into part of the world's jurisprudence. Distance matters. Privacy matters. Whether a person was addressed directly, speaking to a square, whispering through a grate, or merely overheard matters. Covered passages, windows, screens, balconies, and crowds become legal terrain. A phrase said under the Bellstand might carry a force it lacks in a tavern corner. A deadline might be “before Lamplight” rather than an interface timer.

The law should not pretend that automatic transcription is infallible. Ombreval has developed procedures precisely because human hearing is unreliable: repeated formulae, response phrases, hand signs, named witnesses, notaries who recite terms back, bells that mark the moment, and opportunities to challenge a word immediately. The city's deepest legal arguments concern not only what was said but what could reasonably have been heard.

### Why it can sustain a large lore corpus

This subject can contain an entire civic legal culture. There can be distinct courts for market disputes, guild discipline, church matters, water rights, inheritance, and serious crimes. It needs judges, beadles, advocates, notaries, criers, professional witnesses, oath-helpers, interpreters, jailers, and disreputable people who rent their ears. Each profession brings vocabulary, ethics, corruption, and social position.

The law can grow through famous precedents. Was a promise binding when the bell began ringing halfway through it? Can a deaf bellfounder witness by feeling speech and watching mouths? Does a confession overheard through a damaged screen remain protected? If a crier misspeaks an edict but the posted text is correct, which version governs until corrected? Can a person withdraw an offer after the crowd has audibly accepted it? These cases are both lore and design tests.

Diegetic writing would include charters, oath books, court rolls, witness depositions, debt tallies, apprenticeship indentures, pardons, marriage contracts, lawyers' glosses, prison petitions, broadsheets summarizing scandalous trials, and private letters advising someone exactly which words not to say. Folk culture contributes courtroom proverbs, children's mock trials, insults for informants, and widespread but incorrect beliefs about legal phrases.

The Candor's existing language — oaths “by the light that does not lie” — can remain one powerful strand, but the legal system should not depend on the Second Sun mystery. Merchants, wards, guilds, and boatfolk can have older forms. This permits conflicts between sacred and civic authority without making either a modern secular court in costume.

### Gameplay possibilities

The most natural mechanic is the spoken promise. An NPC asks the player to deliver an item, return before a bell, keep a confidence, or repay a small loan. If the player explicitly accepts, the world records the terms in a bounded form. Keeping or breaking that word changes specific relationships and rumors. There need be no quest log pretending to be absent; NPCs can remind the player, and notaries or personal tokens can provide diegetic records.

Trials and hearings then become advanced conversations using existing actor memories. Witnesses testify to what they actually perceived, with gaps and distortions. The player can question them, locate contradictory physical evidence, find another hearer, or argue that the acoustic situation made certainty impossible. A court should never ask an unconstrained LLM to decide the whole game state. Objective facts, procedural stages, and possible verdict ranges belong in code; actors provide testimony, judgment, persuasion, and bias.

Earshot also creates stealth and architecture gameplay without crouch takedowns. The player seeks a private place for a bargain, ensures a third party is present, listens from an overhead bridge, closes a shutter, or deliberately speaks loudly enough for a crowd. NPCs can warn the player when a formula is about to carry legal force. Misunderstanding becomes a recoverable social event, not an invisible parser trap.

Items matter as tokens of speech: split tally sticks, seals, knotted cords, witness coins, pledged tools, and written abstracts. Offering such an item can open the correct conversational procedure. This uses the existing item-transfer system while giving objects legible legal functions.

### A possible full expansion

- A legal canon separating objective game state, recognized procedure, popular misconception, and disputed doctrine.
- Court and office bibles, with jurisdiction maps and living personnel.
- A book of oath and contract forms written for actual spoken use.
- Twenty or more precedent cases, each testing a different hearing or timing edge case.
- Witness, notary, advocate, debtor, guild, and clergy prompt packs.
- A promise-state design with explicit acceptance, reminders, renegotiation, and graceful STT failure.
- Several hearings ranging from a loaf dispute to a politically dangerous public case.

The boundary is consent and comprehensibility. Casual player speech must not silently create major obligations. The moment a game-state promise is formed should be unmistakable in dialogue and recoverable through clarification.

## 6. The Roof Republic

### The central idea

There is another Ombreval above eye level: roof ridges, gutters, parapets, overhead bridges, scaffold walks, belfry passages, loft doors, drying platforms, chimneys, and narrow rights of way across adjoining buildings. It is not a secret fantasy city. It is a dangerous working landscape used by tilewalkers, roofers, chimney children, bell crews, fire watchers, pigeon keepers, messengers, thieves, servants hanging cloth, and residents escaping the press of the streets.

“The Roof Republic” is a street nickname for the web of customs governing this upper city. No single authority owns it. Access depends on ladders, household permission, guild privilege, old agreements, and toleration. A bridge between houses may be private on most days but become a public fire route when the Ruin rings. A gutter can establish a property boundary. Blocking a long-used roof path might isolate three households from the nearest tower or water hoist. The people who maintain these routes believe the city survives because they can cross above a street jam.

The Roof Republic has its own geography. Street names are inadequate from above, so workers navigate by ridges, finials, chimneys, bell voices, and lines of sight. A roof that appears adjacent from the ground may be separated by an impassable drop; a covered passage may secretly join two blocks. The cathedral towers are always visible but do not necessarily tell you how to reach them.

### Why it can sustain a large lore corpus

Vertical space creates occupations and institutions absent from conventional street-level worldbuilding. A tilewalkers' guild can regulate training, rescue, roof damage, and courier routes. Fire watchers and bell crews overlap with civic authority. Householders contest access. Children and the poor may know paths that officials do not. Wealthy residents build private galleries that appropriate older communal ways.

Lore could define roof law, hand signals, route classifications, safety rituals, footwear, tools, weather knowledge, fall customs, rescue equipment, and the social stigma or prestige attached to high work. Named routes can acquire histories: the Widow's Seven, the Red Ridge, Ambrelle's Step, the long slate run above Cinder Row. Memorial marks at fall sites and jokes about ground-bound trades give the culture emotional depth.

Documents might include route licenses, roof-access covenants, fire plans, damage claims, courier tallies, apprentice tests, forbidden-route lists, architectural sketches, and a clandestine guide copied by thieves. Oral material could include counting rhythms for jumps, warnings shouted between roofs, work songs, weather sayings, and stories about impossible crossings that may be exaggerations.

This proposal also makes the city's architecture narratable. A procedural block is no longer only façade variation. It has ownership, access, maintenance, and a place in a traversable network. The lore corpus can set rules by which generated rooftop routes receive plausible social meanings.

### Gameplay possibilities

The obvious core is traversal. A continuous but demanding network can connect major districts through roof ridges, plank bridges, interior lofts, and controlled drops. Learning it offers a different mental map of Ombreval. It should be useful rather than simply faster: street gates, crowds, flooded lanes, or curfew patrols may make the roof route preferable, while rain, ice, repair work, or hostile householders can make it worse.

Courier work gives the network purpose. Deliver a light parcel without touching street level, carry a bell instruction across a processional crowd, fetch a roof doctor after a fall, or guide an apprentice through a qualification route. NPC directions use upper landmarks. The player earns access through trust and training rather than unlocking glowing grapple points.

Observation becomes another mechanic. Roof workers witness events in courtyards and upper windows that street NPCs miss. The player can follow smoke to a fire, watch a procession's route, track a messenger, or locate an address by a household's laundry mark. Conversely, citizens below can see a conspicuous person crossing a skyline. Roof travel should not confer universal invisibility.

The existing flying support should remain a developer tool unless deliberately fictionalized. The Roof Republic offers authored vertical exploration with danger, gates, and social meaning. Flight can still help developers inspect it, but normal play becomes richer when the player must understand the network.

### A possible full expansion

- A vertical atlas of public, private, emergency, guild, and illicit routes.
- Occupational bibles for tilewalkers, fire watchers, messengers, and roof-maintaining households.
- Roof law, access customs, weather practices, jargon, signals, and memorial traditions.
- A generator specification for traversable edges and their social ownership.
- Named route trials, courier jobs, rescues, disputes, and seasonal closures.
- Prompt blocks describing what each rooftop NPC knows and what they can see from work.
- Diegetic route sketches and licenses that are useful but never perfect minimaps.

The boundary is physical credibility. The roofs may be exhilarating, but they should still feel steep, fragile, wet, inhabited, and capable of causing harm. A universal parkour playground would erase the households beneath it.

## 7. The Houses Behind the Doors

### The central idea

Every façade in Ombreval implies a household, and a medieval household is more than a modern family in a private home. It can include kin, apprentices, servants, lodgers, business partners, dependents, workshop laborers, animals, and people informally sleeping near an oven or under a stair. Work and domestic life overlap. A shop opens onto a room where somebody also eats, argues, stores winter fuel, receives creditors, and watches a sick relative.

This lore engine asks the world to take those implied lives seriously. Houses differ not just by visual theme but by household economy, stage of prosperity, tenure, inheritance, trade, fire risk, water access, and relationships. The threshold becomes socially important. Being addressed in the street, admitted to the shop, invited to the hall, allowed upstairs, and trusted near a bedchamber are distinct levels of access.

Doors and walls also carry public marks. A tax chalk, guild sign, parish blessing, plague memory, fire bucket, debtor's notice, household emblem, or funeral sprig tells a practiced citizen something before anyone speaks. The city can be read at walking speed. What is visible is never the whole truth: a prosperous sign may conceal debt, while a mean doorway may lead to a thriving workshop kept deliberately inconspicuous.

### Why it can sustain a large lore corpus

Domestic life is effectively inexhaustible and makes grand lore credible. A full corpus could define building and room types, heating, light, cooking, sleeping, storage, hygiene, waste, laundry, childbirth, illness, courtship, mourning, privacy, hospitality, rent, subletting, workshop organization, household religion, pet and livestock keeping, and relations between masters and dependents. Each subject produces class, ward, seasonal, and occupational variations.

Household generation also yields strong NPC context. Instead of giving an actor only an occupation and secret, give them an address, co-residents, responsibilities, resources, recurring disputes, and a room they regard as theirs. A fish seller worried about her brother missing from the Cut, an apprentice sleeping beneath a cutting table, and an elderly lodger whose rent is paid by copying letters all have immediate reasons to act.

Diegetic documents abound: inventories, wills, rent books, marriage settlements, household accounts, recipes, remedy notes, devotional cards, children's exercises, letters hidden in rafters, pawn tickets, fire inspections, and lists of debts written where only the family understands the marks. Oral culture includes table prayers, bedtime stories, family nicknames, household sayings, lullabies, complaints passed through party walls, and rules for guests.

This can expand address by address for years without requiring every building to be equally authored. Procedural household templates provide a baseline; selected houses receive deep histories; notable objects and relationships can migrate between them.

### Gameplay possibilities

At first, only some doors need open. The important change is that interiors are social spaces, not loot containers. An NPC may invite the player into a workshop to continue a private conversation, ask for help moving an object, offer a meal, hide someone, or refuse entry because the household is in mourning. The player's relationship with one resident does not automatically grant trust from everyone inside.

Interiors change voice gameplay. Walls and shutters bound who hears a conversation. An argument upstairs may be faintly audible in the shop. Speaking loudly in a crowded household creates several witnesses. A private promise requires finding privacy, not selecting a menu option. Domestic schedules also let NPCs move between credible roles over a day: stallholder, parent, guild member, parishioner, sleeper.

Items become useful when households have needs. Firewood, food, lamp oil, cloth, medicine, tools, rent money, and letters carry understandable stakes. The player can help without becoming the owner of every problem. Small acts — returning a borrowed pan, bringing news, replacing a broken latch — can expose relationships and earn access more naturally than generic affinity points.

If interiors are generated from façade footprints and windows, the lore can guide the constraints. A narrow projecting house may have a shop below, hall and hearth above, and rented loft. A court building may share a well and privy among several households, creating dependencies visible in both architecture and actor goals.

### A possible full expansion

- A household canon covering tenure, kinship, labor, privacy, access, domestic religion, and material standards.
- Interior-generation grammars by building form, ward, wealth, and occupation.
- Several deeply authored example households with relationship maps and daily rhythms.
- Door-mark, shop-sign, fire-safety, and threshold-custom catalogues.
- Domestic object lists, recipes, accounts, letters, inventories, and oral traditions.
- Prompt assembly that gives each actor household knowledge without exposing everybody's secrets.
- A staged implementation plan: workshops first, then shared courts, then ordinary residences.

The boundary is voyeurism and repetition. Opening every door is valuable only if interiors feel inhabited and if access has meaning. Hundreds of interchangeable rooms full of collectible clutter would make the city feel less alive, not more.

## 8. Everything Has Passed Through the Tallage

### The central idea

Objects in Ombreval have social lives. A knife is not merely “knife”; it was made by a known workshop, repaired with a mismatched pin, pledged for a debt, carried on a salt barge, used at a wedding feast, pawned at the Tallage, and recognized by someone who should not have seen it. Goods move among people, and their provenance can be true, mistaken, forged, concealed, or transformed into legend.

The Tallage and its pawnshops are the obvious center, but the subject reaches the entire city. Guild marks claim workmanship. Customs stamps prove or pretend that toll was paid. Repair styles identify neighborhoods. Households mark linens and bowls. Relics have chains of custody. Stolen property is altered for resale. Gifts create obligations that survive resale. Cheap pilgrim badges imitate sacred forms. A used object may be more desirable because of who owned it, or nearly unsellable for the same reason.

This lore engine would make material culture part of narrative memory. Actors should care not only what an item is worth in abstract currency but what it means for this person to offer this item to that person in public. Bread, tools, letters, keys, tokens, clothing, devotional objects, cargo, and scraps of old building stone all participate in different economies.

### Why it can sustain a large lore corpus

Material culture opens dozens of document families. There can be workshop catalogues, maker dynasties, guild marks, customs registers, pawn ledgers, bills of lading, household inventories, wills, salvage lists, relic authentications, repair manuals, price tables, sumptuary rules, stolen-goods notices, and collectors' correspondence. Each trade requires its own knowledge: a glazier reads glass, a boatman reads cargo damage, a pawnbroker reads desperation, and a priest reads whether an object is fit for sacred use.

The economy becomes narratively specific. Money is only one dimension. Liquidity, season, reputation, legality, sentiment, and danger affect value. A mason's best tool cannot be replaced by its coin price before tomorrow's shift. A widow may refuse a valuable cup because accepting it acknowledges a disputed debt. A child values a worthless lead figure because it was the last gift from a missing parent. These are excellent inputs for smart actors because judgment depends on backstory rather than a universal shop table.

Counterfeiting and provenance disputes provide mysteries with grounded evidence. Marks can be copied, papers altered, and honest memories wrong. The game can preserve uncertainty without arbitrary solutions by encoding an objective item history, then giving characters incomplete perspectives. A famous relic need not be conclusively holy for it to have a traceable sequence of owners.

Folk lore follows goods along trade routes. Wool from Brede, salt from Salorge, old coins, foreign buckles, and river salvage bring stories beyond the walls into the city. The same type of item can acquire district-specific meanings through how it is used and repaired.

### Gameplay possibilities

The current offer-and-accept interaction becomes dramatically richer if items carry stable identity and provenance. Give a person an object, and it can later appear in another hand with the transfer remembered or misremembered. Actors gossip about an inappropriate gift, recognize stolen property, ask why the player has a dead person's token, or value a familiar tool above a generic replacement.

Small investigations can center on tracing objects. A pawn ticket leads to a shop; a repair mark leads to a craft household; dried canal mud suggests a landing; a customs stamp conflicts with a barge's arrival date. The player solves by speaking to people with different expertise and physically carrying or showing the object. No magical detective vision is required.

Commerce can remain conversational and bounded. Haggling, credit, loans, gifts, pledges, and exchanges are different social acts. An actor may accept an item while rejecting the giver's claimed meaning. The player can ask for appraisal, but appraisers have interests and limited knowledge. Prices should be stable enough to play while allowing story-specific exceptions.

Objects also preserve choices across sessions. A returned tool, recopied letter, broken seal, or repurposed gift is a concrete memory more affecting than a global reputation score. The most important items need not be unique quest artifacts; ordinary things become important because of where they have been.

### A possible full expansion

- A material-culture canon defining item identity, marks, condition, ownership, custody, and knowledge.
- Trade and craft catalogues with plausible ordinary objects, prices, repairs, and maker conventions.
- Tallage institutions: customs, pawnbrokers, brokers, carriers, fences, appraisers, and courts.
- Provenance-aware prompt rules separating objective history from what each actor knows.
- Ledgers, tickets, manifests, inventories, maker's marks, wanted notices, and relic papers.
- A handful of authored object biographies crossing several households and wards.
- System designs for showing, offering, inspecting, repairing, pledging, and transferring items.

The boundary is database burden. Not every loaf needs a six-generation biography. Deep provenance belongs to durable or narratively touched objects; ordinary fungible goods can remain batches until an event makes one particular item matter.

## 9. The Parish of the Dead

### The central idea

The dead remain part of Ombreval's social order. Their bodies occupy scarce ground; their wills direct money; their names are rung, chalked, carved, prayed, inherited, erased, and occasionally restored. Chantries employ the living to remember them. Guilds march at members' funerals. Houses display mourning. Streets and bridges preserve dead names long after the official reason is forgotten. A citizen can be politically and economically active for decades after burial through an endowment, disputed inheritance, unfinished obligation, or reputation defended by descendants.

“The Parish of the Dead” is not necessarily an actual administrative parish, though people might use the phrase. It names the overlapping institutions that manage memory: Saint Maren's church and its flooded crypt, cathedral tombs, charnel houses, graveyards, guild burial funds, poor funerals, plague pits, execution ground, family vaults, relic custodians, bell ringers, stonecutters, and clerks maintaining the books of names.

The lore should begin with ordinary death rather than generic horror. Who washes a body, who carries it, who pays, how quickly burial occurs, what happens when the ground is full, how mourning changes work, and which neighbors bring food? Ghost stories and disputed miracles can grow from those practices, but the dead feel present because the city continually performs their absence.

### Why it can sustain a large lore corpus

Death touches every class and institution while exposing inequality clearly. The rich commission stone and masses; the poor may depend on a communal fund for a knell and chalked name. Foreigners, criminals, drowned people, unidentified bodies, infants, plague victims, clergy, and craft masters may each receive different treatment. These differences generate moral and political conflict without requiring villainous caricatures.

A full corpus could include burial law, cemetery history, funeral trades, mourning customs, guild funds, wills, inheritance patterns, chantry finance, epitaph styles, relic translation, exhumation procedure, plague response, and beliefs about memory. The physical city gains a chronological layer: reused grave slabs, displaced bones, sealed crypts, tombs incorporated into later walls, and memorials whose inscriptions were intentionally altered.

Documents would be plentiful and naturally playable: necrologies, wills, burial registers, chantry accounts, epitaph collections, coroner-like depositions, grave relocation notices, letters of condolence, funeral invitations, petitions over disputed bones, and lists of names copied before water destroys a page. Oral culture includes laments, wakes, jokes by grave diggers, children's ghost stories, prayers, death omens, and family versions of public scandals.

The central epistemic tension is memory rather than proof of an afterlife. Institutions claim the right to say who the dead were. Families disagree. Records are partial. A person officially remembered as a benefactor may have been hated at home. A nameless body may be recognized by an object that several people have motives to misidentify.

### Gameplay possibilities

Funerals are moving city events. Bells sound, shops close selectively, a route forms from house to church to grave, guild members assemble, debts are discussed, and estranged people occupy the same space. The player may carry a candle, deliver news, find a missing mourner, prevent a procession from taking a flooded route, or simply attend and learn relationships from who stands where.

Wills and memorial obligations create quests that do not require tomb raiding. Locate a named witness, return a pledged tool, determine which chapel an endowment belongs to, identify why a knell was rung with the wrong count, or help copy a water-damaged register before names disappear. Items with provenance connect directly: seals, rings, clothing repairs, and pawned possessions can help identify a dead person, but evidence remains socially contested.

The system also supports persistent NPC memory. When an actor dies, leaves, or is otherwise removed, the world need not reset as if they never existed. Other actors remember them differently. Their possessions move. Their goals become obligations, abandoned tasks, or sources of relief. A named death changes a household, market stall, debt network, and set of daily routes.

Ghost stories can produce exploration without confirming ghosts. NPCs direct the player to Gaunt Passage at a certain bell or to a flooded crypt after a name-knell. The player may find a physical explanation, ambiguous evidence, deliberate fraud, or nothing. The city continues to tell the story either way.

### A possible full expansion

- A death-and-memory canon: institutions, burial grounds, objective procedures, and deliberately unresolved beliefs.
- Funeral customs by class, ward, guild, age, circumstance, and season.
- A necrological timeline connecting past disasters to visible graves and current families.
- Wills, registers, accounts, epitaphs, petitions, letters, laments, and ghost-story pools.
- Funeral and mourning schedules that alter NPC goals, shops, bells, and routes.
- Several authored dead whose possessions and reputations remain active in the present.
- A persistence design for death consequences without turning the city into a mass-casualty simulator.

The boundary is tone. The subject should deepen attachment to ordinary citizens, not become a constant parade of grisly revelations. Restraint, ritual, paperwork, and absence can carry more weight than exposed bones.

## 10. The Year Walks Through the Streets

### The central idea

Ombreval's calendar is a city-scale machine. Feast days, fasts, market cycles, guild observances, legal terms, rent days, river seasons, building seasons, pilgrim surges, weddings, funerals, fairs, and processions change how streets are used. Time is visible because people move objects through space: shutters close, cloth appears in windows, temporary stalls fill a square, a relic leaves its chapel, greenery is tied to doors, scaffold work pauses, bonfires occupy paving, and a procession claims a route normally used by carts.

The important idea is not “lots of festivals.” It is that each part of society has a calendar and the calendars collide. The Candor's solemn day may be the tavern trade's best night. A guild holiday shifts work onto apprentices. A fast changes fish prices at Maren's Green. A legal term fills the Bellstand with petitioners. A river thaw brings boats and strangers. Roofers race the wet season. Households plan marriages between rent days and mourning anniversaries.

There is no single universally accepted account of what a day means. Official liturgy, ward custom, trade necessity, and family memory overlap. The same procession is sacred duty to a canon, lost income to a carrier, flirtation season to apprentices, and an opportunity to a pickpocket. That multiplicity is the lore engine.

### Why it can sustain a large lore corpus

A fully built year could hold hundreds of entries without feeling like filler because each date affects different systems. Major feasts need origins, rituals, routes, music, food, dress, decorations, sermons, civic offices, markets, taboos, scandals, and variant ward practices. Minor observances provide texture: a day when glaziers do not cut red glass, a boat blessing, the opening of roof-work season, a household night for settling small debts, a children's door-to-door rhyme, or an annual inspection of fire hooks.

The calendar connects all other proposed lore engines. Fabric projects follow weather and donations. Bells announce offices and emergencies. Water levels shape river work. Wards compete over processional precedence. Promises mature at named hours. Roof routes close during frost. Households prepare food and receive guests. Objects are exchanged or displayed. The dead are remembered on anniversaries. It is the structure that lets specialized lore appear in play at the correct moment.

Documents could include official calendars, household almanacs, guild yearbooks, market schedules, weather notebooks, processional route books, sermon cycles, feast accounts, menus, song collections, costume inventories, watch orders, and complaints about damage caused by crowds. Oral culture includes mnemonic rhymes for moveable dates, seasonal predictions, festival jokes, rival songs, and stories explaining customs that historians know began for entirely practical reasons.

Chronology also gives NPCs a shared near future. Smart actors are more convincing when they anticipate something together: buying supplies, dreading a deadline, inviting someone, arguing about weather, or remembering what happened at last year's event. A calendar supplies topics and goals without needing a constant crisis plot.

### Gameplay possibilities

At a basic level, time changes ambient life. Different stalls, sounds, clothing details, decorations, and NPC routes appear on particular days. The player who revisits a square finds it serving another purpose. This increases variety while making change predictable enough to learn.

Processions are especially valuable in Ombreval's pinching streets and overhead bridges. A moving crowd reorders navigation, creates vantage points, exposes private tensions, and brings actors from separate networks together. The player might guide a route around collapsed scaffolding, carry an object, find a missing participant, overhear a plot from above, negotiate passage for a delayed barge, or decide whether to warn officials of a threat. The event remains interesting even if the player does nothing because the city performs it.

Deadlines become diegetic and memorable. “Before the Snuffing on the eve of Maren's Wake” is supported by bells, preparations, and NPC reminders. The world should offer recovery: actors can renegotiate, explain what was missed, or respond to a broken promise. A calendar is most useful when it creates consequences, not when it hides content permanently from players who log in at the wrong time.

Real-world time could eventually be an optional mapping, but the lore should first work under an accelerated in-game clock. Major observances can be announced well in advance and recur. Developers can force a date for testing. `fake_backend` scenarios can place the player at controlled points before, during, and after an event.

### A possible full expansion

- A calendar canon distinguishing fixed dates, moveable observances, market cycles, seasons, and exceptional proclamations.
- Twelve month-or-season books covering labor, food, weather, travel, worship, and household activity.
- Major-feast dossiers with routes, participants, assets, sounds, scripts, and branch points.
- Ward, guild, household, and outsider variants of the official year.
- Almanacs, route books, menus, accounts, songs, sermons, notices, and retrospective chronicles.
- Scheduling rules connecting calendar state to NPC goals, locations, stock, bells, and city dressing.
- A small launch calendar with three deeply simulated events rather than fifty shallow holidays.

The boundary is content dilution. A long list of named feast days is not a living year. Each implemented observance should alter at least one place, one group of actors, and one practical decision the player can make.

## A useful way to choose among them

These ideas emphasize different strengths, so the best next corpus depends on what the game most needs:

| If the priority is... | Strongest candidate |
|---|---|
| Making the cathedral itself socially and mechanically central | The Fabric Without End |
| Sound, navigation, schedules, and accessibility | The Commonwealth of Bells |
| Environmental change, trade, and old-city archaeology | The Serle Remembers Its Old Course |
| Better NPC prompt grounding and distinct procedural districts | Five Cities Inside One Wall |
| Turning speech and hearing into consequential gameplay | The Law of Earshot |
| Giving the vertical city a believable exploration culture | The Roof Republic |
| Making interiors and everyday people worth visiting | The Houses Behind the Doors |
| Making item exchange, memory, and investigation interlock | Everything Has Passed Through the Tallage |
| Persistence, inheritance, and emotionally consequential absence | The Parish of the Dead |
| Making time, crowds, and recurring city events coherent | The Year Walks Through the Streets |

They also combine cleanly. The Fabric can commission a bell whose bronze arrives on the Serle; its casting is funded by five rival wards under spoken contracts; a Roof Republic crew raises it; a Tallage pawnbroker traces stolen alloy; a dead founder's will decides ownership; and the consecration procession occurs on a date the entire city has been preparing for. That is the real test of a lore engine: it does not only generate pages about itself, but gives every other part of Ombreval more reasons to exist.
