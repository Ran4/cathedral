No faction should function as a dispenser of the correct lore answer. Each is a community with internal differences, material interests, ethical strengths, and dangerous temptations. The Second Sun is valuable because it makes people reveal what they already believe about truth, authority, memory, and walls.

The Cathedral Chapter

The chapter is the cathedral's governing body: bishop, dean, canons, treasurer, precentor, and offices attached to worship and property. Outsiders often imagine it collectively hiding “the truth.” In reality it contains several philosophies held together by liturgy, revenue, and a shared terror of schism.

Reservists

Reservists teach the doctrine of Reserved Creation. The Second Sun is a genuine divine sign but not a physical rival to the created sun. They want reverent, controlled access and careful language. Their best representatives are humble before mystery. Their worst use mystery to make institutional claims impossible to challenge.

Typical line: “A thing need not occupy the sky to illuminate the world.”

Sufficientists

Named for the Council of Sufficient Light, they minimize attention. Some are skeptics; others believe the phenomenon spiritually dangerous precisely because it encourages fascination over charity. They run soup kitchens, schools, and burial societies and ask why wealthy pilgrims buy lenses while neighbours starve.

Typical line: “You have one sun, one day, and one hungry person before you.”

Conjunctivists

A discreet apocalyptic circle believes ordinary and reserved creation will overlap at a coming conjunction. They disagree whether this means judgment, transfiguration, or reunion. Several senior clerics fund astronomical work while publicly discouraging predictions.

Typical line: “Contradictions are not resolved. They are brought face to face.”

Political texture

The chapter needs pilgrimage income but fears uncontrolled pilgrimage; needs the glaziers but distrusts their hereditary secrets; needs the Office of Custody but denies ordering its harsher acts. A player can gain influence by making one wing indispensable, but every success alters the balance rather than “winning the Church.”

The Office of Lucent Custody

Founded to make the inexplicable administrable, Lucent Custody maintains guards, archives, viewing protocols, witness interviews, confiscated objects, and a tiny laboratory beneath the west tower. Its seal is a closed eye surrounded by rays.

Citizens call its officers Dousers, Half-Shadows, or politely Custodians.

Ranks and practices

  • Lectors interview witnesses and compare accounts.
  • Weights operate instruments, mark light-falls, and maintain the archive.
  • Veilers handle crowds, contraband glass, and direct threats to the rose.
  • The Lucent Warden reports to the bishop but holds an independent seal.

Custodians record testimony in two columns: what was perceived and what may be safely affirmed. This began as intellectual discipline and became a machine for turning inconvenient experience into manageable wording.

The humane case for custody

Counterfeit relics have poisoned people. Crowds have crushed pilgrims. Exposure vigils during plague killed the sick. Attempts to remove glass could erase an irreplaceable phenomenon. Custody is not imaginary oppression.

The corruption within it

The sealed archive gives the Office monopoly over patterns no outsider can test. Confiscated fragments sometimes reappear in private collections. Veilers recruit informants among children and debtors. Its doctrine of “preventive ambiguity” permits destroying proof if proof might cause unrest.

The Guild of Saint Lume

The glaziers mix furnace craft, geometry, pigment knowledge, and inherited ritual. They deny being theologians while describing glass in language any theologian would recognize.

The guild's emblem is a pair of tongs holding a six-pointed burst. Its lodge has no west-facing windows.

The three workshop laws

  1. No colour alone. Every pane is judged through neighbouring colours.
  2. Admit the wound. A perfect repair is a false repair; replacement glass receives a tiny deliberate flaw.
  3. Name the heat, not the light. Workers describe observable process and do not claim ownership of what passes through their work.

Internal divide

Continuists want to conserve every original piece and lead line exactly. Reframers believe accumulated repairs have frozen a living instrument; they want to rebuild portions using Ysabet Vey's inferred methods. Ash Readers secretly analyze furnace waste and suspect debt glass was made from an older, destroyed window.

The guild can offer the player material investigation, craft apprenticeship, access to scaffolds, and moral dilemmas where preservation conflicts with discovery.

The Fellowship of the Unwalled Day

The Fellowship is less a church than a network of route-keepers. It inherits Mara Venn's belief that uncertain boundaries reveal the hidden sky. Members meet in covered passages, bridge chambers, scaffolds, gate tunnels, shared attics, and houses whose upper storeys project across parish lines.

They do not generally worship the Second Sun. They call it the Occluded, the Guest Day, or the Neighbour. To call it a god is considered a failure of imagination: a sun might be sacred without being sovereign.

Cells and passphrases

Each cell maintains a walking chapter of the Book of Openings. A route has a question and response derived from its newest observation. For example:

“Where did evening lodge?”
“Under the fourth bridge, where no west remains.”

The phrase changes when an opening is sealed, falsified, or compromised. Knowing words without knowing the place is suspicious. Meetings begin with every person naming one wall they depended on that day, keeping the theology grounded in the material city.

Moderate aims

  • record and protect transient light-falls;
  • aid witnesses harmed or disbelieved by institutions;
  • preserve erased street names and counter-memory testimony;
  • loosen the Church's monopoly on access;
  • prepare the city to look without panic.

Their blind spots

Persecution has made secrecy feel like virtue in itself. Cells hoard information, romanticize destabilizing counter-memory, and may treat vulnerable witnesses as proof rather than people. Some prosperous members enjoy theatrical danger while poorer route-keepers suffer punishment.

The Children of the Open Sky

This radical splinter holds that every enclosure strengthens the false heaven. They remove roof tiles, break shutters, paint false windows, and steal lead from the rose's tracery. Their ideal city has no locked door and no private archive.

The Children are not merely villains. They expose secret prisons, open granaries during famine, and reject the Fellowship's endless caution. Their critique is powerful: moderates have spent generations mapping the cage without opening it.

But their program abolishes privacy along with secrecy. Some cells coerce public confession, destroy homes to create sightlines, and plan an Unleading of the rose during conjunction. If the tracery is lock, this might free the hidden day. If it is frame or seal, the act might end the phenomenon or something worse.

The College of Measures

A small federation of clockmakers, physicians, surveyors, lens grinders, and natural philosophers. They maintain competing models and a shared etiquette: write the observation before the explanation, publish failed predictions, and never use a witness's counter-memory to seize their property.

Schools within the College

  • Spectrists: the rose reveals a natural radiation or distant body.
  • Attentionalists: witness expectation participates in manifestation.
  • Substructuralists: remote falls trace buried foundations and absent streets.
  • Harmonicists: light, bell resonance, and lead geometry form one instrument.

The College's conflict is methodological rather than simply religious. A trial that isolates variables may prevent the very unplanned encounter being studied. The player is useful because they move unpredictably and can carry instruments through a procedural city faster than official teams.

Keepers of the Absent Streets

Part mutual-aid society, part oral archive, the Keepers serve people with counter-memory. They record alternate routes, kinships, houses, and events without insisting they are physically present. Their meeting room has two maps: the chartered city in black and the remembered city in blue thread.

Keepers practice double courtesy: never tell someone their absent life is a mere delusion; never let an absent claim erase a present person's rights. This principle is difficult in practice. If a widow remembers a door into your cellar and knows what is buried there, is her knowledge evidence, trespass, or grief?

Some Keepers believe enough mutually consistent testimony could reconstruct the other city. A secret inner group, the Residents, already uses absent street names exclusively and considers the present map temporary.

The Concord of One Shadow

A lay movement born among families injured by pilgrim riots, glass frauds, and obsessive exposure. Its members paint a single black line above their doors and refuse all Second Sun trade. They advocate closing the cathedral west end and destroying detached fragments.

At their best, Concord houses provide practical refuge from apocalyptic pressure. They remind scholars that not everyone wants life converted into evidence. At their worst, they harass marked witnesses, burn archives, and demand children be tested for “divided sight.”

The Concord's most unsettling members fully believe the Second Sun is real. They simply think some realities become stronger through attention and should be starved.

Pilgrims, guides, and the commerce of wonder

No single guild owns the pilgrimage economy. Lens sellers, hostel keepers, licensed guides, pickpockets, printers, candle makers, relic brokers, and canal boatmen all profit. They turn metaphysics into prices:

  • a place on the nave's north side costs more because double shadows separate cleanly on pale stone;
  • blue smoked lenses are respectable, violet ones “heretical,” though most are the same glass;
  • guides promise appearances they cannot control and hire children to create false reflections;
  • counterfeit rose fragments may be harmless, toxic, or politically planted.

Merchants can be cynical without being unbelievers. Someone may fake a miracle at noon and attend an Unwaller meeting at night.

Cross-faction pressure points

Access to the rose

The chapter owns the building, the guild claims craft custody, Lucent Custody controls safety, pilgrims claim spiritual access, and Unwallers reject ownership of any window onto a shared sky.

Counter-memory and property

Keepers document absent houses. Courts recognize only present deeds. The College finds alternate knowledge evidentially valuable. The Office fears coordinated claims are a route to civic chaos.

The approaching conjunction

Conjunctivists want ritual preparation. The College wants uninterrupted access. The Fellowship wants public witness. The Children want to remove lead. The Concord wants the rose bricked up. Merchants want a festival. Each can ask the player for a small, reasonable action that becomes part of a dangerous whole.

Who owns a witness?

A person who sees something unprecedented may immediately face clergy seeking discernment, Custodians seeking testimony, scholars seeking measurements, heretics seeking validation, merchants seeking a story, and family seeking the person they were before. The ethical question is not only who believes them, but who lets them remain a person.

Faction-writing rules for smart actors

  • Give every committed NPC one doctrine they can state, one doubt they hide, one material dependency, and one person across the divide whom they love or owe.
  • Let class and occupation matter as much as creed. A glazier, guard, widow, and child within one faction should not sound interchangeable.
  • Factions remember player conduct, not abstract reputation points: kept a route secret, returned a fragment, mocked a witness, sold a testimony, opened a door.
  • Cooperation should create enemies and obligations, not simply fill a faction bar.
  • Defection is possible, but rarely because of one persuasive speech. It follows accumulated contradiction between doctrine and lived consequence.
