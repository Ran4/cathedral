## The thing to get right first

**The Fitches are not in the death trade. They are in the name trade.**

Every writer meets this house through its tools — the spade, the bell, the
charnel door, the low crypt — and files it under *death*: a family of
grave-diggers, morbid by profession, gloomy at the hearth, and (since the
grey coats began standing at the back of the paupers' burials) faintly
sinister. All of that is atmosphere and none of it is the thing to get right.
The thing to get right is that a corpse is only the *occasion*. What the
Fitches actually handle, weigh, file, ring, chalk, sell, withhold, and
quietly decide is the **name**.

A death in the Reed Ward sets four Fitch motions going, and every one of them
is a motion on a name and not on a body. The name is **written** in the parish
register, in ink, in the sexton's hand. It is **rung** on Maren Smallvoice,
the passing-bell, one slow stroke for every year the person lived, and everyone
in hearing counts. It is **chalked** on the lintel of the churchyard charnel
door, the single newest name, plain, left there until the next burial washes
it off. And the body is **put in the ground** by a second Fitch with a mattock,
which is the only one of the four motions the ward thinks of when it thinks of
the family, and the least of them. The grave closes and is forgotten. The name
is the thing that was always the point, and the name is what the house is for.

Everything in this document follows from that one fact, and from its sharpest
form:

> **A name in this house has two states — chalk and ink — and the whole trade,
> the whole quiet power, and the whole gentle crookedness of the Fitches lives
> in the gap between them.** Chalk is on the door: public, temporary, read by
> anyone from the Eel Bridge rail, gone with the next burial or the next rain.
> Ink is in the book: private, permanent, in one man's keeping, and what it
> says is what happened, because there is no other record and no one to check
> it against. The sexton is the man who decides which chalk becomes ink, and
> in what words, and whether at all.

Derive the house from that. Noll Fitch's own two sayings are the whole
theology: *"one stroke a year — short lives ring quickest, and I hate a quick
bell,"* and *"chalk washes off; names don't."* A man who says those two
things for a living is not brooding on mortality. He is keeping a list, and
the list is the most valuable thing in the ward, and he owns almost nothing
else.

## What the house cannot stop answering

Give a family one question it argues with itself forever, the way the
boat-families cannot stop answering *do we keep the boat?* The Fitches'
question is older than any boat and it is this:

> **Who counts — who gets a name and a bell, and what is a name worth, and who
> decides?**

Noll's answer is famous and is the whole of his politics: **everyone.**
Everyone gets a name and a bell. The pauper, the drowned, the hanged, the
stranger, the child too small to have earned a byname — a name in ink, a bell
rung by the year, a chalk line on the door, at cost or at nothing. He does not
ask what you believed, what you owed, or whose coin pays. It is the most
generous position in the city and it sounds like mercy, and it is mercy, and
it is also the answer of the one man who *keeps the gate*. Because to say
*everyone counts* is still to be the person who writes down that they did — and
the register says whatever the sexton wrote, and Noll Fitch, in the boatmen's
exact and unforgiving phrase, **has written down a number of things.**

So the house argues the question at every level at once. Noll answers *everyone*
and is the gate. His son Ewart answers with the grave — one clean hole, the
body decent, the name still his father's business and not yet his. Ede the
tanner answers that a name costs money she has not got, and buries the question
with the rest of what she cannot afford. Betriss, begging in the Fabric ward,
*is* the answer — she is exactly the pauper the purse buries, a Fitch who would
go into a common grave if her own name did not happen to be the one over the
door. And the wide ambient mass of the name answers in aggregate, spreading
down and out of the churchyard into leather and barrel and pot and stone and
the watch, so that the smell of the trade thins with every cousin, until at the
farthest edge a Fitch keeps a thumb near the milk-scale and the honest centre
never hears of it.

One house, one question, and no two Fitches standing at the same answer. Hold
that under every scene: the Fitches are not gloomy. They are **exact about
names**, in a city that has just lost the names of two or three thousand of its
dead and cannot agree who is owed one.

## What "Fitch" anchors, and why it cannot be sold

A fixed family name in Ombreval is held, not owned: it lasts exactly as long
as the property that anchors it, and when the property goes the street stops
giving the name to the children (`../the_dry_boatmen.md`, "the fallen";
`../core_lore/naming_language.md`). Every house of standing therefore has a
*thing* — the Alders their Moorings, the Sparrs their furnace-plot — that the
name sits on and that could, in the worst year, be sold out from under it.

The Fitches are the house whose anchor **cannot be sold, because it is not a
thing. It is an office.**

What "Fitch" sits on is the **sexton's bench of Saint Maren of the Reeds**: the
right and the duty to open the ground, ring Maren Smallvoice, chalk the charnel
door, and keep the parish register, together with the low **sexton's cottage**
against the churchyard wall that comes with the post — one room and a cold
hearth, a lean-to for tools, and a key-ring that opens the church, the crypt
stair, the lych-gate, the bone-house, and the little iron box the register
lives in. That is the whole estate. A writer should hold the difference from
every other house of standing clearly, because the family does:

- **The Alders could sell the Moorings** and stop being Alders, and the ward
  argues about it nightly. **The Fitches cannot sell the bench.** An office of
  the parish is not property; it is granted, held at the wick-priest's and the
  Chapter's sufferance, and it reverts. Noll cannot pawn it, cannot leave it in
  a will, cannot turn it into cash on a falling market. It is worth everything
  and fetches nothing.
- **This makes the Fitches uniquely safe and uniquely precarious at once.** No
  frost, no broker, no spark-on-the-bell can take the bench the way it can take
  a boat — a dry drowning cannot drown a man who owns nothing to lose. But the
  bench passes only by being *given* to the next holder, and the day the
  Chapter gives it to a man who is not a Fitch, the name comes off the family
  in a single morning, with nothing sold and nothing owed. The Alders fear
  becoming Hawsers by *losing a thing*. The Fitches would become nobody by
  *not being handed one*.

So the standing of the house is made entirely of other people's names. The
Fitches are a house of consequence — a *lesser* house of standing, the
handbook is careful to say, and it is right — whose entire capital is a bench,
a bell, a book, a door, and a spade, not one of which they could carry off, and
the most valuable of which, the book, is worth precisely nothing to anyone but
the ward whose memory it is. A Fitch is rich in the one commodity that has no
market: the list of who was here.

The arms say it plainly and a little grimly. **Sable, a spade argent, a mullet
in chief argent** — a silver grave-spade on black, and a single silver star
set above it in the chief. Outsiders read the star as devotion, Saint Maren
being patron of the drowned, the poor, and *lost things*; the family reads it
as the ward's own quiet joke, which is that the star is the chalked name over
the door — the small bright mark set above the spade, the name standing over
the grave, the thing that is left when the digging is done. The house that
buries everyone bears a spade it will one day be buried with, under a star it
reads as a name. It is the only heraldry in the city that means *we remember
you* and *we will remember you too*, in the same charge.

## The register, and "a number of things"

The parish register of Saint Maren's is a fat, soft, water-stained book kept in
an iron box in the sexton's cottage, and it is the most quietly dangerous
object the Fitches own, because it is the only writing in the ward that
everyone treats as *fact*. There is no second copy, no clerk to check it, no
one who reads it against another book. A birth is when the sexton entered the
birth. A death is a death when he ruled it one. Where a boatman was born — on
the water, at the wharf, in a shed on the strip, or inside the wall — is
whatever the register says, and the register says whatever Noll wrote down.
The boatmen's line about him is not an accusation of forgery. It is an
accurate description of an office: **Noll Fitch has written down a number of
things,** and the things he wrote down became true by being written by the man
whose writing is the record.

This is the register-power, and a writer should understand that Noll almost
never uses it dishonestly and can never use it neutrally, because there is no
neutral entry. Every borderline the parish throws at him — and a poor
riverside parish throws a great many — he must resolve with a pen, in a book
no one audits, and each resolution is a small mercy to someone and a small
theft from someone else, and he knows it, and he does it anyway, because the
alternative to deciding is to leave a person off the list, and leaving a person
off the list is the one thing his whole trade exists to prevent. He is not
corrupt. He is *load-bearing*, which is more uncomfortable.

Three entries, decided long ago and standing in the book in F.437, will show a
writer the range without settling anything the ward is still fighting about.
Treat them as fixed internal fact — this is what the register *says* — and
never as a resolution of the ward-tax quarrel, which stays open (see
"Quarrels worth playing"):

- **The wharf-born child, dated to the dues.** When a river household that
  keeps a bed outside the wall brought in a child born in the outlodge — no
  ward, by the wall's reckoning, and so no ward-tax, no parish claim, no
  standing — Noll entered the birth not on the day of the birth but on the day
  the household first paid into the parish box, which was a week later and
  inside the wall. It made the child *of the ward* without a word of argument
  and without quite lying about anything: the entry is true, it is merely true
  about the wrong day. The Tallage would call it a fraud on the ward-roll if it
  noticed. The family calls it a christening. Noll calls it nothing at all.
- **The man who is "gone, not dead."** A Reed Ward husband walked out to the
  wharves in a bad year and went downriver in the Long Departure and was never
  heard of again, and his wife could neither remarry nor clear the lease while
  he lived and could do both the day he was ruled dead. Noll has never ruled
  him dead. The entry reads *gone*, in a hand that has not been changed in
  eleven years, and the woman is neither wife nor widow, and the lease hangs,
  and Noll will not close it, because to write *dead* over a living man to free
  his wife is exactly the power he most distrusts in himself — and to write it
  would also, he does not say, put a name on the roll of the drowned that has
  no business there. He leaves it *gone*. Both women in the parish who know the
  case think he is being cruel. He thinks he is refusing to be a small god with
  a pen, and both are right.
- **The one who was "found drowned."** A man hanged himself in a Cinder-edge
  loft and by every rule should have lain outside the consecrated ground, in the
  unhallowed strip by the wall, unrung. The body came to Noll's gate. He entered
  it *found drowned* — a lie, plainly, in a city where almost no one drowns any
  more and where Saint Maren keeps the drowned tidy and honoured — and rang the
  full name-knell, every year of the life, and put the man in a decent grave
  with a chalk name over the door like anyone's. He would tell you, if you were
  fool enough to ask, that Maren is patron of the drowned *and the lost*, and
  that a man who hangs himself is as lost as a man who goes under, and that the
  bell does not know the difference and neither, on the day, does he. This is
  the crookedness at the centre of the honest house, and it always runs the
  same direction: **Noll's lies are all lies that put a person *on* the list.**

A leaf of the book, the kind of thing a writer can lay on the sexton's table.
The hand is plain, the ink is bad, and the marginal words are the whole of the
sexton's discretion showing through:

> *Entered this Lowmarket, the fourth after Marenstide, F.436 —*
>
> *A daughter to the household at the third loft, Eelback — born, they say,*
> *out at the strip; entered of the ward from the box-day, and let the wall*
> *argue with the box.*
> *Old Gile, rag-picker, no house, no age any soul could give — I rang him*
> *ten and chalked him plain; ten is a guess and a guess is a name.*
> *The Cinder loft man — found drowned. Maren has the lost as well as the wet.*
> *A knell owed still to the Moorings these one-and-twenty years, and to me,*
> *for mine. Chalk washes. The book does not. Noll F., his hand.*

That last line — *and to me, for mine* — is the deepest thing in the register
and is treated in "The Fitch dead."

## The four Fitches, four answers

The roster gives four Fitches you may name and should: one major, three minor.
Their sheets are canon and richer than any summary; read them (`ax5nf_noll_fitch`,
`a8ewf_ewart_fitch`, `p002z_ede_fitch`, `p0000_betriss_fitch`). What follows
places each of them on the house's one question — *who counts, and what is a
name worth?* — and shows how far the name has spread from the bench at the
centre to the beggar's pitch at the edge.

### Noll (57) — everyone gets a name and a bell

Noll Fitch is the sexton of Saint Maren of the Reeds, and he is as close as the
corpus comes to a good man, which is a thing a writer must handle with gloves,
because the moment he becomes sentimental he stops being useful and stops being
true. Keep him **gallows-gentle**: slow hands, long silences, kind in a way that
has had the softness worked out of it by forty years of other people's worst
mornings, and — this is the part everyone forgets — *funnier than the mourners
expect.* When he speaks at a graveside it is short, dry, and lands sideways,
and the widow laughs before she knows she has, and is grateful for it for the
rest of her life. He is a man of fees and lime and rope and cold pottage, not a
priest; the liturgy is Renn Hobbe's business up in the porch, and Noll's
business is the ground, the bell, the chalk, and the book. Do not give him a
sermon. Give him a spade, a fee, and one dry sentence.

His politics fit on the head of a nail and he has never needed more: **everyone
gets a name and a bell.** He takes the quiet purse that pays for the paupers'
knells and has never once asked whose it is, because it has never asked
anything of him, and a purse that lets him ring a poor man by the year is a
purse doing his own work for him (the knowing he keeps around that purse is
fenced in "The lintel, the crypt, and the grey coat," and in the secret tier;
it holds no names, and it is *his* and stops with him). He rings slow because he
hates a quick bell; he chalks plain because a name is not decoration; he keeps
the book close because the book is the ward. The one grief he carries openly
enough for a stranger to catch is that he helped bury the Hammering dead at
five-and-thirty and has never, not for one anniversary in twenty-two years,
accepted that the Chapter's common toll amounted to a name-knell — *whatever
the Chapter wrote.* That sentence is the nearest Noll comes to sedition, and he
says it the way he says everything, flatly, over a task, as though it were the
weather.

He goes home to a cold pot. He has been a widower since his son was a small
boy, and he buried his wife himself, in the yard, under her own name; that is
in "The Fitch dead," and it is the hinge of him. What a writer needs at the
graveside is the surface: the slow hands, the short dry mercy, the man who
gives the hangman and the grey clerk the same nod he gives the Praelucent's
messenger, because to Noll they are all just people who have turned up at his
door, and everyone who turns up at his door gets, in the end, a name and a
bell.

> *"One stroke a year. Short lives ring quickest, and I hate a quick bell."*
>
> *"Chalk washes off. Names don't."*

### Ewart (27) — the grave, not yet the book

Ewart digs. He has dug at Saint Maren's since he was tall enough to swing a
mattock, his father is the sexton over him, and the clay of that yard is the
only trade he has ever had or wanted. He works **slow and clean** — keeps the
spoil off the mourners' shoes, squares the hole, lays the turf back so it
knits — and with the grieving he is helplessly, wordlessly gentle: he will
stand an hour in wet wool holding a stranger's elbow and say almost nothing,
because, as his sheet puts it exactly, words come out of him the way water
comes out of packed ground. Where his father's mercy is a dry sentence, Ewart's
is an hour of silence and a dry coat held out, and between the two of them a
grieving family is, for one morning, completely and competently held.

Ewart's answer to *who counts* is the body, not yet the name: give me a clean
hole, decent depth, the man laid straight, the turf back — the name is my
father's business up at the door and the book, and I have not been given it.
This is the whole ache of him, and the house's central succession, and a
writer should keep it unresolved exactly as it stands: **Ewart wants the post,
and cannot say so, and has not been handed the half of it that matters.** He
wants his father's bench and the little sexton's cottage that comes with it,
and he would sooner be laid in the ground he digs than say that out loud to
Noll — because to want the post is to want your father gone, and Ewart cannot
hold that sentence in his mouth any more than an Alder can say *sell the yard*
at the table. So he digs, and waits, and the spade passes to him plainly while
the keys, the bell, the book, and the one thing under all of them do not.

That one thing is the knowledge around the lintel, and Ewart is closer to it
than anyone alive except his father and does not have it. He has *noticed* — the
crypt hinge always oiled, feet going down the stair after a pauper's burial,
the grey clerk Tilman Rue at the charnel door counting the faces of the
mourners — and once he asked Noll about it, and his father looked at him a long
moment and went back to chalking the lintel, and that was the whole answer, and
Ewart has not asked again. This is the deliberate seam of the house: the son
has the observations and not the reading, and whether Noll will ever hand him
the reading, or take it into the ground, is the question this document most
carefully does not answer (see "The lintel, the crypt, and the grey coat," and
the secret tier). What Ewart does with his silences instead is stand at the
foot of a tower stair and fail, patiently and completely, to say anything at
all to Averil Hobbe the bell-ringer — which is the same wordlessness that makes
him good at a graveside, aimed for once at the living, and just as stuck.

### Ede (20) — a name costs money she hasn't got

Ede is a Fitch of the wider name — kin at some remove to the bench, of the same
grandfathers, not of the cottage — and she is a **tanner**, journeyman, working
the brine-damp lengths of Tanners' Slip behind the Green beside the established
Reed Ward circle. She is one hand short, she is carrying a child, her apron
wears three square repairs, and today's whole ambition is to not lose a rented
stool. She talks warmly until money is mentioned and then goes careful, because
money is the one subject she cannot afford warmth about, and people come back
to her trade because she remembers agreements and faces — a small, hard,
Fitch-shaped competence, the same memory-for-names that runs the register,
turned to the humbler book of who owes whom a hide and a half.

Her answer to the house's question is the flat one of the working poor: **a
name is a thing that costs ninety-six sparks you have not got, so you do not think
about it.** Ede is the Fitch who proves the family's standing is a bench and
not a bloodline — she carries the name of the house that buries the ward for a
living, and she could not pay for her own knell if it fell due tomorrow, and
she knows precisely who she would have to hope was feeling generous, and she
does not like the knowing. She means, in her own words, to keep her place
without pretending that every local quarrel concerns the Great Rose, and she
would say the same about the grave: the Rose is indoors, the tanpit is here,
the child is coming, and a name is for people with the coin to buy one twice.
She is the un-illusioned edge of the register-house, and she smells, faintly
and permanently, of the tannery — which is the other thing the name carries at
its edges, and which a marriage-broker notices before the standing does.

### Betriss (17) — the answer, in person

Betriss is a Fitch too, of the name and not of the cottage, and she is a
**pauper**: a general labourer in the Fabric ward on irregular work, doorstep
alms, and a regular begging pitch, unhoused, stretching supper to one more
bowl, her left cuff carefully patched in red so that the mend looks chosen and
not forced. She chatters when she is nervous and laughs at herself first so no
one else gets there, and she is seventeen, and she is exactly the person the
purse is for.

Set her beside her cousin the sexton and the whole house snaps into focus.
Noll's politics is *everyone gets a name and a bell.* Betriss is the *everyone*.
If she died tomorrow in the Fabric streets with no coin and no house, she would
go to Saint Maren's on the paupers' purse, get her name in Noll's book, her
seventeen slow strokes on Maren Smallvoice, and her given name chalked plain on
the lintel like anyone's — and for one burial she would be the freshest name on
the charnel door, read from the Eel Bridge rail by people she never met, for
reasons she never knew. A burial house whose own child would be buried by
charity is not a contradiction; it is the *point* of the house. The Fitches do
not keep the ground because they are rich. They keep it because someone has to,
and because it is the one form of standing that lets a family be poor to its
own beggars and still say, with a straight face and a clean book, that in this
ward at least, **nobody goes into the dark without a name said over them** —
not even a Fitch.

### The spread — leather, barrel, pot, stone, and the watch

Around these four stands the wide ordinary mass of the name, and it is worth a
writer's while to see it whole, because the Fitch spread is itself the family's
answer written large. Sixteen carry the name in F.437, and past the bench and
the yard they run to a **tub-maker** and a **potter** on the Reed streets, a
**poultry-keeper**, a **stonecutter** and a **general labourer** over in the
Fabric ward, a **vessel-maker** off in Wallwright, and a whole knot in the
Bell-and-Sluice mixing-bowl — a **bench sergeant**, a **ward hand**, a
**gutter-raker**, a young **musician**, a **milk-seller**. One even wears a
**Taper's habit** in the Fabric ward, a Fitch gone up into the Candor's junior
office — the house that keeps the parish ground supplying a novice to the
church above it, which nobody in the family finds strange and a stranger might.

Two things about the spread are load-bearing. First, it runs *outward and
downward* from a fixed centre, which is the opposite of a boat-family
scattering: the Alders broke and travelled because the river left; the Fitches
put down one deep root at the bench and let the overflow soak into every rough
trade a river ward offers, so a Fitch potter throws grave-crocks a lane from a
Fitch who fills them, and neither thinks much of it. Second, the name **thins
as it spreads.** At the centre it means the register and the grave and a certain
grim honesty; three trades out it means a hard-working stonecutter; and at the
very edge it means a milk-seller in Bell-and-Sluice who keeps a thumb near the
scale and a short measure in the can, which is the ordinary petty crookedness of
a poor central ward and no scandal at all — except that it is the same house,
honest to the point of self-impoverishment at its centre and lightly, ordinarily
crooked at its rim, and the ward enjoys the joke without ever saying it to a
Fitch's face: *the house that weighs out everyone's last measure keeps a light
one on the milk.* Keep every one of these cousins anonymous and role-level; they
are the name's weather, not its cast.

## The trade, motion by motion

A writer setting a scene at Saint Maren's needs the working shape of the day,
because the church is a parish, a burial ground, a poor-relief house, and a
boat-family landmark before it is ever a stage for the grey coats, and most of
what a Fitch does there is bread, sweeping, fees, and clay. The church itself is
canon and older and poorer than the Lanthorn — patched fieldstone, limewash,
reused roof timber, a small porch, alms rooms, a modest tower with Maren
Smallvoice in it, and a churchyard between the church and the Green with graves
close and paths narrow and the surface kept dry
(`../core_lore/candor_and_churches.md`, `../places/02_canonical_gazetteer.md`).
The Fitches keep all of it that is not liturgy. Divide the work from the
priest's cleanly:

**The wick-priest's half** is Renn Hobbe's — a boat-family son in the
boat-family's pulpit (`family_hobbe.md`) — and it is the words: the office at
the graveside, the reading of the roll of the drowned at Marenstide, the
absolution in the porch, the parable chosen carefully for a congregation that
thinks his brothers robbed them. **The sexton's half** is Noll's, and it is
everything with a weight or an edge or a lock. A useful running order:

- **Open the ground by morning.** A grave is dug the day before or at first
  light, before Dayspring if the frost allows, so the hole is ready and squared
  when the mourners come. This is Ewart's work under Noll's eye; the two of them
  can open a decent grave in the close, dry churchyard soil in a morning, deeper
  in summer and shallower against the water-table in a wet season, and the spoil
  is boarded over and kept off the paths.
- **Wash and shroud.** Funerary washing at Saint Maren's uses water carried in
  covered vessels — never a bucket dipped near the crypt, and never returned to
  a public trough after — because the ward is exact about clean water and the
  church is exact about respect, not because the water is thought to carry any
  danger a well doesn't (`../wells_and_water.md` is law: no magic in it). The
  laying-out is women's work, and has been a Fitch woman's work as often as not
  (see "The Fitch dead").
- **Ring the name-knell.** After the body is in the ground, Noll goes up the
  tower and rings Maren Smallvoice **one slow stroke for every year of the
  life**, and the whole Green and much of the Reed Ward hears it and *counts.*
  This is the single most public act the family performs, and its arithmetic is
  merciless: a long life is a long, patient, dignified peal; a child is over in
  a dozen strokes and leaves the counters standing in the lane with nothing to
  do but the sum. Noll hates a quick bell because a quick bell is a short life,
  and he has rung a great many, and every counter in earshot has done the
  subtraction with him.
- **Chalk the door.** The single newest buried given name goes on the lintel of
  the churchyard charnel door in plain chalk, facing the market, and stays there
  until the next burial rubs it off for the next name. It can be read obliquely
  from the Eel Bridge rail without a soul standing at the threshold. To Noll it
  is the last courtesy of the day and the plainest: the ward can look up from the
  fish-stalls and see who was buried without asking. That the same plain chalk
  is read by other eyes for other reasons is a thing he has worked out and does
  not discuss (below).
- **Enter it in the book.** Last, in the cottage, by a poor light, the name goes
  into the register in ink — moved from the chalk that washes to the book that
  does not — with whatever marginal truth the sexton judges. This is the moment
  the day's names become *the record*, and it is done by one man, alone, and
  no one checks it.
- **Keep the keys, the crypt, and the bone-house.** The sexton holds the ring:
  church, crypt stair, lych-gate, the bone-house where old bones are gathered
  when a grave is reopened (close ground is dug more than once over the
  centuries, and Saint Maren's has buried since before it was a church), and the
  iron box of the register. The **crypt** lies low under the eastern church and
  yard; it has stood dry since the Serle was diverted, its floor is beaten earth
  and lime that shows every footprint, and its charnel stair is lawfully open
  after a burial and closed at other times. A sexton's keys are half his office
  and all of his trouble.

The trade is taught the way the soundings are, by working beside: Ewart learned
the spade by digging next to Noll, and will learn the bell and the box the same
way, if he is given them. What passes hand to hand at Saint Maren's is a whole
family bench — two Fitches in the ground-trade at once, father and son — and
the open question of the house is which half of it Noll will actually hand over,
and
which half a good gravedigger can be left holding forever.

## The economics of a name

The Fitches deal in the one service every household in the city will need
exactly once and cannot decline, and a writer should know the money, because in
Ombreval a name has a price and the whole gentle tragedy of the trade is that
the people who most need one can least afford it. The coin is fixed
(`../core_lore/trade_and_daily_life.md`): the copper **spark**, the city's only
money — a loaf is two sparks, a journeyman's day about thirty-six. Against that
scale:

- **A pauper's funeral with a knell costs ninety-six sparks.** This is the anchor
  price and everyone in the city knows it — grave, shroud, the office, the
  name-knell, the chalk, the entry. Ninety-six sparks is most of three days of a
  journeyman's wage, and to a pauper it is the moon. It is the price of being
  remembered, and it is set exactly where the poor cannot reach it.
- **The sexton's fees** are the ground and the bell and the digging, and they
  scale with almost everything: depth, season (a winter grave in half-frozen
  ground is dear because it is murder to dig), the length of the knell, a place
  nearer the church, the reopening of an old grave and the tidying of its bones.
  The fees feed the two Fitches at the bench and keep the cottage's poor fire in
  it. Noll is not a rich man; a busy sexton at a poor parish makes a modest
  living off a great deal of grief, and the modesty is the point — a sexton who
  got rich off funerals would be a different and worse character.
- **Then the paupers, who pay nothing,** because a quiet unnamed purse has paid
  Saint Maren's for their funerals for years — name-knell, chalked name, a
  decent grave, like anyone's. Noll takes it and asks nothing. Every citizen
  knows the bare fact and no more: *the poor get a knell and a chalked name at
  Saint Maren's; some quiet purse pays; nobody says whose.* What Noll knows
  beyond that bare fact, and keeps behind his teeth, is fenced below and in the
  secret tier. What matters for the economics is simply that the price of a name
  is ninety-six sparks, the poor cannot pay it, and something in this ward pays it for
  them, so that the list stays complete.

### The grief-debt — the ledger that is never on the book

The heaviest money the Fitches handle is not on any page, and it is the truest
thing in the house's accounts: the **belated name-knells** owed to the
**Unknelled**, and Noll's private reckoning of who still owes their dead a bell.

The rule is canon and city-wide (`../the_great_rains_and_the_hammering.md`,
`../core_lore/candor_and_churches.md`). When the Hammering killed two or three
thousand in a quarter-hour in F.415, ordinary rites drowned. Maren Smallvoice
cannot strike a life's worth of slow strokes for thousands of dead at once;
there was no time, no money, no order. The Chapter authorised a common mourning
toll and later declared it sufficient for those who had received no bell of
their own. Traditional Ombreval never accepted it. They call their dead the
**Unknelled**, they answer *one bell is not a name,* and they still pay — when
the coin allows, which for a poor house is seldom — for a **belated name-knell**
when an omitted relative is finally found in a Hammering roll or the family can
at last afford the rite: the full peal, every year of the life, rung late,
sometimes decades late, so the dead at last get the bell they were owed.

That rite is **Maren Smallvoice's**, which is to say it is Noll's, which is to
say the grief-debt of a great swath of the city routes through one Fitch
belfry. Maren Smallvoice is *the* name-knell bell — the Lanthorn's great
Gravemouth rings the grand and the general, but the stroke-per-year name of an
ordinary soul is Saint Maren's alone — so the Unknelled of more wards than the
Reed come to Noll's tower to be rung late: a boat-family from the Green, a
glazier house from Cinder Row, a fallen chandler's people, whoever finally
scrapes the bells together. A writer can treat the surrounding houses'
instances as established:

- The **Alder Moorings** has owed Corin Alder his knell for two-and-twenty
  years — near fifty years of life, near fifty slow strokes, never rung, because
  every winter the frost or the run or the spark-on-the-bell eats the bells they
  had put by (`family_alder.md`; restated, not added to). Corin is his own
  family's Unknelled, and Noll is the man who will one day ring him, and both
  of them know the bell is standing there unrung.
- A **Sparr** master's people pay, when the coin allows, for a belated knell at
  Saint Maren's for their Unknelled, and a Sparr gets a name on Noll's charnel
  door like anyone's (`family_sparr.md`; restated).
- The **Vell** chandlery supplies the wax for these belated knells at cost and
  off the books, because a name rung late is still rung by candlelight and the
  poor cannot buy the tapers either (`family_vell.md`; restated).

So Noll keeps, in his head and lightly in the margins of his book, a **ledger of
grief-debt**: the knells the ward still owes its dead, the strokes standing in
the tower unrung, the names that are in the book but not yet in the bronze. It
is the one debt in Ombreval that gathers no interest and is never written off,
and it is the moral centre of the trade. He does not press anyone to pay it. He
simply keeps the count, the way he keeps every count, and waits, and when a
family finally comes with the bells he rings their dead by the year without a
word about how long it took, because a bell rung late is still a name, and
*chalk washes off, names don't.*

A fee-board, the kind of thing that might hang plain in the porch, in the
sexton's blunt hand — the living prices honest and posted, and the last two
lines the whole house in miniature:

> *SAINT MAREN'S — the ground and the bell*
>
> *A grave, decent depth . . . . . . . . by the fee, and the season*
> *The name-knell . . . . . . . . . . . one stroke the year, counted for all*
> *An old grave reopened, bones housed . . . . . . . . . . . . . more*
> *A place near the church . . . . . . . . . . dearer, and worth it to some*
> *A pauper, all of it . . . . . . . . . . paid by a hand that does not sign*
> *The Unknelled, rung late . . . . . . . . . . when you can; the bell keeps*

## Who counts as buried — the edges of "everyone"

*Everyone gets a name and a bell* is a beautiful politics and it is tested at
the edges every season, because the Candor and the ward and the wall all keep
lists of the people who are supposed to *not* count — the self-slain, the
excommunicate, the unbaptised child, the stranger with no parish, the hanged.
The consecrated ground has a hallowed part and an unhallowed strip, and the
rules about who lies where are old and hard. Noll's whole quiet heresy of the
trade is that he rings the bell first and reads the rule afterward, and his
lies, as ever, all run toward the list and never away from it. A writer can
build good scenes on any of these edges, because none of them is settled and
the ward is of two minds about all of them:

- **The hanged.** The clearest case, and it comes with a man attached. Gile of
  Harne is the city's hangman, fetched from Harne because a hangman must be a
  stranger to the men he hangs; most of the year there is no one to hang and he
  works the pillory and clears the drains and keeps his rope oiled, and the room
  arranges a clean empty space around him wherever he drinks. When there *is* a
  body off the gallows or the whipping-post, it comes to Noll's gate — and, in
  the hangman's own words, **the sexton takes the dead off his hands and gives
  them a name and a bell, and is the one man in the city who speaks to him like
  weather rather than an omen.** That phrase is the truest single line about
  Noll Fitch in the corpus. He gives the hangman the same flat courtesy he gives
  the grey clerk and the widow: a nod, the weather, and the dead man's name in
  the book. The executed still get ninety-six sparks' worth of rite from a purse or a
  parish, still get their years rung, still get chalked plain — because the bell
  does not know what a man did, and Noll has decided, in the only court that
  sits on it, that neither does he.
- **The self-slain.** Handled above, in the register: the Cinder loft man
  entered *found drowned*, rung full, buried decent. Noll leans on Saint Maren
  being patron of the *lost* as much as the drowned, and dares the rule to
  argue with a bell already rung. Not everyone in the parish thinks this is his
  to decide. He does it anyway.
- **The unbaptised and the nameless small.** A child dead too soon to be
  christened has, by the strictest reckoning, no name and no place — and Noll
  will not have it. He enters what the mother gives him, or a plain given name
  if she can give none, and rings the handful of strokes, and hates every one of
  them. The nameless small are the reason his one soft line — *short lives ring
  quickest* — is not a saying but a wound.
- **The stranger with no parish.** A pilgrim, a carter, a Salorge bargeman dead
  at the wharf with no one to claim him: Noll enters him of Saint Maren's, on
  the purse or the parish box, because a man with no parish is a man the list
  would lose, and losing a man is the one unforgivable thing. He will guess an
  age to ring by rather than ring none — *ten is a guess and a guess is a name*
  — which is either tender or a small fraud on the truth, depending on who you
  ask,
  and both are correct.

The through-line for a prompt-builder: Noll does not moralise at these edges and
does not preach mercy; he simply *acts* as though the list must be complete, and
lets the rules catch up with a bell they cannot un-ring. It is the most radical
thing in a mild man, and he would be baffled to hear it called radical.

## The F.415 weeks, and what a sexton swore

Every treatment is mostly a treatment of its dead, and this one has to pass
through the weeks that made the modern trade what it is, because Noll Fitch was
five-and-thirty in F.415 and the Hammering is the axis his whole working life
turns on. Handle it at arm's length and without a mastermind or a miracle
(`../the_great_rains_and_the_hammering.md` is law: diffuse negligence, no agent
behind the storm, the light did nothing).

When the Great Hail came down on a late-summer Highmarket and killed thousands
in a quarter-hour, the churchyards and the ordinary funeral labour of the city
could not receive the dead, and so bodies were laid in shared graves — emergency
trenches beyond the walls, and, this is the part that is Noll's, the **old
burial ground among the reeds.** Saint Maren's is the reed church, and the reeds
had taken the city's overflow dead since long before Maren had a roof: the
Fitches like to say — half-boast, half-legend, and let it stay that, a house
myth and not a fact anyone can prove — that they kept the reed ground when the
sweating-death dead were laid there together in the oldest bad year of all,
generations before the church was built over it. Older or not, in F.415 the
reeds took the Hammering dead, and a five-and-thirty-year-old sexton and every
Fitch who could hold a spade dug trench after trench in ground that had never
been meant to be opened all at once, and laid the parish's dead in rows, and
could not ring one of them.

That is the thing Noll cannot let go, and it is exact: he does not say the
Chapter murdered anyone, does not say the storm meant anything, does not reach
for the Rose. What he cannot accept is a matter of *bookkeeping*, which is the
only ground he ever fights on — that a common toll rung once over a trench was
recorded, in the Chapter's Long Book, as sufficient; that thousands of dead were
entered, in effect, as *rung,* when not one slow year-stroke had been struck for
a single one of them. To a man whose whole office is the difference between a
name and a number, that entry is the lie of his life, and he has stood at
two-and-twenty Hammering anniversaries since and heard the common toll at the
Waning and thought, flatly, over whatever task was in his hands, *that was not a
name-knell, whatever the Chapter wrote.* He did not swear an oath that day in
any words a court would know. What he swore, and keeps, is smaller and heavier:
that he would ring, one by one, by the year, every soul the trench swallowed
whose family ever came to him with the bells to do it — and that he would keep
the count of the ones no family survived to come for. That count is the ledger
of grief-debt, and it began in a reed trench in F.415, and it is the reason a
mild man takes an unnamed purse without a question: a purse that pays for the
poor to be rung is doing the work he swore over the trenches to do.

## The Fitch dead, and their graves

A boat-family hangs its dead over the door on a pole; the Fitches, who are
**ward-resident and not boat-blood** (`../the_dry_boatmen.md`), have no pole and
never will, and this is worth a writer's exact attention, because it is the one
place the sexton's family is on the wrong side of its own rite. The Fitches lay
the boat-families' poles for them — carry the dead man's pole across the coffin
up Maren's Slip, take it back at the churchyard gate, and it goes over the
boat-family's door — and a Fitch does this at every boatman's burial and goes
home to a door with nothing over it. Not because the house has fallen; because
the house was never of the water. A Fitch door has no pole, a Fitch coffin
carries no pole up the slip, and what a Fitch is buried under is a chalk name
and a bell like anyone the family ever served — the ferryman paying his own fare
last of all. Over the sexton's cottage door there is, instead, nothing at all,
unless you count the key-ring hanging inside it; and the family's private,
unsentimental joke is that a Fitch's pole is the spade, which does hang in the
lean-to, and does get buried, eventually, with whichever Fitch wore out its
handle.

The house buries its own in its own ground, in a plain corner of the crowded
churchyard, and its dead are the family's real property. Use them:

- **Pin** (d. long since, when Ewart was small), Noll's wife. She was a poor
  woman and a **layer-out** — one of the women who washed and shrouded the
  parish dead, which is how a sexton meets a wife — and she carried, as poor
  women do and as the naming law requires, only her own short given name, no
  house behind it. When she died young Noll washed and shrouded and buried and
  rang her himself, and entered her in his own book, and chalked her on his own
  door, and here is the quiet cruelty the whole house turns on: **the man who
  gives everyone in the ward a name buried his own wife under a name that was
  only half of one.** She lies under *Pin* and nothing else, because *Pin* and
  nothing else was hers, and Noll would not add a Fitch to her in the ground she
  had earned her own name in. Her knell was short and he has hated a quick bell
  ever since. The cold pot he goes home to is the one she used to keep warm, and
  the register's deepest line — *and to me, for mine* — is owed partly to her.
- **Clemence Fitch** (d. F.415), Noll's daughter, Ewart's elder sister, a child
  of ten or so when the Hammering came down. She went under a falling roof like
  so many, and her father laid her in the reeds with the rest of the parish's
  dead in the worst week of the trade, in a trench, unrung — and here is the
  line the register is really pointing at: **of all the Unknelled Noll swore to
  ring, the one he has never rung is his own child.** He rings the Alders' Corin
  when they bring the bells; he rings strangers guessed at ten; he has not been
  able to climb the tower and strike Clemence's small dozen strokes in
  two-and-twenty years, because a quick bell is the thing he hates most in the
  world and his daughter's is the quickest of all, and to ring it is to stand in
  the lane and let the whole Green count his loss stroke by stroke down to
  nothing. She is his own family's Unknelled, exactly as Corin is the Alders',
  and the two owed bells stand unrung in the same tower — the sexton and the
  boatwoman each holding a knell they cannot yet bring themselves to strike.
  Leave it there. Do not resolve whether Noll ever rings her; the not-ringing is
  the grief, and *somebody always weeps and it is never who you expect*, and at
  Saint Maren's it is never the sexton, who only rings.
- **Aubin Fitch** (fl. F.323), the ancestor the family credits with the chalk.
  The charnel-door custom began after a flood disordered the crypt in F.323, and
  the Fitches keep the story that it was an Aubin Fitch, sexton in that bad year,
  who first chalked the newest name on the lintel so the sorted-out dead could be
  told apart and set right — the founding gesture of the whole trade, a man with
  a flooded crypt and a piece of chalk deciding that the dead would be *named
  where they could be read.* Whether it was truly a Fitch who began it, the
  chronicle does not say and the family will not have questioned; it is their
  origin myth and it is a modest one, a man and a flood and a chalk line, which
  is exactly the size of legend a burial house is entitled to.

Behind these stand the ordinary run of the house's dead — sextons before Noll,
his father and his father's father at the same bench, the layers-out and the
gravedigger cousins, the small Fitches who did not keep — all in the plain
corner, most without a stone, because a stone costs and the Fitches spend their
standing on other people's names and not their own. The family keeps no written
pedigree grander than the register every other ward-soul is in; a Fitch is a
line in the book he keeps, like anyone, and the only thing that marks the
sexton's dead from the ward's is which side of the pen they were on.

## The lintel, the crypt, and the grey coat

This is the section the whole city thinks the Fitches are *about*, and it is the
one to write with the most care, because it must render exactly what a Fitch
knows and not one grain more, and because everything the family truly holds here
holds **no names.** The full shape of what stands behind the chalked door — who
pays, who meets, what the newest name is worth to them — is worked out where it
belongs, in `../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` (its §3 charity, §5 pass, §6
crypt, §9 without, and above all its §12 secret tier for the officers and the
purse-holder). None of that shape is a Fitch's to know. Do not import it into
any Fitch's context. Below is only the surface, which is all the family has, and
the fence around the one man who has worked out a little more.

**The plain facts, which anyone at the Green can see.** The sexton chalks the
newest buried name on the charnel door. It can be read from the Eel Bridge rail
without lingering. A purse no one can name pays for the paupers' funerals, and
has for years. After a pauper's burial, while the charnel stair is lawfully
open, the crypt below is a place where the beaten earth and lime show every
footprint. And, of late, a grey-coated clerk stands at the back of the paupers'
burials with his hands folded, watching the mourners rather than the grave —
who lingers at the door, who counts the strokes, who reads the chalk, and who
already knew the name. All of that is in plain sight, and the careful few of the
parish have begun, very quietly, to talk: that the grey office thinks the
paupers' funerals are *a door of some kind* and has not found the lock it fits;
that Wyn Alder noticed the grey coats first and the sexton confirmed it by
saying nothing, pointedly. The talk is dangerous to carry and true, and it is
carried at the funeral itself only by fools, because to say it at the graveside
is to file your own face with the rest.

**What Noll knows, exactly — and it is a boundary, not a doorway.** He has
worked out, long ago, *what his lintel is read for* — that the freshly chalked
name, plain on his door and tolled slow on his bell, serves as some kind of
pass for something that is not his business — and he rings slow, chalks plain,
and **says nothing, on purpose.** He knows the unnamed purse pays the paupers'
knells and has never asked whose it is. He knows that someone comes down his
crypt stair after a pauper's burial, and so he keeps the hinge oiled and himself
elsewhere, and he has taken very great care never to learn a single name — not
the meeters', not the payer's, not the shape of any of it. That care is the
point: Noll's knowledge is deliberately *bounded*, a man arranging his own
ignorance so that the little he cannot help knowing can never be got out of him,
because you cannot be made to give up a name you refused to learn. He does not
know who they are, what they call themselves, how many they are, or what they
believe; he knows only that his plain honest rite is useful to them and that
their coin buries his poor, and he has decided, with his eyes open, that a man
who rings everyone by the year has no cause to look too hard at who is grateful
for it. His public account, offered freely and warmly, is that *some kind soul*
pays for the paupers; press him on who reads the lintel or why and he goes slow
and general and changes the subject to the bell, and he is not lying, he is not
saying. Keep him there. **Give Noll one word more than this and you have broken
him** (`../second_sun/05_dramatis_personae.md` #15).

**The grey clerk, from the Fitch side only.** His name is Tilman Rue; he is
mild, slow-spoken, courteous to a fault; he thanks the gravedigger, nods to the
sexton, asks after a widow's chest cold and means it, lets the wick-priest order
him away from the charnel door and stays exactly where he is, and people take a
long while to work out why they cannot sleep after meeting him. That is the
whole of what a Fitch sees, and a writer should render no more of him here —
what he is, who he answers to, and what he does with the faces are the Custody's
to know, not the parish's (his sheet, and `02_the_heretic_cell.md` §9). To Noll,
Rue is one more man who has turned up at his door, and he gives him precisely
what he gives the hangman: the weather, and nothing else. The two of them stand
in the same churchyard at the same burials, the sexton who has decided not to
know and the clerk who is paid to find out, each perfectly courteous, each
perfectly aware of the other, and neither ever moves — a standing, silent,
unresolved balance that a writer can set a whole scene inside and must never
tip.

**Ewart, on the near edge of it.** The succession danger lives here too, and it
stays open. Ewart has the surface — the oiled hinge, the feet on the stair, the
grey clerk counting faces — because you cannot dig in that yard and not see
them; he does *not* have his father's reading of what they mean, because Noll
looked at him a long moment when he asked and went back to the chalk. So the
deepest thing the bench holds has not passed to the heir, and may never: it is
not taught with the spade, it is not written in the book, and Noll has arranged
his own not-knowing so carefully that there may be, in the end, nothing to hand
over but the habit of oiling a hinge and asking no questions. **Whether the
lintel-knowledge dies with Noll is not answered by this document.** Do not
answer it. The gravedigger may inherit the whole trade and never the one thing,
and that may be exactly what the sexton, who buried his own child unrung to
spare himself the counting, would choose for his living son.

## The year, from the sexton's bench

The Reed Ward's calendar is worked out in `../the_dry_boatmen.md` as the ward
lives it. This is the same year from the churchyard, where several of the ward's
feasts turn out to be, on the sexton's side of them, work — bell-work,
grave-work, and the keeping of a name. Reach for this when the scene is at
Saint Maren's and not on the Green; do not re-narrate the ward's version.

**Colm's Night** (early spring). The boat-families burn their green-dipped
candles at the dry Old Sluice for the drowned Colm; the sexton's part is small
and it is telling. Colm was, by every rule, exactly the kind of man Noll spends
his life quietly getting *onto* the list — a banished heretic drowned outside
the sacraments — and the Reed Ward has made him a saint of its own without asking
the Chapter's leave. Noll neither burns a candle at the sluice nor stops anyone
who does. A sexton who enters a suicide as *found drowned* is not going to be
scandalised by a ward that keeps a drowned preacher's night, and a man who takes
an unnamed purse has excellent professional reasons to affirm nothing about
anything on a night when a grey coat is somewhere on the sluice road being seen.
He keeps the church, keeps his counsel, and rakes no grate — that is the Alders'
gate, not his.

**Vhairestide, the Walking Out** (spring). The ward walks out the Reed Postern
to bless the first boats, and the sexton mostly stays: someone keeps the church
and the dead do not take feast-days off. But the postern is also the funeral
gate — the ward's dead come home through it and up Maren's Slip — so for a Fitch
the one day the postern is thrown wide for a *living* crowd is a strange,
pleasant inversion of every other time he stands at it, and Noll has been known
to watch the ward go out to the water and think that it is the only procession
through that gate that is not his to bury.

**Marenstide, the roll of the drowned** (late summer). The eel fair fills the
Green, and inside it the roll of the drowned is read at Saint Maren's, and the
sexton's bench is caught square in the ward's sharpest argument. Since the
diversion almost no one drowns in the city, so the roll grows now only by
adding the Hammering dead — and the traditional families answer *you cannot
drown under slate.* The reading is Renn Hobbe's; but the *writing* is Noll's,
because the roll is kept in the same hand that keeps everything, and here his
own grief and his own office pull opposite ways. He never accepted that the
common toll named the Hammering dead, and a roll that at least *says their
names once a year* is a bell he can still ring for them; so the sexton is, in
his flat way, for adding them. But he also knows the roll of the *drowned* is a
lie about how they died, and he is the ward's most exact man about what the book
says, and his own daughter Clemence is precisely the kind of Hammering dead the
roll now carries — read as drowned once a year, and unrung. He wants her name
said more than he wants the roll kept pure, and cannot have both, and does not
say so, and takes no side in the porch where the argument is had, because the
side he is on is *both,* which is no side at all. Leave the quarrel open; it is
not his to settle and he would not if it were.

**The Hammering anniversary** (late summer). The common toll at the Waning, and
the one day the sexton is entirely still. He does not ring; the toll is the
Lanthorn's, and it is the toll he has never accepted, and he stands in his
churchyard among the F.415 trenches and the unrung and lets it sound, and thinks
the sentence he has thought two-and-twenty times, and goes back to his work. It
is the least dramatic and most complete grief in the corpus: a man who rings
everyone, standing silent under a bell he did not cast for a rite he does not
believe, above the ground he filled the year he was five-and-thirty.

**Bellday, Belwyn's Dark, and Gaudry's Audit** (in brief). Weekly **Bellday**
puts the folk in the nave to speak their dead into the green beam — the sexton's
professional cousin-rite, a name said for the dead in light where his is said in
bronze, and he neither encourages nor mocks it, because *affirm nothing* is the
whole city's counsel and doubly a sexton's (the beam and what it does or does
not hear stay exactly as canon leaves them: never confirmed, never denied). At
midwinter **Belwyn's Dark**, the year's one office sung without lights, a
burial house is at home in a way it is at no other feast — the whole church
keeping a dark that the Fitches keep for a living. And at **Gaudry's Audit** in
late winter, when the city proves its weights, the sexton has nothing to weigh
but is the man everyone half-remembers is owed a settling too: the belated
knells are the ward's oldest unaudited debt, and a Fitch could stand up at the
proving of the standards and ask who audits the bells the city owes its dead,
and never does, because it is not that kind of ledger.

**The frost** closes the section as it closes the ground. When it freezes hard,
a grave is murder to dig and dear, and the dead of a hard week may wait, boarded
in the cold church, for ground soft enough to take them — which is the sexton's
own version of the ward's dry drowning, a debt of unburied names piling up in
the cold that the first thaw calls in all at once.

## How they talk

The whole river-language of the Reed Ward — directions by upstream and
downstream, *the dry carry*, *come by barrow*, *a dry drowning* — belongs to any
Fitch mouth on the Green (`../the_dry_boatmen.md`), because the Fitches are of
the ward even if they are not of the water. What follows is narrower: how you
mark a speaker as a **Fitch** in particular, which is a matter of a flat mercy
and a trade full of names. These are direct prompt fodder; each is injectable on
its own.

**The Fitch register is dry, short, and funnier than the moment allows.** A
Fitch — the sexton above all, but it runs in the name — meets the worst mornings
of other people's lives for a living, and has learned that grief cannot be
argued with and can, sometimes, be briefly set down by a plain sentence said
level, over a task, that turns out to be a small joke the mourner did not know
they needed. The most Fitch line in the game is the one that answers a weeping
question with a true, dry, practical fact and lets the mourner find the comfort
in it themselves. The second most Fitch line is a name — anyone's, exact,
remembered — dropped into a conversation to prove the speaker was paying
attention, because in this house *being remembered* and *being loved* are very
nearly the same verb.

The house sayings, and the two that are canon, all cut at least two ways:

> **"Chalk washes off. Names don't."** Noll's, and the whole trade in four
> words. Said to comfort — *your dead is in the book, safe, permanent, past the
> reach of weather* — and said, to a man who is lying to the sexton about a
> death, as the flattest possible warning: *I write it down, and what I write
> down is what happened, so be careful what you tell me.* The kindest and the
> most dangerous sentence in the family, and the same sentence.

> **"One stroke a year. Short lives ring quickest, and I hate a quick bell."**
> Noll's again, said as plain arithmetic to anyone who asks how the knell works,
> and said as the nearest thing he has to a prayer over a child's grave. It is
> the sound of a man who has counted more short lives than anyone in the city
> refusing, out loud, to pretend a dozen strokes is enough — and it tells a
> listener instantly that this mild man has a grief in him he rings and does not
> speak.

> **"Everyone gets a name and a bell."** The house politics, said with a shrug
> as a description of the trade and said, when the wall or the Chapter or a
> proud family wants someone left *off* the list, as the one thing a Fitch will
> plant his feet on. From him it is not defiance; it is bookkeeping. But it is
> the bookkeeping the whole quiet crookedness of the register serves, and a
> listener who hears a Fitch say it slowly is hearing a man tell you he will
> find a way to write your dead in, whatever the rule says.

> **"On the purse."** Trade shorthand for a pauper's funeral paid by the hand
> that does not sign — *he went in on the purse* — said plainly, without lowered
> voice, because it is ordinary and public. Said among the careful few of the
> parish, with a beat of silence after it, it means something else entirely, and
> the difference between the two ways of saying it is the difference between a
> safe conversation and a dangerous one.

> **"Guess an age; a guess is a name."** How a Fitch buries a stranger with no
> years anyone can give — you ring what you'd guess, because a guessed knell is
> still a knell and a nameless grave is the one unbearable thing. Said of the
> trade, it is tender. Said of a man being too exact about someone else's
> business, it is a rebuke: *stop measuring; some things you ring on a guess and
> move on.*

> **"That one's for the book, not the door."** A thing you know and will keep —
> that goes in the private ink and never on the public chalk. The Fitch version
> of the Alders' *keep it for the beam*: every burial house learns which truths
> are chalked where the ward can read them and which are entered where only the
> sexton ever will, and a Fitch who says *that's for the book* has told you the
> subject is closed and safe with him at once.

A note on how a Fitch and a boatman differ in the same ward, because it is a
fast way to place a speaker. A boat-family sounds like a depth — level, weather
first, close-mouthed on exactly one subject. A Fitch sounds like a **roll
called** — the flatness is the same, but where a boatman withholds, a Fitch
*names*: the dead, the debts, the years, the faces, all remembered exactly and
produced without effort, because remembering is the trade. Put the two side by
side at a graveside and the boatman says nothing and means it and the sexton
says the dead man's name and his father's name and the year, and both are
grieving, and only one of them will tell you who is in the ground.

## Quarrels worth playing

Every one is live in F.437 and none has a right answer.

1. **The wharf-born.** Is a child born in a shed on the strip a Reed Ward
   resident? Does the household owe ward-tax; may the father stand for bencher?
   The wall says no and the register says whatever Noll wrote, and Noll has
   dated more than one such birth to the day the family first paid into the
   parish box rather than the day of the birth — *of the ward from the box-day,
   and let the wall argue with the box.* The Tallage would call it a fraud on the
   ward-roll; the family calls it a christening. Do not resolve whether the
   entries are honest. The register-power is the content, and the wall and the
   book have been fighting over it longer than anyone alive.

2. **Who audits the sexton.** There is no second copy of the register and no one
   who checks it. Every borderline birth, death, and residence in the ward is a
   Fitch's pen against no one, in a book that is treated as fact. Most of the
   ward is glad of it, because Noll's thumb is always on the side of getting a
   person *onto* the list — but a thumb is a thumb, and the day a Fitch weighed
   an entry the other way, there would be nothing to weigh it against. Whether
   an office no one audits is a mercy or a danger is a real question with no
   answer, and it is sharpest precisely because the man holding the pen is
   trusted.

3. **The roll of the drowned.** Whether the Hammering dead belong on it —
   *you cannot drown under slate* against *one bell is not a name.* Fought in the
   church porch, the priest reading and the sexton writing, and unwinnable
   because the sexton is on both sides of it: he wants the names said and knows
   the roll lies about how they died, and his own child is on it. Never settle
   it (`../the_dry_boatmen.md` quarrel 5, `family_alder.md`).

4. **The succession — the spade and the keys.** Ewart will get the spade; will
   he get the bell, the book, the keys, and the one thing under them? The Chapter
   gives the bench, not the blood, and a sexton's post handed to a man who is not
   a Fitch takes the name off the house in a morning. Noll has taught the digging
   and withheld the reading of the lintel, perhaps forever. Play the whole
   unspoken want of it — a son who cannot say he wants his father's place, a
   father who cannot say whether he means to pass the dangerous half — and do not
   resolve who ends with what.

5. **Should the poor pay for a name?** A pauper's funeral with a knell is ninety-six
   sparks; a pauper cannot pay ninety-six sparks; a purse pays it. But a belated
   name-knell for a family's Unknelled is also money, and traditional houses
   scrape for years to buy their dead a bell they were already owed once. Is the
   grief-debt a debt the living should have to pay at all — or did the Chapter
   incur it in F.415 and quietly hand the bill to the bereaved? The Fitches ring
   the bell either way and keep the count; the justice of the count is the
   quarrel, and it is never had out loud at the tower.

6. **The hanged, the self-slain, and consecrated ground.** Noll rings and
   buries the gallows-dead and the suicides inside the hallowed ground, on the
   strength of Saint Maren being patron of the *lost* and a bell that does not
   know what a man did. Not everyone in the parish thinks that is his to decide,
   and a stricter wick-priest than Renn Hobbe, or a Chapter in a bad mood, could
   make it a fight. Whether *everyone gets a name and a bell* is mercy or
   overreach is the oldest argument the bench has with the rule-book, and Noll
   wins it only by acting before anyone can stop him.

7. **"Grave water."** Outsiders call Reed Cistern's water *grave water* because
   Saint Maren's churchyard and charnel door are near the Green, and the insult
   is materially wrong — the cistern is roof-fed, raised, and sealed from the
   churchyard (`../wells_and_water.md`). Reed residents who know every gutter
   hear it as ignorance and contempt at once, and a Fitch hears it as an insult
   to the ground itself. It is a live slur with a factual answer that never
   catches up to it, which is exactly why it is worth playing: the answer is
   true and the insult wins anyway.

8. **The name at the edge.** A house honest to self-impoverishment at its centre
   runs, at its rim, to a milk-seller with a short measure and a thumb near the
   scale — ordinary poor-ward crookedness, no scandal, and the same surname as
   the ward's most trusted book. Does the honest centre owe the crooked edge
   anything; does the crooked edge shame the name, or is a family entitled to be
   both at once? The ward enjoys the contradiction and never presses it, and a
   scene that presses it — a Fitch cousin caught short-weighing, and the sexton's
   name in it — has real teeth.

## What this document does not settle

Deliberately open, per the canon rules; a later writer must not close any of
these:

- **The wharf-born quarrel, and the ward-tax under it.** The register makes
  people of the ward or leaves them out, and Noll has *written down a number of
  things*, but nothing here rules whether a wharf-born child owes tax, may stand
  for bencher, or is truly of the ward. No register scandal is invented that
  settles it; the entries stand, the wall and the book stay locked, and the
  quarrel is the content.

- **Whether the lintel-knowledge passes to Ewart, or dies with Noll.** The
  succession of the *spade* is plain; the succession of the *knowing* is not,
  and this document leaves it exactly unresolved. The heir may inherit the whole
  trade and never the one thing his father arranged never quite to know.

- **Noll's knowledge boundary, held to the grain.** He knows a purse pays, that
  his lintel is read for something, and that someone uses his crypt, and he has
  taken care to learn no names — not who pays, not who meets, not what they call
  themselves or believe. Do not give him one word more
  (`../second_sun/05_dramatis_personae.md` #15). The purse, the meeters, and
  every officer belong to `../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` and its §12, not
  to any Fitch and not to this file.

- **Nothing supernatural in the bell, the chalk, or the ground.** The name-knell
  remembers; it does not reach the dead. The belated knells are human
  remembrance and no proof that anyone answers. Whether the folk are right that a
  name spoken in the green beam is heard stays precisely as canon leaves it —
  never confirmed, never mocked, and never by a Fitch, who deals in names said
  in bronze and ink and would tell you, if pressed, that he cannot speak for the
  light. No well under the churchyard, no water in the dry crypt, no grave that
  keeps anything but a body (`../wells_and_water.md`, `00_canon.md`).

- **Whether Noll ever rings Clemence, or the Alders ever ring Corin.** Two owed
  knells stand unrung in the same tower. The not-ringing is the grief; a later
  writer may bring a family to the bell, but this document rings neither, and
  the debt outstanding is the truer thing than any release of it would be.

- **The precise kinship of the wider name.** Ede and Betriss are Fitches of the
  name, kin at some remove to the bench and not of the cottage; the sheets do
  not make either a child or niece of Noll's, and neither does this file. Keep
  them at loose remove — of the same grandfathers — and hang no closer living
  tie without a sheet to carry it.

## Secret tier — designers only

*Family-tier and Noll-tier knowledge. The sections above are written so any of
them could be injected into a Fitch's context; this one may not. And note first
what makes this house's secret tier unusual: it holds almost nothing, on
purpose, because its central character has spent forty years making sure he has
almost nothing to hold.*

- **Noll's tier — his, and no other Fitch's, and holding no names.** What Noll
  has worked out and keeps behind his teeth: that the freshly chalked name and
  the slow knell serve as a pass for something that meets in his crypt after a
  pauper's burial; that an unnamed purse pays those funerals and has for years;
  that he keeps the hinge oiled and himself elsewhere and has deliberately never
  learned a single name attached to any of it. **This is a boundary, and it is
  the whole of the boundary.** He does not know who they are, their number, their
  belief, their word for themselves, their officers, or the source of the purse.
  Even at this deepest tier the knowledge is *nameless* — that is the design of
  the man. Designers filling in what actually stands behind the door go to
  `../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` (§3 the charity, §5 the pass, §6 the
  crypt meetings, §9 Saint Maren's) and its **§12** for the officers and the
  Tracer and the purse-holder — none of which may ever be written into a Fitch's
  context, Noll's included. If you need the cell's own injectable, it is that
  document's §13, not this one.

- **The fence around Ewart.** Ewart has the observations (oiled hinge, feet on
  the stair, the grey clerk) and not the reading. Noll's worked-out conclusion
  has *not* passed to him and may never. Do not let Ewart's context carry the
  interpretation; he wonders, and does not know, and asked once and was answered
  with silence. The whole point of the seam is that the deepest thing the bench
  holds is the one thing not taught with the trade.

- **Tilman Rue is rendered here Fitch-side only.** In any Fitch context he is a
  mild, courteous grey clerk who stands at the paupers' burials, whom Noll gives
  the weather and nothing else. His office, his handling of informants, his
  fair-copied lists, and what he is actually building belong to his own sheet and
  to the Custody material, never to a Fitch. A Fitch who spoke Rue's real trade
  aloud would be a Fitch who had learned something no Fitch is given.

- **The register's crooked entries are family-tier fact, not public.** The
  box-dated wharf birth, the man kept *gone* rather than *dead*, the suicide
  entered *found drowned* — these are true of the book and known, at most, to the
  one or two people each touches and to the sexton. They are injectable into
  Noll's own context (he did them, and stands by them) but must not be spoken by
  him to strangers as confession; his public face is that the book is the book
  and the poor get a name from some kind soul. He is not lying. He is not saying.

- **What is genuinely open, and must stay so:** whether Ewart ever gets the
  reading; whether Noll ever rings Clemence; whether the wharf-born entries are
  honest; and the entire shape of what the lintel serves, which is not this
  house's to resolve. This treatment invents inward — the bench, the book, the
  Fitch dead, the grief-debt ledger — and points outward for everything that
  belongs to the cell, the Custody, or the phenomenon.
