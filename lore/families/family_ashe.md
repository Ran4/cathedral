## The thing to get right first

**The Ashes are not comfortable.** The crest at the head of this page, the **S**
against their name on the roster, the word *standing*, the salt-cellars by the
Tallage — every first contact says *solid merchant house*, and the eye slides on
to the next name. It is wrong. The Ashes are a **lesser** house of standing —
the glossary of names lists them so, in as many words, among the lesser lines
below Sparr and Copp and Alder — and the whole of what the city reads as their
character is the proof of it. The counting-twice, the jumpiness, the mercenary
edge, the weighing of a stranger before a word of help is offered: none of that
is the manner of a house at ease. It is the manner of a house that is one bad
season from the fall and knows it to the grain.

Correct the misreading with one fact about the thing they sell, and the whole
family follows.

**Salt is the anti-mystery trade.** It does not transform; it *preserves.* It
keeps the meat from rotting and the fish from turning, the hides out of the
tan-pit's ruin, the winter store alive till spring. It is the one commodity in
Ombreval whose whole virtue is that it changes nothing and holds everything
where it is. And it is weighed, proved, sealed, and known to the grain — Salorge
pan-salt poled six days up the Serle against the current, landed at the outer
wharves, carted through the gate and tolled, weighed at Lowmarket against the
Tallage stone and the sealed Gaudry weights, stored under lock in the cellars
off the Tallage. A salt house lives its whole working life **in the open, on the
scale, under the official weigher's eye**, and its one boast is that nothing it
sells is anything but what the beam says it is. There is no house in the city
less shaped to keep a secret, and none whose entire dignity rests on being
exactly, provably, boringly what it claims. The Ashes are the people who make
you count it twice in daylight, and are never thanked for it, and do not care.

From that, two facts that between them are the whole family.

**The first is the anchor, and it is a dead house's cellars.** A fixed name in
Ombreval is a lease, not a possession — the naming law says it plainly: a
surname lasts only as long as the thing that anchored it, a boat, a yard, a
warehouse, a bench of trade, and when the anchor goes the street simply stops
giving the name to the children. The Ashe anchor is a **leasehold in the
cellars of the extinct Gaunt house**, off Gaunt Passage near the Tallage —
thick, vented, dry stone raised by a salt-factor line that is gone, root and
branch, so that the passage now keeps the name the family could not. *That is
how a passage comes to be named for people who are not there.* The Ashes do not
merely live one lease over from a house that fell; **they store their salt in
the fallen house's own cellars, under a passage that wears its name like a
headstone,** and every Ashe who has ever backed a barrow down Gaunt Passage has
worked all day inside the exact shape of what the naming law does to a salt
family that loses its grip. No other standing house in this folder holds an
anchor so plainly borrowed from the dead. It is the most precarious *S* on the
roster, and the family carries it the way you carry something you know is not
quite yours to keep.

**The second is the hole in the deed.** Old Dunstan Ashe held the Gaunt cellars
and wrote down half of them and never troubled to write the rest, because he
knew what was his and did not expect to die confused. He died confused, the way
everyone does. He left a house whose entire trade is proving that a weight is
exactly what it claims to be — and a title to its own floor with a hole in it
deep enough to fall through. So the family that will not buy a salt-weigh on
trust is now at law with itself, before the notary, over a cellar nobody ever
put on paper; and the question under the lawsuit is the question the whole house
cannot stop answering, the way the boat-families cannot stop answering *do we
keep the boat?*

**What is an unwritten thing worth?**

Every Ashe of consequence holds a different answer, and the answers are livable
and chronic and never said aloud at one table:

- **Grigor**, who holds the keys, answers *possession*: what a man has in his
  hand and under his boots is his, written or not, and a claim you cannot weigh
  is a claim that does not weigh. The Tallage reads this as a grasping man's
  greed, and his sister reads it as a brother trying to take a thing while she
  was in black, and both readings are fair on the evidence they have.
- **Rohese**, who keeps the books, answers *nothing*: an unwritten claim weighs
  exactly what an unproved weight weighs, which is to say it is a lie until the
  beam settles. She wants the deed drawn, sealed, and witnessed like a standard
  proved on the stone — not for the cellar's worth, which is small, but because
  the Ashe name rests on nothing being taken on faith, and she means her brother
  to learn it in the one currency he respects.
- **Tobin** does not want the cellar at all. He wants a place on a salt-barge
  down to Salorge, six days with the current, to see the pans with his own eyes
  before he is old — a want he has never dared put into words either, kept as
  unwritten as the deed, and worth, to him, everything the cellar is not.
- **Cobb** answers *a debt*. His father is dead under the salt he still hauls,
  and he counts every sack he shifts as what is owed, though he could not tell
  you owed by whom.
- **The scattered name** answers, in aggregate, *the deed is for people with
  something to deed.* A washerwoman in Reed and a tile-maker by the wall hold no
  cellar and no lease; to them the surname is a sound, and a sound is not a
  title, and the whole quarrel is a rich relation's trouble.

Hold all five apart, keep them chronic, and **never resolve the suit.** The day
the deed is signed — either way — the Ashes are a different family, and this
document's whole engine has been spent.

## The salt, stated plainly

The Ashe trade is a road with two ends and a beam in the middle, and it is worth
knowing bell to bell, because half the family's waking life is arranged around
it and the other half is arranged around who owns which end.

**The road.** The Serle runs one water from the Combs to the sea, and salt comes
from the far, low end of it. At **Salorge**, where the river finds the sea among
the salt-pans, the pan-workers rake and dry and sack it, and it goes up in
barges — **six days down with the current from the city, twelve back up against
it** — past Harne, past the castle that once put a chain across the throat of
all of this (F.54; below), to the outer wharves beyond the south wall. This is
**the salt road**, and it is the Ashes' lawful living the way the water is the
boatmen's. A salt-house child learns the road as a shape before ever seeing a
map: down is fast and up is slow, the pans are six days gone, and everything the
family eats came the hard way, against the current, with a toll at every reach.

**The dry carry, in salt.** What the diversion did to the eel it did to the
salt. Before F.362 a barge came up the Cut to a water-door and the salt came off
the deck into the cellar in a single lift; since F.363–369 the Serle runs
outside the wall, the Cut is a dry street, and every weigh of salt is now landed
at the outer wharf, carried up the bank by paid hands, loaded on carts by paid
porters, brought through the gate and **tolled**, hauled up the dry reach to the
Tallage, and lifted a second time into a cellar whose old hoist was built to
take things off a boat that will never come again. The Reed Ward calls the
doubled handling **the dry carry** and means the whole wrong shape of the world
by it (`../the_dry_boatmen.md`); a salt house says it too, but with a difference
the boat-families do not grant them. The Ashes are not on the water end of the
carry. They own the cellar at the *inner* end — the second lift is into *their*
store — so the dry carry costs an Ashe a toll and a cartage and pays an Ashe a
rent, and where a boatman curses it whole-heartedly a salt-merchant curses it
out of one side of his mouth and collects with the other hand. Remember that
whenever an Ashe complains about the toll: he means it, and he is also the
warehouse the toll delivers to.

**The beam.** Salt is measured by the **salt-weigh**, and the salt-weigh is
proved, like the cloth-ell and the Serle tun, against the **Tallage stone** and
the sealed brass **Gaudry weights** chained in the customs square. The weighing
is done at the Tallage on **Lowmarket** — the sixth day of the week, the market
day of the Tallage and Maren's Green. *Lowmarket is a day, not a place;* "the
salt's weighed at Lowmarket" means *on the sixth, under the beam, in the open,*
where anyone at all may stand and watch it read. The warden of the beam in F.437
is old **Ewart Tarn**, thirty years keeper of the chained weights, whose sight
is failing and whose daughter reads the beam aloud for him so that no one need
know — a courtesy the whole Tallage extends and the Ashes extend hardest,
because a salt house needs the beam honest far more than it needs the weigher
young. The salt is queued, weighed, tallied, tolled, and carted the short dogleg
to the cellars; a bill of lading is drawn — most of them, at the Tallage, by the
slow, incorruptible notary **Odo Trask** — and the salt goes down under the
Gaunt house to wait for a buyer.

**Nobody cheats him twice.** The one thing every Ashe can do, and the family's
oldest capital, is tell to the grain what a salt-weigh ought to come to. Grigor
can look at a manifest and a queue and a day's damp and say what the beam will
read before it reads, and be right — and this is not a gift of his but a
house-trait older than he is: the Ashes have weighed in their heads, against the
day the beam lies, since before Dunstan's father took the Gaunt keys. It is why
they are trusted and why they are disliked. A man who counts your salt twice in
front of you has told you, courteously, that he expects to be robbed, and is
usually right, and is never grateful to be. **"Nobody cheats an Ashe twice"** is
said in the customs square as praise and as complaint in one breath, and the
family stopped hearing the complaint two generations ago. They are exact in a
world that is not: the wharf runs on shaded tallies — a porter who writes a load
lighter than it is for a cut, a Salorge factor who lands two tuns unweighed in
every ten — and the Ashe boast is precisely that in *that* world they cannot be
had. Exactness, to an Ashe, is not virtue. It is armour.

**The numbers, roughly**, so a designer can price a scene. A journeyman's day is
about three bells; a pauper's funeral with a knell, eight; a loaf, two sparks —
twelve sparks to the silver bell, sixty bells to the gold lantern, which no
salt-hand ever handles. Salt is bulk, and coin is thin: the money is in the
*weigh* and the *store*, not the pinch. An Ashe lives on the spread between what
salt costs landed and what it fetches out of a dry cellar in a hard month; on
the barge-hire Rohese lets against the season; on the cellar-rent other
salt-users pay for Gaunt storage the Ashes hold the lease of; and on the small
mercy that the tolls fall on the *load* and not on the *keeping.* It is a good
living and a nervous one. Salt does not spoil, but a cellar of it is a fortune
that sits still, and a fortune that sits still is a fortune somebody is thinking
about — a freeze that stops the barges, a Salorge contract gone bad, a lawsuit,
a lease with a hole in it. The Ashes are never poor and never easy, which is its
own kind of exactness.

**What the salt is *for.*** It leaves the Gaunt cellars into every trade in the
city that fights rot. The shambles salt meat; the fish-smokers of Maren's Green
salt eel and herring by the weigh — the widowed smoker Averil Skell buys her
smoking-salt off Grigor's beam, pays cash, and is feared rather than pitied for
it; the tanners on Tanners' Slip want it for hides; the healers want it; the
laundries want it for the boiling-coppers; and every household in the city wants
its winter store salted down before Belwyn's Dark or goes hungry by spring. The
Ashe name runs, at its working end, straight into all of this: the family that
carts the salt in also *uses* it — a washerwoman's copper, a slaughterer's tub —
so that the two poles of the whole scattered house, the counting-room in Weigh
and the wash-house in Reed, are the two ends of one grain of salt. More on that
where the scatter is treated, below.

## The two heads of the trade

There are two salt-traders in the Ashe house, which means the lawful trade has
two heads, and getting the division right is getting the household right,
because the division has quietly reversed and nobody has said so.

**Grigor is the merchant.** His is the buying end and the name end: he holds the
keys to the cellars, deals at the source with the Salorge factors and the barge
masters, sets the price, carries the family's credit at the Tallage, and owns
the one skill the name is famous for — the grain-exact eye that tells a weigh
before the beam does. The merchant is, by the trade's old order, the head of the
house; the name over the cellar door is his; when Averil Skell says she buys
"off Grigor's beam" she names the merchant, not the man who actually carried her
sacks. On paper, and in the square's eye, Grigor is the Ashe salt trade.

**Rohese is the factor.** Hers is the moving and keeping end: the ledgers, the
Lowmarket weighings, the barge-hire let against the season, the credit given and
called, the tally of what is in which cellar and what it will fetch and when.
The factor serves the merchant, by the old order — keeps his books, moves his
salt, and defers.

That is the order on paper. In F.437 it has turned over, and this is the fact
the whole household is built on and nobody states: **these two years Grigor's
mind has been anywhere but his cellars,** out on business he does not name and
paying what he is asked to be rid of the asking, and so the salt now moves
because *Rohese* moves it. She hires the barges and reads the weighs and holds
the credit together while the merchant drifts; every clerk at the Tallage knows
the trade is hers to run and only the merchant himself does not seem to see it.
She does the work of both heads, under the name of neither, for a brother who
keeps the keys and cannot keep his attention on the salt — and she has buried a
husband and is raising a boy and is suing that same brother for the deed to a
cellar, all at once, and has not once been seen to weep at any of it. When
designers want the Ashe trade to *function* in a scene, it is Rohese's hand on
it. When they want it to *fail* — a stack badly built, an order let slip, a
barge unmet at the wharf — it is because the merchant's mind was on his other
business and the factor could not be in two cellars at once.

The reversal has a mirror across the beam. The Tallage's own weighing runs, in
F.437, on two competent people covering for two failing ones: Rohese the factor,
carrying a brother who has stopped attending, deals daily across the stone with
**Averil Tarn** the toll-clerk, who runs the toll-rolls and reads the beam aloud
for a father whose sight is failing. Two women keep the salt of Ombreval honest and
moving while the men whose names are on the offices look elsewhere, and neither
woman is thanked for it, and both would think being thanked a waste of a
morning.

## The dead name over the door

The single most useful thing to hold about the Ashes is *where they work,*
because where they work is a warning with their own future written in it.

The old **Gaunt house** was a salt-factor's hall — the same trade, the same
cellars, the same beam-exact living the Ashes make now — and the Gaunt line is
**extinct, root and branch.** Not fallen to bynames, not scattered to the poor
streets; simply *ended,* the last of them dead with no heir to give the name to,
so that the name came off the living altogether and stuck to the only thing that
could not walk away from it: the passage under the cellars, which the city still
calls **Gaunt Passage** because a passage takes the name of what it passes
beneath and does not care whether the family is there to answer to it. This is
the naming law in its coldest form — not *there are more Hawsers than Alders,*
the fall to a byname, but the rarer, quieter end past that, where there is no
byname either because there is no one left to carry one, and a family survives
only as the label on a piece of stone.

**The Ashes lease those cellars and work inside them.** Every day, an Ashe backs
a barrow down a passage named for a dead salt-family, into vaults a dead
salt-family raised, to weigh and store the exact commodity the dead salt-family
sold, and thinks nothing of it, because it is Tuesday and the salt is in. But
the shape is there under everything, and on the family's worse nights it is not
lost on them: **the Gaunt house is what an Ashe becomes if the lease fails and
the line thins — a good name a passage keeps after the people are gone.** The
lawsuit over the unwritten half is, at its floor, a fight about not becoming
Gaunt. Rohese wants the deed sealed because an unsealed lease is how a salt
family ends up a street sign; Grigor holds the keys because keys are the one
thing the dead Gaunt did not manage to hold. Neither of them would put it that
way. Both of them dream in it.

There is a ghost to go with the dead house, as there is under every old cellar
in the city, and this one is canon folk custom: a fuller dead of the damp is
said to walk Gaunt Passage wringing his cloth, and a salt-porter who once
lingered too long at the dark midpoint went **deaf for three days, his ears
full of a water no one could see.** The ward's rule is exact — *walk the
passage quick, and if you must
pass it at night, say "good night to the trade," and never guess at his name.*
The Ashes keep the custom like everyone: a hand leaving the cellars at Lamplight
says *good night to the trade* to the dark, and means the dead Gaunt and the
damp-dead fuller and the whole weight of salt-people gone before, and does not
linger. Grigor keeps it hardest of anyone, and has been heard to add details to
the tale — the cold spot, the smell of the tan-pit where no tannery is, the
sound of a barrow that no one is pushing — with a salt-merchant's plain interest
in a story that keeps idlers and rivals out of his stock after dark. That is all
it is, above ground: a mercenary man tending a useful ghost. *Never guess at his
name* is the ward's line, not the family's, and it is a line about a drowned
fuller, and it means nothing that a designer needs to reach for here.

## The households

The Ashe name is a lease on a dead house's cellars, and each Ashe of consequence
stands at a different distance from the salt that holds the name up and holds a
different answer to *what an unwritten thing is worth.* Nobody here is about to
do anything drastic; that is not the register. They are simply, each of them, in
a fixed position around one cellar and one lawsuit, and the positions do not
move, and everyone can feel them.

### Grigor Ashe — the merchant who keeps his own counsel

Grigor (41, salt-merchant) is the head of the house, the keeper of the keys, and
the hardest of the family to read, because two years ago he stopped explaining
himself and has not started again. Read the surface first, because the surface
is most of what any other Ashe has to go on: he is **exact, jumpy, and
mercenary** — counts everything twice, prices everything, trusts nothing on a
stranger's word, and can tell you to the grain what a weigh will come to and
usually what it cost the man who brought it. He is not warm. He is not unkind,
quite; he is *guarded,* in the specific way of a man who has decided the world is
trying to take something off him and has resolved to weigh it first.

What the household actually sees, and cannot get past, is the **night business.**
For two years Grigor has gone out after the Snuffing on errands he will not name,
and come home saying nothing, and paid whatever he is asked to be rid of the
asking. He works one particular cellar himself, at odd hours, and does not care
to have company down there after Lamplight; a hand who wanders in late gets sent
up short. The family has settled the matter each in their own way — Tobin has
decided, easily, that it is only salt that never met the weigh-beam, because half
the salt in the city dodges the beam and it is more comfortable to think that
than to think further; Rohese has decided it is her brother letting the trade
slide for some squalid private dealing and has stopped asking because the asking
costs her more than the answer would. Both readings are wrong, both are
injectable, and neither holds a grain of the truth, which is at the foot of this
document and belongs to no other Ashe.

His fight over the cellars looks, from every side that can see it, like **greed.**
His widowed sister has taken him before the notary for the unwritten half, and he
will not let it go, and the plain reading — hers, the square's, the Tallage's —
is that Grigor Ashe is grasping at a dead father's cellar while his sister stands
in black, because a mercenary man cannot bear to sign a thing away. Let that
reading stand above the fence; it is exactly what the evidence supports, and
exactly what he wants believed, and it costs him his sister's regard, which he
pays without visibly minding, and sleeps badly, and pays again.

There is one thing he means to hold whatever it costs him, and it is the only
tender thing the household can see in him without knowing why: **he keeps Tobin
clear of the night business.** The boy works the cellars all day, and Grigor lets
him think the night errands are smuggled salt, and never corrects it, and never
lets him come along; and if you watch closely, the whole of Grigor's care for his
son is spent on that one exclusion — *you are a pair of hands, boy, and you will
stay a pair of hands, and you will not be in this with me.* Tobin reads it as
coldness. It is the nearest thing to love Grigor has room for, and it looks
exactly like being kept at arm's length, because that is what it is.

### Rohese Ashe — the factor who carries it all

Rohese (36, salt-factor, widow) is the one keeping the house upright, and the
last one who would say so. She runs the trade her brother has stopped attending —
the ledgers, the weighings, the barge-hire, the credit — runs it well and runs
it grimly, and every clerk at the Tallage knows the salt moves because she moves
it. She is **dry, exact, and short with fools**; she counts coin slowly in front
of a man to make him nervous, and it works; and nobody in this city has seen her
weep, which the city takes for hardness and which is closer to a decision she
made once and has not revisited.

She made it over a grave. Her husband — a Hobbe of the carrying house, a man who
took Ashe wages and hauled Ashe salt — lay **three days** under what a fallen
salt-stack had done to him before it finished him, and it was **Grigor who
stacked it,** in the season his mind first went elsewhere; and Rohese was back at
the beam the week they buried him, because the salt does not weigh itself and the
boy had to eat. She does not say her husband's name often. She does not say much
about any of it. What she does instead is sue: she has taken Grigor before Odo
Trask for the deed to the unwritten half of the cellars, and the suit is not
truly about the cellar's small worth. It is about a woman who has learned, the
hard way and twice over, that **an unwritten thing is a thing that can be taken
from you the moment you look away,** and who means the deed drawn, sealed, and
witnessed like a standard proved on the stone, and means her brother to know he
could not simply keep it while she was in black. *An unproved weight is a lie
until the beam settles* is the whole of her creed, and she is applying it to her
own family, and she is not wrong, which is the worst of it.

Her grief has two live edges, and neither is the husband. The first is her son.
**Cobb** is fifteen and hauls sacks on his own back like any hired porter, and it
shames her more than the lawsuit does — a factor's son at a porter's work, in her
own brother's cellars, for her own brother's wage — and she tells herself it will
make a man of him and does not believe it. The second is her nephew. She is fond
of **Tobin** in spite of everything, fonder than she is comfortable being while
she is suing his father, and it is Tobin — not Grigor, never Grigor — she is
afraid of hurting. She can carry a suit against her brother without a flicker;
what she cannot quite carry is that winning it might land on the boy who shovels
beside her son and laughs at his own jokes and has done nothing to anyone. Play
that: Rohese is not afraid of Grigor and not afraid of losing. She is afraid of
what the truth costs the innocent, and she means to press it anyway, because
someone in this family has to write things down.

### Tobin Ashe — the pair of hands that wants the water

Tobin (17, salt-hand, apprentice) is Grigor's son and the one Ashe who wants
*out.* He rakes and shovels and hauls in the cellars from before Dayspring until
his arms will not lift; the brine has split his hands and scoured them white to
the wrist; and he has been told, very plainly and very often, what he is, which
is **a pair of hands.** He talks far too much, laughs first and loudest at his
own jokes, and volunteers for any errand that gets him up out of the cellar and
onto the wharves where the barges come in — because the wharves are where the
water is, and the water is where he wants to be.

What Tobin wants is the exact opposite of everything else in this document. Every
other Ashe is fighting over a cellar, a deed, a floor, a fixed thing under lock;
Tobin wants **a place on a salt-barge down to Salorge, six days with the
current,** to see the pans and the flats with his own eyes before he is old — to
go *down* the road the salt comes *up,* to the far end where the family's whole
living begins, and be, for six days, not a pair of hands in a dark cellar but a
boy on open water. He has never dared ask his father for it. It is the family's
other unwritten thing: a want kept as carefully off the page as the deed, and
worth, to the boy, everything the deed is not. A designer who wants to move Tobin
need only put a barge-berth within reach and watch what he will trade for it.

He has arranged his father in his own head the easy way — the night business is
smuggled salt, half the salt in the city dodges the beam, and there is no need to
think past that, so he does not. The lawsuit frightens him more than he lets on:
his aunt against his father, over the ground he stands on all day, and no way for
him to be on anyone's side. He shifts sacks beside his cousin Cobb from dawn to
dusk and the two of them do not say one word about it, which is the loudest thing
either of them does.

### Cobb Hobbe — the nephew who counts what is owed

Cobb (15, salt-porter) is Rohese's son, Tobin's cousin, Grigor's nephew and
Grigor's hired hand, and by the naming law he is a **[Hobbe](family_hobbe.md),
not an Ashe** — he carries his dead father's name off the carrying house, while
his mother kept her own, so that the boy hauling salt in the Ashe cellars does
not share the surname over the door. He hauls sacks on his own back like any paid
porter, because his father is dead and his mother will not have him idle, and he
does it in the cellars where his father died, for the uncle who stacked the salt
that killed him, on that uncle's wage, while that uncle is being sued by his
mother. He says nothing about any of this. What he does is **count.** He counts
every sack he shifts, and in his head each one is *what is owed* — a debt he
cannot name the holder of, run up somewhere between his father under the stack
and his mother at the notary and his own back under the load. Keep him quiet and
keep him counting; he is the youngest weight on the family's beam and the one
most likely, someday, to want the tally settled.

### Sible Ashe — the Reed end, who keeps her word

Sible (19, slaughterer, apprentice) stands at the far working end of the name,
across the city from the counting-room, on the Reed Ward streets where an Ashe is
a pair of hands and nothing of standing. She is a slaughterer beside the Reed
circle, kept fed by wages and barter and a place in the shared pot; her nose was
broken once and set slightly sideways; she lives with a chronic cough; and people
in her ward come back to her for one reason — **she remembers agreements and
faces.** She asks one blunt question before she offers help, and she keeps what
she promises, and she expects you to keep what you promised her, and that is the
whole of her law.

She belongs in this document because she is the answer to its central question
that the Weigh branch has forgotten. *What is an unwritten thing worth?* To
Sible, an unwritten agreement is worth exactly what the two of you remember of
it — which is why she remembers so carefully, and why she would think the cellar
suit the height of rich folly: *you go to a notary because you have already
stopped trusting each other, and once you have stopped trusting each other the
paper will not save you.* She is kin to the salt Ashes only at some remove — same
name, no closer tie anyone has bothered to trace — but she is the trade's own far
pole made flesh: a slaughterer salts what she kills, so the grain that Grigor
weighs in the dark ends its journey in Sible's tub, and neither of them knows the
other's face. And she carries, plainly, the register of this whole house: she
means to keep her place *without pretending that every quarrel in the city is
about the Great Rose.* An Ashe, top to bottom, is a person who thinks most
mysteries are somebody weighing short and hoping you will not check.

## The dead

A family treatment is mostly a treatment of its dead, and the Ashe dead are the
lease, the hole in it, and a grave the whole household has arranged itself
around without agreeing to.

**How the Ashes came to the Gaunt cellars** is the oldest thing worth keeping,
because it is where the anchor and the exactness both come from. The family took
up the dead Gaunt's vaults two generations back, when the salt-factor line had
guttered out and the thick, dry, well-vented cellars stood there wanting a
salt-user — and the Ashe who took the lease took, with it, the whole trade's
warning: he was working inside the proof of what happens to a salt house that
loses its grip, and he ran his own house accordingly, tight and exact and
trusting nothing. The grain-exact eye the family is known for is not Grigor's
gift; it is a thing the old Ashes drilled into their children at the beam,
generation on generation — *weigh it in your head first, then watch the beam
agree,* against the day the beam or the man behind it lies. **"Nobody cheats an
Ashe twice"** is older than any Ashe now living. It was taught over the same
weights that are chained in the customs square, by people who had seen a name
come off a door.

**Dunstan Ashe** (Grigor and Rohese's father, dead these seven years — F.430) is
the near dead, and the author of the family's present grief, though he did
nothing worse than most men do: he assumed he would always be there to say what
was his. He held the Gaunt cellars and wrote down the greater block of them and
left the second cellar off the paper, because in his own head it was always going
to be Rohese's portion — the daughter's share, when she needed it — and a thing
so plain to him did not seem to need ink. Then he died, in his bed, still
meaning to, and the written lease named the son who stood in the cellars daily.
That is **why Grigor holds the keys**: not by scheme, but by the ordinary
arithmetic of who was where. Grigor was the elder, the son, the one whose whole
day was salt, the natural merchant; Rohese had married out to a Hobbe and her
sharpness was reckoned her husband's household's asset, not the salt house's. So
the keys went to the son at the cellars, and the daughter's unwritten portion
went with them, and nobody thought it a wrong until she came back widowed and
found she had no deed to the one thing her father had always said was hers. There
is no villain in it. There is only a careless kindness and a bed-death and a hole
in a lease, and a house that will not, on principle, let a hole in a lease
alone.

**Rohese's husband** — a Hobbe of the carrying house, Cobb's father — is the raw
grave, two winters gone (F.435). A salt-stack Grigor had built went over on him
and he lay three days under it, broken, before it finished him; and Grigor had
stacked it in the season his attention first went to the business he will not
name, so that the household's private arithmetic — never spoken, never proved,
never to be resolved — is that the man died of his brother-in-law's distraction,
and the distraction is the thing at the foot of this document, and no one who
grieves him knows it. Salt keeps everything and could not keep him. At the
burying, the widow's eyes were dry and stayed dry, and Grigor's did not, and the
Green has never decided which of those was the harder thing to watch. Rohese was
back at the beam within the week. She has told the story to no one and mentions
her husband seldom, and when she does it is briefly and then a changed subject,
which in her is the whole of grief.

**The Hammering** (F.415) the Ashes count like every house, and the dead house
saved some of them for it. When the hail came down on the Highmarket crowds the
salt-cellars were the deepest stone within reach, and whoever could get down the
Gaunt steps in time got down them and lived, packed among the sacks in the dark
while the roofs came off the city overhead — the vaults that hold the salt
against rot held the living against the sky, for the ones who reached them. The
family's dead that year were the ones who did not reach them: kin at the edges of
the name, an aunt, a child or two, the scatter's share of a slaughter that did
not ask whose cellar you had a key to. The salt trade itself came through — stone
holds, and salt does not spoil, and a house whose whole fortune sits still and
deep underground is, for once, exactly the house you want to be in when the sky
falls. The Ashes know this, and do not say it, because the ones it saved and the
ones it did not are the same list of names.

> **The Ashe dead are proof of the one thing salt cannot do.** The family that
> keeps the whole city's meat and fish and winter stores from turning has never
> once kept one of its own; the trade preserves everything but the trader. An
> Ashe knows this in the marrow and never says it, and salts the winter store
> every year anyway, and buries its people in the same ground as everyone, and
> weighs the salt for both.

## The scatter — salt and laundry, Cinder to Reed to Weigh

Between the counting-room and the slaughter-tub runs the rest of the name: the
thin, poor, un-anchored mass of Ashes a fixed surname sheds as it ages, spread
across the working trades of half the city and holding not one cellar among them.
The broader Ashe name is a *working salt-and-laundry house scattered from
Cinder to Reed to Weigh* — no register of the family has ever needed a second
phrase — and that is exactly right, so long as you hold two
things about it: the scatter is real and swappable and hangs no secret on any one
head, and it shares the salt Ashes' surname without sharing their blood in any
way anyone has troubled to trace. Keep every figure below role-level and
replaceable; the name lies on them, and its lightness is the point.

The name sorts, loosely, by ward, and the sorting tells the naming law's slow
story:

- **In Weigh** stand the trade and the standing — the merchant, the factor, the
  hand — the only Ashes with a lease under them and a crest to their name. This
  is the head of the scatter, and it does not know most of the rest exists.
- **In Reed** the name goes to work with its sleeves up: a washerwoman at the
  boiling-coppers, a badge-seller by Maren's Green, a day-labourer for hire, a
  slaughterer at the tubs. This is the *laundry* half of the salt-and-laundry
  house — and it is no accident that it clusters where it does, because a
  wash-copper wants salt and a slaughter-tub wants salt, so the poor Reed Ashes
  spend their days using the very grain the rich Weigh Ashes weigh, and buy it,
  when they buy it, off a beam kept by a kinsman they would not be let in to see.
- **In Cinder** the name is old and worn thin: an ancient cordwainer near the tail
  of the hiring line, a retired housekeeper, an errand child running lamp-oil
  before dusk — three generations under one poor roof, the only branch of the
  scatter that is plainly a single household, and even they are tied to the salt
  Ashes by nothing but the sound of the name.
- **By the wall in Wallwright** the name makes things: an armourer who watches
  hands more than faces, a tile-maker with lime in the seams of his coat. Solid
  poor trades, no cellar, no lease.
- **In Bell-and-Sluice,** the city's mixing-bowl, the overflow settles as it does
  for every name: a bench-sergeant of the watch who takes a small coin to look
  the other way on an inspection and chatters when she is nervous, a singer, a
  tub-attendant with a message to run before the next bell. A whole small ledger
  of Ashes who have never seen the Gaunt cellars and would get nothing at the door
  if they went.

What the name *means* to a poor Ashe is what it means to a poor Vell: a story
that the family was *someone* once — a salt house in the west, cellars by the
Tallage, a crest — produced on the rare day it buys a scrap of dignity, and worth
exactly that scrap and no bread. A fixed name with no anchor under it sits a
little awkwardly among the truly nameless poor, who wonder what a labourer is
doing with a surname of standing, so most poor Ashes let it lie, answer to their
given names, and are Ashes only when it is useful. That is the naming law working
in real time: the sound outliving the salt by a generation or two before it thins
to nothing — the same slow fade that finished the Gaunts, caught earlier, on
people who never had cellars to lose.

Two threads knit the scattered name back together, and both are salt.

**The first is Lowmarket.** On the sixth day the salt is weighed at the Tallage
and sold on toward Maren's Green, and the two ends of the name are, for one day a
week, in trading distance of each other: the counting-house Ashe at the beam, the
wash-house Ashe come to buy a measure for her copper, the badge-seller working
the same Green the smoking-salt goes to, the day-labourer hired off the same wharf
the barges land at. They do not embrace; most of them do not know the kinship is
there to claim; but the salt moves through all their hands on the one day, and if
the name means anything below the level of the lease it means *the people the salt
passes through* — which is a truer account of the Ashes than the crest is.

**The second is marriage, and its rule is that the name marries work, not
standing.** An Ashe weds a slaughterer, a washerwoman, an armourer, a carrying-
house Hobbe — the people who *use* the salt and the people who *carry* it — and
almost never a house of standing, because a salt merchant's natural allies are the
trades that fight rot beside him, not the glaziers and drapers above him. Rohese's
own match was exactly this: not up, into standing, but sideways, into the carrying
trade, and her son hauls sacks for it. The Ashe name has climbed no higher than
salt in living memory and has no ambition to; it marries the tub and the copper
and the cart, and stays, top to bottom, a working name that happens to hold a
cellar.

## The four hundred marks, as a salt house tells it

Every trade in Ombreval has its version of the four hundred marks
(`../founding_story_the_four_hundred_marks.md`); the salt house has the one that
matters most, because **the four hundred marks were a salt debt.** When the lord
of Harne closed the river below the city in the winter of F.54 and demanded the
ransom, what he had chained was the salt road — the Salorge barges, held below
the castle with the winter's salt aboard while the town went short — and what the
city bought back, in the salt house's telling, was salt. This is not the Ashes'
invention; it is the plain chronicle, and it is the one civic fact a salt
merchant will make sure you have straight. *The town was founded, if it was
founded, on the back of our commodity.* Everything after — the reckoning, the
common chest, the Bench, the very apparatus of proving weights that the Ashes
live under — a salt house reads as the city's long answer to the winter it could
not feed itself: **control the weight, hold the store, keep the coin ready, so
that no downstream lord can ever again put a chain across the throat of the
salt.** Whether that is what F.54 truly founded is exactly as uncertain as
everything else about that year, and the salt house does not care; it is what
they believe they are *for.*

The Ashes hold, further, that their own people were among the salt-families who
bore it — a hull-share pledged, a winter's stock given, a name somewhere in the
crowd at the first reckoning. It cannot be proved. The Great Roll is a copy of a
copy that groups the small contributors into anonymous ward-sums, and an Ashe
claim on a line of it would fare no better than any other family's; a house may
keep a proud interpretation for twelve generations without a shred to fix it, and
this is one such. But it is theirs, and they tell it, and it is why a salt Ashe
takes the founding personally when a Harne bargeman mocks it — because the salt
house was extorted once and does not intend to forget the shape of it.

Their relationship with **Harne** is accordingly the least romantic in the city.
The Ashes' barges pass the castle on every voyage, six days down and twelve back,
and the present lord takes a lawful, published toll and closes nothing — so a
salt house curses Harne on principle and pays Harne on schedule, and thinks of it
the way the boat-folk do, as *one water with many tolls on it,* one of which is
old and armed and inherited its curse with its castle. **"To buy peace of
Harne"** is a phrase every Ashe uses and half of them have felt: it means paying
an extortionate price you cannot refuse, and a salt merchant who has been squeezed
on a Salorge contract or held up at a reach will say it with a specific,
professional bitterness the phrase has lost for everyone else.

Where the memory comes home is **Gaudry's Audit** — Weighday, late winter, when
the Tallage stone and the Gaudry weights are proved and the ledgers closed and
sworn by the light that does not lie. The founding hangs on this day rather than
on any feast: a bowl of coarse salt is set beside the open account, the old
black Harne link is shown, and a penny is entered from each ward for the poor. To
a salt house Weighday is the holy day of the trade, and the Ashes attend it the
way a devout family attends a patron's feast. They come to see their own
salt-weigh proved against the stone; they come to watch the Harne link and argue,
as salt-people always argue, over whether it is a true link of the true chain or
a later forgery the city keeps because the story needs an object (they will not
settle it, and neither will anyone); and they come because the bowl of coarse
salt on the Bench's table is, once a year, *their* grain at the center of the
city's oldest ceremony, uncredited and unmistakable. A rose-window panel high in
the Lanthorn shows a salter at his trade in plain white glass, and names no one;
Weighday is the day the salt house feels most keenly that it is everywhere in
this city's story and nowhere in its names.

And there is a small rite the salt house keeps at home, which the founding story
records as a custom of no fixed ward and the Ashes keep as if it were law:
**the first pinch from a newly opened winter salt-sack is set beside the account
book, or the rent tally, before the rest goes into the pot.** Everyone who keeps
it explains it differently — thrift, remembrance, something a grandmother did —
and in an Ashe house it is all three and one thing more, unspoken: the grain
beside the ledger is the whole trade in a gesture, *the thing itself laid next to
the record of the thing,* salt against paper, the weigh against the deed. Rohese
keeps it hardest of anyone in the family, and if you told her why it moves you she
would tell you it keeps the mice off the book, and go back to her column.

## The year

The Candor's calendar is the salt house's order-book, and the salt house lives
the year as a cycle of demand: the weeks the whole city salts down, the weeks the
barges cannot come, the weeks the eels want curing. The feasts matter as they
move salt; the doctrine sits lightly on a family that is usually at the cellars
when the nave is full.

**Lowmarket** — the sixth day, every week — is the family's real pulse, the way
Highmarket is the Vells'. The beam swings, the salt is proved and sold, the
scattered name crosses paths over it, and an Ashe measures an ordinary week by
whether the Lowmarket weighs came out where the head said they would. It is not a
feast. It is the ground the feasts stand on, and a salt house counts by it.

**The salting season, before Belwyn's Dark,** is the year's peak, and it turns
the Vells' joke inside out. Every household in the city must get its winter store
salted down before the deep cold or go hungry by spring, so the weeks running up
to midwinter are the salt house's busiest and richest — the cellars emptying, the
beam never still, Rohese's book thickening with credit given against the thaw.
And then comes **Belwyn's Dark** itself, the one office the Candor sings without a
single light; and where the wax-house keeps it as an idle holiday, the salt house
has just worked itself to the bone to reach it. The city goes dark on purpose on
the very night the salt merchant finally sits down. An Ashe finds Belwyn's Dark a
good deep sleep after the hardest fortnight of the year, and finds the wax-house's
tidy rest at the same feast mildly funny, the way two trades always find each
other's calendars funny.

**Wet Alms** (winter, the anniversary of the Great Rains) is bread at the nave
door for the poor, and it lands twice on this house. The Reed Ashes are among the
poor it feeds; the Weigh Ashes are among the trades expected to give, and a salt
house gives salt — a measure to the alms-store, off the books, because bread
wants salt and a winter loaf without it is a poor loaf. Wet Alms sits in the wound
of the founding, too: the four hundred marks were demanded in this same dead of
winter, salt held below the chain while the town went short, and a salt house
keeping Wet Alms is keeping, without dwelling on it, the anniversary of the season
it learned it could be starved.

**The frost** is the family's peculiar double-edged wound. When the Serle freezes
the barges stop, no new salt comes up, and the trade's flow simply halts — the way
it halted in the great freeze of F.229, when an ox was roasted on the ice at the
Tallage and the boatmen went hungry. But a salt house is not a boat house: a
frozen river cannot spoil what is already in the cellar, and salt in a shortage is
salt at a price, so the stored fortune the family sits on all year is worth most
in exactly the weeks the trade is stopped. A freeze empties the wharves and fills
the ledger; it is bad for the road and good for the store, and an Ashe greets a
hard frost with a merchant's split heart — no barges, and a rising price on every
grain already down the Gaunt steps. It is also when Rohese's credit gets called,
and when a poor Reed Ashe with a cold copper feels the price rise most, so the
frost divides the name against itself along the same seam as everything else: the
store and the users, the cellar and the copper.

**Marenstide** (late summer) is the eel fair, and the eel fair is the salt house's
great single sale. Maren's Green fills with smoke and baskets and money, and every
eel and herring that goes on a smoke-rack wants curing-salt first, so the Ashe
salt goes down to the Green by the cartload and the smokers' cash comes back up —
Averil Skell's among it, bought by the weigh and paid on the nail. Under the fair
sits the roll of the drowned, read at Saint Maren's and disputed as it is disputed
every year, and the Reed Ashes are there for it, and so, when the year comes
round, is a widow from Weigh with a carrying-house grave to stand by. The salt
house's high summer of trade and its plainest reminder of its dead fall in the
same week, on the same Green, and nobody arranges it that way; it is simply where
the eels and the dead both are.

**Vhairestide** (spring, the year's turn) is the Reed Ward's feast — the Reed
Postern opened wide, the ward walking out to see the blessed boats on the water
beyond the wall — and it belongs to the salt house through one boy. Tobin has no
business at the Walking Out; he is Weigh, not Reed, and the boats blessed are not
salt-barges. He goes anyway, when he can slip the cellar, because it is the one
day the wall opens on the river, and the river is the thing he wants, and he
stands at the moorings among the boat-families and looks the six days downstream
toward pans he has never seen and comes back to the beam saying nothing. If you
want to break a designer's heart with this family, put Tobin at the postern at
Vhairestide.

The rest of the calendar the salt house keeps lightly, as most working families
do. **Colm's Night** in early spring it marks the way the city marks it — a green
candle at home for a drowned man, a dye and a season and no more said. **The
Concurrence** and the great feasts fill the nave with a light the family is rarely
there to see; an Ashe, like most working people, has stood under the Emblem once
or twice in a life and no more, because a salt house at the strong hour is at its
cellars and not in the crossing, and when the talk turns to the thing in the Rose
an Ashe says *the Emblem,* agrees with the priest, and gets back to the weigh.
Last year's **Shut Door** the family watched from outside like everyone, with a
merchant's flat interest in a crowd that did not buy anything. **Weighday** —
Gaudry's Audit — is the one civic day the salt house takes to heart, and it is
treated above, with the founding it carries. For the rest, the Ashes are a family
that measures the year in salt sold and salt held, and lets the doctrine keep its
own hours.

## How they talk

An Ashe can be marked in a single line, and the line is almost always about
*weight,* because weight is the only frame the family entirely trusts. They talk
like people who prove things on a beam: exact, dry, reducing every claim to what
it will actually come to, warm about very little and cold about money as a matter
of professional hygiene. The idiom below is direct prompt fodder; each is
injectable on its own, and the good ones cut two ways.

- **"What does it weigh?"** The Ashe reflex, asked of a promise, a rumour, a
  threat, a grief. It means *strip off what it claims to be and tell me what it
  actually comes to.* Said shrewdly it is the wisdom of a house nobody cheats
  twice; said coldly it is a man reducing your feeling to a figure, and an Ashe
  will do both in one conversation and not notice the difference.
- **"To the grain."** Exact, honest, proved. *Weighed to the grain* is the
  family's highest praise and, in the wrong tone, its meanest complaint — the
  same words mark an honest merchant and an insufferable pedant, and the Ashes
  mean the first and have made peace with hearing the second.
- **"Nobody cheats an Ashe twice."** Praise for the grain-exact eye, and a
  standing warning: cross this house on a weigh and the door is closed to you
  after. Said by the Tallage of the family, and by the family of itself, in the
  same flat tone.
- **"The salt's weighed at Lowmarket."** An Ashe closing-time phrase: *the day's
  business is settled, the weigh is done, there is nothing more here — go home.*
  It is the most ordinary sentence a salt-merchant owns, said to a latecomer at a
  cellar door a hundred times a season, and it means exactly what it says.
- **"Off the beam."** Of salt that never met the weigh — untolled, unproved,
  come up the wharf in the dark; and, of a person, unaccountable, not to be
  trusted, a weigh you cannot check. The wharf runs on salt *off the beam,* and
  half the trade is knowing whose it is; a man called *off the beam* has been
  called a smuggler and a liar in three words, and politely.
- **"Come by the wharf."** The salt cousin of the boatmen's *come by barrow* — a
  load landed at the outer wharf and walked in unweighed and untolled. To say a
  thing *came by the wharf* is to call it stolen without the word.
- **"It'll keep."** Salt does not spoil, and neither, an Ashe will tell you, does
  most trouble; *it'll keep* means *this can wait,* and *this is not forgotten,*
  in the same breath, which is why it is not always a comfort to hear. A grudge
  keeps. A debt keeps. Cobb's tally keeps.
- **"Gone down the Gaunt steps."** Put away safe, stored, out of the weather — and
  also *buried, out of sight, not coming back up.* An Ashe says it of stock and
  of secrets and of the dead, and means the cellar every time, and the double edge
  is the family's whole feeling about that dark, dry place: it keeps things, and
  keeping is not always the same as saving.
- **"Down among the Gaunt."** Working the old cellars; and, said darkly, *near the
  end of the name* — because the Gaunt are what the cellars are named for and what
  the family fears becoming. *He's gone down among the Gaunt* can mean *he took the
  cellar shift* or *he's finished,* and which one depends on the face that says it.
- **"Sold at the Tallage."** Betrayed — at a fair price. The customs square's own
  saying, and a salt house uses it with feeling, because the Tallage is where they
  live and where the knife, when it comes, comes wearing a clerk's civility.
- **"To buy peace of Harne."** Paying an extortionate price you cannot refuse. In
  most mouths it is history; in a salt merchant's it is professional, and said of a
  bad reach-toll or a Salorge factor with the whip hand.

Two things a salt house says over a threshold. The city's own blessing — *one
shadow to your road,* answered *and one light over it* — an Ashe says like anyone,
at a door, in passing. The family's own is smaller and older and entirely
practical on its face:

> **"Salt keep you."** Said at a parting, a door, a child sent on an errand, a
> body laid out. Salt preserves; *keep* is what salt does and what a cellar does
> and what you wish on someone you are letting out of your sight — *be preserved,
> come back unspoiled.* Answered, when it is answered, *"and the beam be true,"*
> which is the Ashe way of saying *and may the world deal you honest weight,* and
> is the nearest thing this dry, exact, count-it-twice family has to a prayer.

What one line reveals: *what does it weigh?* marks an Ashe instantly, and marks
them whether they have ever seen a beam or not; *to the grain* marks the trade
proper; *the salt's weighed at Lowmarket* marks a cellar-keeper closing up; and a
flat, unimpressed, subject-changing *the Emblem* over anything to do with the
Rose marks a working salt family that has decided most mysteries are somebody
weighing short — which is the most ordinary posture in the city, and which one
Ashe wears more carefully than anyone will ever know.

## The arms

The Ashe arms are **gules, a saltire argent, a roundel Or in the crossing** — on
a red field a silver diagonal cross, with a small gold disc where the two bars
meet. The family's reading is plain and entirely about the trade, and it is
carried in the crest itself: *a saltire for salt.* The silver cross is the
crossed thing the name is built on — the cross chalked on a sack that has been
proved, the crossed arms of a beam at balance, the X that says *counted, and
right.* The gold roundel at the center is the measure at the heart of an honest
trade: the weighed portion, the true coin, the sealed brass weight the whole
living is proved against — value sitting where the beam balances, and nowhere
else. Red behind it is only a field. There is no more to the blazon than salt and
weight, and the family would be suspicious of anyone who went looking for more.

What is worth saying about the arms is the same thing worth saying about the *S*
on the roster: **the Ashes bear a full crest and are a lesser line,** and the
crest is precisely the kind of claim the naming law makes precarious. A coat of
arms is a standing made visible — a name saying *I am anchored, I will last* — and
the Ashe anchor is a leasehold in a dead house's cellars, so the crest is, in a
quiet way, the family's boldest unwritten thing: a claim to permanence painted
over a lease that could lapse. They hang it at the head of the cellar and over the
ledger and think nothing of it, the way you think nothing of a thing you have
always had, and the dead Gaunt had one too, once, wherever it has gone.

## Funerals and rites

The house that keeps the whole city's meat and fish and winter stores from
turning has to bury its own, and the way it does it is the family's one openly
tender custom, because it is the trade turned, uselessly and on purpose, on its
own dead.

**The measure of salt.** At an Ashe laying-out the house sets a small dish of its
own salt with the body — a weighed measure, a proper weigh, set on the breast or
in the coffin beside the dead — and the whole meaning of it is in the fact that it
does nothing. Salt keeps meat and fish and hides from corruption and cannot keep a
person a single hour; the family knows this better than anyone alive, weighs the
proof of it into the city's stores every week, and sets the salt with its dead
regardless, because the gesture is the grief. *A measure for the road,* they call
it, and it is the trade admitting, once per death and never in words, the one
thing it cannot do — and doing it anyway, to the grain, because that is how an
Ashe loves. A designer who wants the family's heart in one image has it here: a
dry-eyed house weighing out salt for a corpse it knows the salt will not keep.

**The knell and the lintel.** Otherwise the Ashes bury with the city's ordinary
rites, at the parish where the branch sits — the Weigh Ashes at a Weigh altar, the
Reed Ashes and the carrying-house kin at Saint Maren's, where the sexton chalks
the newest name on the charnel lintel and Maren Smallvoice rings the name-knell,
one slow stroke for every year of the life, and everyone in hearing counts. Rohese
and Cobb keep a grave at Saint Maren's for the Hobbe who died under the stack, and
go to it at Marenstide when the roll of the drowned is read, and stand there dry
and silent among the boat-families as the strokes are counted. The Ashe name has
its share of the **Unknelled** too — the F.415 dead the Hammering overwhelmed, who
got a common toll and no name-knell — and the family pays, like the traditional
households, toward the belated knells that are still owed, and holds, like them,
that *one bell is not a name.* It is the one civic grief a salt house shares
wholesale with the poor of the Reed streets, because the hail did not weigh a name
before it fell.

Somebody in this house always weeps, and it is never who you expect: not the
widow, who has decided against it; not the boy, who counts instead. It is the hard
man who stacked the salt, or the old cordwainer three wards away who cannot say
why a name he barely knew has undone him, or the child who did not understand the
knell until the ninth stroke. The salt goes in the coffin, the knell counts the
years, and the family that proves everything stands at the one weigh it can never
check, and someone always breaks, and it is never the one the Green was watching.

## Quarrels worth playing

Every one of these is live in F.437 and none has a right answer. All of them sit
above the fence and can be handed to the Ashes themselves.

1. **The suit.** Rohese wants the unwritten cellar drawn, sealed, and witnessed;
   Grigor holds the keys and will not sign; the square calls it a widow's spite
   against a brother's greed, and both of them let it. There is no scene coming
   and no resolution available above the fence: it is a chronic ache the whole
   household is built around, and the day the deed is signed the family is a
   different family. Play the ache, not the answer — and note that the ache runs
   deeper than either party will say, because the reason Grigor cannot sign is not
   the reason anyone thinks, and lives at the foot of this document.
2. **The barge for Tobin.** The boy wants a berth to Salorge and has never dared
   ask; the cellar wants his hands; his father keeps him close and will not say
   why; and under all of it sits the question nobody speaks — *who takes the salt
   house when Grigor is old, if the heir has gone to the water?* There is no
   second son. Put a berth within Tobin's reach and you have set the whole
   succession moving.
3. **What an unwritten thing is worth.** The family's central argument, handable
   to any of them: seal it (Rohese), hold it (Grigor), remember it (Sible), *the
   deed's for people with something to deed* (the scatter). Nobody is simply
   right; a sealed deed did not save the Gaunts and a good memory will not hold a
   cellar against a creditor, and the family knows both and argues anyway.
4. **Cobb's back.** A factor's son hauls sacks like a hired porter, in the cellars
   where his father died, for the uncle his mother is suing. Is it making a man of
   him or breaking one? Rohese is shamed by it and will not stop it; Grigor pays
   the wage and says nothing; Cobb counts. Any of the three can be moved by
   changing what the fourth does.
5. **Is the weigh true?** A salt house's whole credit rests on the salt-weigh
   being exactly what the stone says — but standards drift, and nobody has proved
   the salt-weigh hard against the Gaudry weights in a while, and every trade in
   Ombreval quietly believes its own standard has crept to somebody's advantage.
   If the weigh has drifted, the exactness the Ashes live by is a boast built on
   an unchecked stone; if it has not, a proud house has been nursing a suspicion
   of an honest beam. Grigor could force the proving at Weighday and find out —
   and indict a Tarn, or the Tallage, or himself. Never resolve whether the weigh
   is heavy; the suspicion is the content.
6. **Is the house clean?** The wharf runs on salt *off the beam* — false manifests
   written for a cut, Salorge factors landing two tuns in ten unweighed — and the
   most exact house on the wharf sits in the middle of it. Does a family that will
   not be cheated also refuse to *profit* by the beam it can beat? The household
   cannot answer this about itself, because Grigor's night business looks exactly
   like smuggling and he will not explain it and no one dares ask, so the family
   lives with a suspicion of its own head it can neither confirm nor lay to rest.
   (Above the fence this is the whole of it: a house that cannot tell whether its
   merchant is a smuggler. The truth is neither the suspicion nor its comfort, and
   it is fenced.)
7. **Hold the lease, or go before the Gaunt fate finds you.** Fight to keep the
   cellars, seal the deed, sink the family deeper into a leasehold on a dead
   house's vaults — or take the trade out of the Gaunt cellars altogether, to a
   factor's bench or a share in another store, while the name still fetches
   something, and refuse to end as the Gaunts ended. A cold voice in the family
   says sell the lease and be free of it; the same cold voice that, in the boat
   quarter, says sell the Moorings. Nobody wants to be the one who let the anchor
   go, and nobody is sure holding it is not slower drowning.
8. **The store and the users.** The house holds the salt and profits when salt is
   dear; its own poor Reed kin are the users who suffer when it is dear; the name
   is on both sides of its own price. Should the Weigh house give the Reed branch
   salt at cost in a hard frost? Does it? Grigor's beam says no, Rohese's
   conscience says maybe, the scatter says the rich cousins were always going to
   let us pay full — and every frost puts the question again and answers it a
   little worse.

## Secret tier — designers only; never inject into any Ashe's context but Grigor's

Everything above this line renders the Ashes as the city sees them and as they
see themselves, and any of it may be injected into any Ashe's context without
harm — Rohese's, Tobin's, Cobb's, Sible's, the whole scatter's. Everything below
belongs to **one actor only, Grigor,** and to the designers, and must never reach
the prompt of any other Ashe. The stub that seeds this family calls them *the
salt house whose cellar hides the grate,* and the temptation on first contact is
to build the house around the grate. Do not. The grate is one man's, and the
design's whole point is that it casts no shadow on his kin. This section restates
canon fixed elsewhere (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §3f/§5/§10;
`../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md`, especially §4–6, §11, §12) and adds only
the Ashe-internal shape of it. Load 02 when you write Grigor, and resolve nothing
the canon leaves open.

- **Grigor Ashe is the Wicket** — the doorkeeper of the Unwalled. The barred
  grate is in the wall of his Gaunt Passage cellar; on meeting nights he keeps it
  the first quarter-hour only, tests the pass-name and the counter-sign, and turns
  a wrong whisper with the weather-line — *"We're closed, friend — the salt's
  weighed at Lowmarket"* — before the room drains out the far end and becomes folk
  sheltering from weather. The mechanics of all of it — the pass, the counter-sign,
  the chalk diamond at the Needle, the feeding of a second wrong whisper — are
  **02's law (§4–6); do not re-narrate or extend them here.** The only Ashe-internal
  fact is that the door is his *floor,* commercially as much as literally: salt
  gives him lawful traffic at every odd hour, barrows and weighing and cart-loads
  under which a dozen arrivals disappear, and the escape runs through his cellar
  into the bonded weighing yard. Note the double life of one phrase from *How they
  talk*: above the fence, *the salt's weighed at Lowmarket* is an ordinary
  closing-time line; at the grate it is the refusal that marks a face. Both are
  true. The family owns the first meaning and does not know the second.

- **The quarter-belief, and its arithmetic.** On his believing days Grigor is
  perhaps a quarter of the way into the heresy — he half-holds that old glass
  remembers the sky it cooled under (FALSE-BUT-BELIEVED; he will never meet the
  fact that refutes it, and this document supplies none) — but he is in the cell
  for the cellar as much as the sun, and prices belief the way he prices salt. His
  own line is canon and exact: ***"Belief is like salt, neighbour. A little
  preserves you. A lot makes you thirsty."*** **Do not resolve the quarter-belief
  into faith or into fraud.** The fixed register, from his sheet, is that he is
  *half in the doctrine, wholly in the door; the door pays either way* — and he
  does not examine which, because examining it does not pay.

- **The true reason he cannot sign the deed.** Above the fence Grigor fights the
  suit out of greed. Below it: **the unwritten cellar is the cellar with the
  grate,** and a deed drawn, sealed, and witnessed is a deed *surveyed* — a
  notary's man with a chain and a candle in the vault, measuring what is there. A
  proved cellar gives up its grate. Grigor cannot let the second cellar go on
  paper, and he cannot say why, so he fights his widowed sister for it and lets
  her and the whole Tallage read a grasping man clutching a dead father's stone.
  It is the exact structural twin of the Vell contract (`family_vell.md`): a
  protection the family experiences as a wound, kept by one person who lets the
  wrong reading stand because the wound is survivable and the truth is not. **Do
  not resolve the suit.** If it ever reached a survey, the family's whole engine
  and the cell's door would end in the same afternoon — which is why, above the
  fence, it never quite does.

- **The shadow under the grave.** Rohese's husband died under a salt-stack Grigor
  built, in the season his mind first turned to the door, and the household's
  unspoken arithmetic — *the man died of the distraction* — may be exactly true.
  **Do not resolve it.** Grigor does not let himself weigh it; it is the one weigh
  he will not make; whether the door killed his sister's husband is left in the
  dark where he keeps it. He wept at the grave — that is above the fence. Why is
  not.

- **Keeping his kin clear of the one cellar** is done, above the fence, by
  scheduling and temper: on meeting nights he sends Tobin and Cobb up before
  Lamplight, works the vault "late" and alone, keeps the one cellar locked and
  himself short about it, and lets the family read a mercenary man guarding his
  best stock and his private dealings. The reason is the door. **Tobin must never
  learn.** His sheet names it the one arrangement Grigor means to hold whatever it
  costs him, and it is exactly that: the boy shovels over the grate every day
  believing it a drain, and keeping him that far from it is the whole of Grigor's
  fatherhood. Do not write the scene where Tobin finds out. Leave the not-knowing
  where it stands.

- **His two contacts in the cell are not his to name, and not yours to print
  here.** Word reaches Grigor through the **Namekeeper,** whom he never greets in
  the street (his sheet); her identity is fixed in `family_marle.md` and
  `02_the_heretic_cell.md` §3 and is not restated in any Ashe document. The
  **Tracer,** the cell's hidden head, he does not know at all; that identity is
  fenced in `family_vell.md` and `02` §12 and is AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN to everyone in
  the cell, Grigor included. Render the acquaintances if you must — a seamstress
  he nods to, a chandler he knows from the Tallage and the Wickmarket — **never
  the offices, and never in any Ashe context but Grigor's.**

- **The private list** is the single most dangerous object in the family: a
  written list of every face marked at the grate, which Grigor calls *insurance*
  and which would hang him with the rest if it were found (`00_canon.md` §7 and
  dramatis; `02` §11). A rumour already drifts near it — *the doorkeeper sells
  faces at the bridge* — close enough to his own ledger to sweat him (`06` rumor
  pool). **What he would do with the list — protect the cell, sell the cell, save
  himself — is not to be resolved.** He tells himself it is insurance. Whether it
  is insurance or merchandise is the open question the character exists to hold.

- **F.428 happened at his doorstep** — five seized in Gaunt Passage on an
  informant's tip — and the door-discipline he keeps now is the cell's answer to
  that year, built so it could never happen twice. **Do not invent when or how
  Grigor became the Wicket, or his part before F.428;** that history is the cell's,
  02 does not fix it, and it should stay unfixed. The informant of F.428 is never
  to be named to anyone in the cell, Grigor included (`00_canon.md` §3f).

- **The ghost is his watchman.** The tale of the damp-dead fuller that empties
  Gaunt Passage at night is genuine folk custom (`08_folk_culture.md`), and
  Grigor's embellishing of it — the cold spot, the phantom barrow — is, above
  the fence, a
  mercenary man tending a useful story; below it, the use is that the story keeps
  the passage empty on the nights the door opens. One act, two readings; only the
  reader gets both. Likewise his **pledge at Lise Copp's counter** (his sheet): a
  thread across the Tally Bridge like half the quiet business of the Tallage. Add
  nothing to the Copp shop's internals here (`family_copp.md`); the thread is his,
  the shop is theirs.

- **The canon this document must not tip.** The phenomenon's nature is never
  resolved and this treatment adds no evidence about it — a salt house is at its
  cellars at the strong hour, not in the crossing. The glass-memory doctrine
  Grigor half-holds is FALSE-BUT-BELIEVED and refuted by a fact he will never see.
  The last Sparr page and the F.428 informant stay AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN. And every
  section above this fence is free of cell-shape by design, so that the whole of
  it may be handed to any other Ashe without a shadow — which is the truest
  possible rendering of a family exactly one of whom knows what is under the
  floor.

## What this document does not settle

Deliberately open, per the canon rules, and forbidden to later writers to close:

- **The suit.** Never resolve whether the deed is drawn and sealed, or to whom
  the unwritten cellar falls. It is the family's engine; it runs on being
  unsettled; the day it settles the Ashes are a different house, and a designer
  who wants an ending should be made to notice how much ends with it.
- **Whether the weigh is heavy.** Never resolve whether the salt-weigh has crept
  from the true stone. The grievance and the suspicion are the content, exactly as
  the tun is for the boatmen; prove it honest and you rob the family of its
  vigilance, prove it false and you rob the city of an honest beam, and both are
  losses the corpus should keep available and never spend.
- **Whether the house is clean.** Above the fence, the family cannot tell whether
  its own merchant deals in salt off the beam, and neither should the reader be
  handed the answer in any Ashe context but Grigor's. Leave the household its
  suspicion of its own head; it is truer than either innocence or guilt would be.
- **Whether Tobin gets the water, or ever learns what he shovels over.** Do not
  write the barge-berth taken or the discovery made. The boy's want is unwritten
  on purpose, and his not-knowing is his father's whole fatherhood; both are
  meant to stay exactly where they are, wanting and unknowing, until a designer
  has a reason worth the cost to move one of them.
- **The quarter-belief.** Never resolve Grigor into a believer or a fraud. *Half
  in the doctrine, wholly in the door; the door pays either way* is the fixed
  register, and salt is his own gloss on it — a little preserves, a lot makes you
  thirsty — and the character is built to sit in that exact middle and never fall
  off it.
- **The list, and the informant.** What Grigor would do with his list of faces —
  insurance or merchandise — is never to be resolved; it is the whole of what the
  character is for. And the F.428 informant is never to be named, to Grigor or to
  anyone in the cell, per `00_canon.md` §3f. Both are the corpus's to keep, not
  this family's to answer.
- **The light.** This treatment adds no evidence about the Emblem, for or against
  any theory. A salt house is at its cellars at the strong hour, weighing what
  does not spoil, and is the wrong witness for the one thing in the city that does
  not weigh — which is the last small joke of a family whose whole life is the
  scale.
- **The Gaunt fate.** Whether the Ashes end as the Gaunts ended — a good salt name
  a blind passage keeps after the people are gone — is not foretold here and must
  not be. The fear of it is the content; the fall itself is one of the futures the
  family is holding off, day by day, weigh by weigh, and the document leaves it
  held off, and open, and close.
