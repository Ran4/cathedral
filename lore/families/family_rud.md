# The Ruds

> The cloth-poor of the fulling tubs and the tenter-yards, a surname with
> nothing under it, and the one sworn heretic the name happens to carry. This
> document is about a name in the losing — why "Rud" is still said at all when
> the naming law says it should be gone — and about anger as the family trade,
> most of which goes nowhere near the cell. The heresy is fenced to one man and
> belongs to the secret tier. Author-facing. Load with
> `../core_lore/naming_language.md` and `../core_lore/trade_and_daily_life.md`
> when writing any Rud, and `../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` and
> `family_mott.md` when writing Tam or his cousin. See also
> [overview](overview.md) and, for the dry Cut the Ruds work,
> [the boatmen](../the_dry_boatmen.md).

## The thing to get right first

**The Ruds are not a house.** This is the mistake the roster invites and every
prompt-builder makes on first contact: seventeen people carry the name across
four wards, so the eye assembles a family — a heartland, a trade held in common,
an old couple at the head of a table, a thing that could be sat down and asked
its opinion. There is no such thing to ask. Lay the seventeen out and what you
have is not a house but a **labour-roll**: two fullers, a dung carter, an
apprentice smith, a tile-maker, a cobbler, a wick-maker, a garment-cutter, a
furnace hand, a retired watchman, a militiaman, a file leader, two errand
children, a housemaid, a message-runner, a pilgrim boy — not one master among
them, not one workshop held in the name, not one freehold, not one bench, not
one pole over a door. The highest rank the whole name reaches in F.437 is
*journeyman*, and it reaches it four or five times, in four or five trades that
have nothing to do with one another except that they are poor.

So the fact to get right is not what the Ruds own or believe. It is that by the
city's own law they should not still be *called* anything at all:

> A fixed family name is held, not owned; it lasts only as long as the thing
> that anchored it — a boat, a yard, a warehouse, a bench of trade. Lose the
> anchor and nobody strips the name from the living; the street simply stops
> giving it to the children, and within a generation the line falls back to
> bynames of gear and place like anyone's. *There are more Hawsers than Alders.*

That is the rule the whole corpus turns on (`../core_lore/naming_language.md`;
the [Vells](family_vell.md) show it mid-fall and the
[boatmen](../the_dry_boatmen.md) show it landed). The Ruds anchor **nothing**.
There is no Rud boat, no Rud yard, no Rud bench; there is not, in living memory,
agreement about whether there ever was one. By the rule the name should have
fallen a generation or two ago, the way a hundred names fell after the
Hammering, and the children should be Tam *the fuller* and Aldith *of the
Sluice* and Petronel *Smith*, each a byname of their own trade, and "Rud" should
be a sound old people made.

It is not. It is still said, still written, still answered to by seventeen
people, and **why it is still said is the whole of this document.** Everything
below is one long answer to a single question the naming law does not expect to
be asked twice: *what keeps a name alive after the thing it named is gone?* The
Ruds are the corpus's worked example of the rule's last stage — not the fall,
which the Vells have, and not the landing, which the Hawsers have, but the
strange long **after**, a name outliving not only its property but the memory of
its property, kept in the mouth by pure use while everyone who says it has
forgotten what it was for.

Get this wrong and you write a dynasty — an old head, a family seat, a Rud
opinion — and throw away the only thing the name actually is, which is a handle
other people hold.

## The second thing to get right

The stub calls the Ruds *the angry cloth-poor; the raw timber of the Unwalled*,
and the second half of that phrase is a trap. It slides the eye toward the cell
and reads the whole name as a nest of radicals — a heretic family, anger turned
to heresy, seventeen Ruds who might any of them be Greensick. That is wrong the
way the Marle misreading is wrong (`family_marle.md`), and worse, because the
Ruds have no standing to make the coldness a performance: they are just poor.

The heresy is **one man.** Tam Rud is sworn; no other Rud is anything. And the
anger the stub names is real and general — it is the truest thing the name has,
a temper that runs down three generations like a fault in stone — but **Rud
anger is overwhelmingly ordinary, and it goes everywhere except the cell.** It
goes into the fulling stocks, where a day is beaten out of wet wool. It goes
into drink, at Renna Tapster's, two pots past what a man should say. It goes
into the militia file and the ward watch, where the city pays a Rud to be angry
at its enemies for it. It goes, in one bitter old man past seventy, into a
warehouse in the dark, where the family's temper has already done its one act of
sabotage for reasons the family does not ask about. Out of the whole angry name,
exactly one man's anger went to a green sun, and even his did not go there for
the sun.

> **Do not make the Ruds a coven and do not make the Breach competent.** The
> dread the design wants from this house is not a cell of firebrands. It is a
> tired man with a list, in a name full of people too poor and too spent to be
> dangerous to anyone but themselves. Render the anger as *labour* — the thing
> that gets beaten into cloth six days a week — and keep the one exception
> fenced where it belongs.

## The central question

The boatmen ask *do we keep the boat?* The Vells ask *which end of the name are
you on?* The Marles ask *what is Betriss, and what do we owe her?* The Ruds own
nothing to keep, sit at no single table, and cannot ask a family question,
because they are not a family. What they have instead is a question the naming
law forces on every one of them separately, and none of them can finally answer:

> **Is it worth being a Rud at all?**

The name secures no credit at the Tallage, no pew, no apprentice article, no
share in anything. It is worth nothing to *hold*. But it is worth something to
be *called*, because to be a Rud is to be **known** — a breed a fulling-master
will hire without asking twice, a face the ward remembers, a household the
neighbours can find in the dark when a child is sick. And being known is also
being *findable*: by the master's ell-tally, by the dunning man, by the ward
hand who docks your crew's day, by the militia muster, by the pauper-roll, and —
for one man — by a grey clerk's watch-list. The name is a handle, and a handle
can be gripped by a friend hauling you up or by a hand closing round your wrist.
Every Rud is somewhere on the question of whether that handle is worth carrying,
and the answers are livable, chronic, and made mostly with the feet:

- **Petronel** (18, apprentice smith) is the name's one climbing edge, and if
  she makes her articles the street will start to call her *Petronel Smith*, and
  the Rud will fall off her like scale off cooling iron. She is the answer that
  says the name is a chrysalis: you grow out of it if you are lucky, and being a
  Rud is a thing that happened to you before you had a trade of your own.
- **Aldith** (56, dung carter) has built an entire working life out of being
  known — *people come back to her because she remembers agreements and faces* —
  and for her the name is worth precisely its recognizability and not one spark
  more. She is the answer that says: the name is worth being known by, and I
  have made it pay.
- **Tam** (29, fuller) is past the question. His face is worth something to
  people he would rather it were not, and he stopped weighing the name the year
  the doors were barred. He is the answer that says the name does not matter;
  what was done to us does.
- **The ambient mass** holds no answer, because they have never had the leisure
  to choose. To the furnace hand and the errand child and the widow at the tubs,
  *Rud* is simply what the ward calls them, a fact like the weather, no more
  chosen than the cold. That is the aggregate answer, and it is the honest one:
  for most of the name, being a Rud is not a position. It is an address.

Do not resolve this. No Rud decides, on the page, that the name is worth keeping
or worth shedding; Petronel's articles are not finished, and the question is
remade every time a child is born poor into it and every time one of them stands
close enough to a trade to be renamed by it. The name is a slow fall the family
is living inside, and the living-inside is the house.

## What "Rud" fails to anchor

A name of standing is a lease on a thing (`../core_lore/naming_language.md`).
The [Alders](family_alder.md) lease theirs on the Moorings, the
[Vells](family_vell.md) on the wax-house and the Lanthorn contract, the
[Marles](family_marle.md) on a cloth bench and a mason's yard. Walk the Rud name
looking for the thing under it and you find, over and over, that there is
nothing there.

- **No workshop.** The fullers work another man's tubs. The smith learns at
  another forge. The tile-maker fires in a shared yard, the cobbler cobbles in a
  shared work-and-lodging group, the wick-maker dips in the shadow of the Vells.
  Nobody called Rud owns the place they work, the tools they work with, or the
  labour they sell; they own their hands, and rent everything the hands touch.
- **No bench, no pew, no article in the name.** No Rud takes an apprentice under
  Rud articles, because there is no Rud trade to article into. When a Rud child
  is bound, it is bound to a master of some other name, and comes up learning to
  be a smith or a cobbler, not to be a Rud.
- **No mastership.** Four or five journeymen; below them apprentices, labourers,
  children, and the two old soldiers on their pensions. The name has no
  mistress-of-the-trade to hold it up, no one whose word is good at the Tallage
  for a little more than a stranger's.

And here is the part the family cannot settle: **they do not agree what "Rud"
ever anchored.** Ask three old Ruds and you get three histories. One will tell
you there was a fulling-house on the Cut with its own tubs and its own
drying-ground, Rud tubs, lost in the Great Rains when the Cut broke and the low
workshops drowned (F.362), or lost to debt a generation after — she is not sure
which, and neither was her mother. One will tell you it was never a
fulling-house at all but a raddle trade, marking cloth and sheep with red ochre,
and that the name is the ochre — *rud*, the old word for the red — and this one
says it as though it settled something, and it settles nothing. And one, the
driest, will tell you there was never anything, that "Rud" is just a short hard
sound the ward hung on a pack of cloth-workers because you have to call people
something, and that looking for a lost freehold under it is a rich family's
habit that the poor cannot afford.

They are probably all a little right, and it does not matter, and *that* is the
deepest turn of the naming law in this house: a name of standing remembers its
anchor — an Alder can point to the ring the boat was tied to — but the Ruds have
forgotten theirs, if they had one. First the property goes; then, given long
enough, the memory of the property goes; and the name floats free of every fact
that ever fastened it, a pure sound worn smooth by use, meaning only *that
household, those hands, those poor streets.* The Ruds are what a name looks like
one stage past the Hawsers: not fallen, but *unmoored* — still afloat, and no
longer tied to anything at all.

## Why it is still said

If the name anchors nothing and even the family cannot say what it once was, the
naming law's own prediction is that the street should have dropped it. It has
not. The Ruds are the exception that shows the rule's hidden machinery, because
the thing that keeps "Rud" alive is not the family. It is **everyone else's
paperwork.**

A name of standing keeps itself: the house guards it because the house profits
by it. A fallen name dies because nobody has a use for it — a Hawser is nobody's
concern, and the street lets the byname take him. But "Rud" sits in a rare
middle: too poor to keep itself, too *useful to others* to be let go. The name
is written down, over and over, by people who are not Ruds and have every reason
to keep writing it:

- **The fulling-master's ell-tally.** A master who hires cloth-workers by the
  day keeps a book of who owes him what and who beat which cloth short, and in
  that book the hands are Ruds, because a master needs to know which breed of
  poor he is dealing with. A Rud can be found again next season; a byname walks
  off.
- **The ward hand's crew-roll.** When the dung is carted and the crew's days are
  tallied for the Bench's thin coin, the roll wants a name it can dock and a
  name it can find, and *Rud* is both.
- **The Wall Muster and the watch-roll.** The ward keeps a list of the able who
  owe drills, and the militia keeps a file, and both prefer a household name to
  a shifting byname; a name that soldiers under is a name the muster clerk keeps
  alive on purpose.
- **The parish pauper-roll and the charnel lintel.** Even the dead are written:
  when a Rud is buried too poor to pay, the sexton chalks a name on Saint
  Maren's door, and it is a name, because the poor get a name and a knell like
  anyone (`../core_lore/candor_and_churches.md`).
- **And one grey file** keeps the name for its own reasons, which is the darkest
  use of all, and belongs below the fence.

So the name persists as a *label*, kept legible by the institutions that need to
grip the poor: hire them, pay them, muster them, dun them, bury them, watch
them. The Ruds do not carry the name so much as the name is carried *on* them,
by other hands, for other purposes. This is the answer to the whole document's
question, and it is not a warm one:

> **A Rud is a person the city has decided to keep track of.** The name survives
> because being poor in one place, in one trade, for three generations, makes
> you worth writing down — and a name written down enough times by enough
> ledgers stays said, long after it stops meaning anything to the mouth that
> answers to it. The Ruds are known. In a city of grey coats, *known* is a coat
> that keeps the rain off and a coat that marks you in a crowd, and it is the
> same coat.

The family's own word for this, said flat, is the house's truest saying, and it
cuts exactly two ways:

> **"There are more Ruds than there is Rud."** Most of us who carry it have
> nothing the name was ever for. Said as consolation — *you are not alone in
> owning nothing* — and said as a warning — *the name is worthless, and worth
> less every year.* Both at once, and the Ruds know it.

## The trade — fulling, and the water a dry city sells

More Ruds work cloth than anything else, and the cloth-work the name is thickest
in is the lowest, wettest, and least loved of the whole trade: **fulling.** A
writer setting a scene at the tubs needs the mechanics, because the poverty, the
temper, and the slow dying of this house all come off the same trough.

**What the work is.** Woven wool comes off the loom loose, greasy, and open; it
must be *fulled* — cleaned of its grease and beaten until the fibres close and
mat and the cloth shrinks tight and warm — before it is any use. In a city with
a mill and a running river this is water-work, done under a wheel. Ombreval has
neither. The Serle runs beyond the south wall and the Cut is filled and dry
(`../the_dry_boatmen.md`), so the fulling is done the hard way, by foot and
hand, in **tubs** — the **stocks** — set in workshops and yards along the dry
Cut, at the Cinder end and below Maren's Green. A fuller stands to the knee in a
trough of cloth and liquor and treads it, hour on hour, or beats it with a
stock-hammer, from the **Kindling** bell until the light goes. Tam Rud does
exactly this, "from the Kindling bell," and hauls the sodden cloth up afterward
to the tenter-yards with his arms dead to the shoulder, and the stub's summary
of his day is the trade's summary entire:

> *Stale piss, hot lye and wet wool: that is your day, for three bells a day and
> a master who counts every ell.*

**The liquor, and who pays for it.** The trough is not water. It is water made
foul on purpose: **stale urine** for the ammonia that cuts the grease,
**fuller's earth** for the fine clay that lifts it, and **hot lye** to scour.
Every one of these costs a poor city money, and the costing is the fullers'
standing grievance, which no one outside the trade has ever thought about:

- **Water itself is bought.** A dry city has no free stream to full in; the
  water in the tubs is drawn from wells or carted, and a fulling-yard drinks a
  great deal of it, and every bucket is a spark out of a trade that has no
  sparks to spare. The boatmen say the diversion made the whole ward *lift it
  twice*; the fullers say the same river left them buying by the bucket what a
  mill town gets for nothing, and nobody counts a fuller's grievance because a
  fuller is beneath counting.
- **Urine is collected and paid for.** At the Kindling the pots are carried out
  — from houses, from taverns, from the barracks — and a fuller or a fuller's
  boy buys them, a **piss-penny** the pot, a copper spark or less, and the
  households that sell think it filthy and funny and take the coin. *A
  piss-penny* is the Ruds' word for the smallest money there is, the price of a
  thing worth almost nothing that you still cannot afford to give away.
- **Lye burns.** It is made from wood-ash and it eats skin, and a fuller's hands
  and forearms carry it — cracked, raw, pale-scalded, slow to heal. A Rud's
  hands tell the trade before the mouth does.

**The tenters.** Fulled cloth is rinsed and then stretched, wet, on frames set
with rows of hooks — **tenterhooks** — to dry flat and true to width. The
frame-grounds are the trade's one open, lawful, almost pleasant face:
**Tenterhook Lane** south of the cloth halls, where the frames catch sun and
moving air, and the **tenter-yards below Maren's Green**, where Tam hauls his
day's cloth up from the Cut. To be *on the tenters* — *stretched on the
tenterhooks* — is the city's whole idiom for suspense and for being squeezed,
and the fullers gave it the city, and use it themselves for a debt pulled tight
or a wait that will not end.

**The theft off the frames.** Cloth left stretched overnight to dry is cloth
left out of doors, and it walks. **Tenter-theft** — lifting a length off another
man's frame in the dark — is the cloth-quarter's steady, unglamorous petty
crime, the way *coming by barrow* is the Reed Ward's (`../the_dry_boatmen.md`).
A length that **"came off the frame"** is a length nobody can prove was ever on
it, and the phrase means *stolen, and quietly* exactly as *come by barrow* means
*untolled, and quietly.* The bitter part, for a Rud, is that the name sits on
both ends of it: a Rud is as likely to have a length lifted off his master's
frame — and be docked for it — as to be the shadow lifting one off somebody
else's, and the ward watch that walks the tenter-grounds at night is as likely
to be a Rud as the thief is. The family is on every side of its own trade's
crime, and at none of the profit.

**The fuller in the window.** There is one place a Rud fuller stands in glory,
and he will almost never see it. In the Great Rose, among the twelve trades of
Ring III, the **fuller** has his own panel — *cloth trodden in the trough,* in a
cold **grey-white** (`../second_sun/09_rose_window_iconography.md`, III-18) —
and at mid-forenoon the Emblem climbs the south lights and passes across him,
*the fuller before the draper,* the disc's own green-white laid across the
panel's cold grey-white. The least-loved trade in the city is set in the most
sacred window in the city, all but in the colour of the impossible light. But
a fuller is at his tub at the
strong hour, not in the nave, and most Ruds have seen the Emblem once or twice
in their lives and many never; the panel that shows their work to a thousand
pilgrims is a thing they are told about and do not go to look at, because
looking costs a morning's wage and the cloth will not tread itself.

### The arithmetic of three bells

The wage is the same three silver bells a journeyman gets anywhere
(`../core_lore/trade_and_daily_life.md`), and it is the ceiling: a Rud fuller is
paid by the day, not by the piece, so working harder fills the master's cloth
and not the fuller's purse. Set against it:

- A **loaf** is two sparks; a day's three bells is thirty-six sparks, which is
  eighteen loaves before anything else — and there is a great deal else. A
  corner in a **widow court**, water by the bucket, the piss-penny, fuller's
  earth and lye against the master's dock, fuel that is dear in the cold, a
  patch of thread, and whatever the household pot must hold for the ones too
  young or too old or too sick to earn.
- A **pauper's funeral with a knell costs eight bells** — near three days' work
  — which is why a Rud who dies poor does not pay for his own burying; *some
  quiet purse pays, nobody says whose*
  (`../second_sun/07_what_everyone_knows.md`), and the family takes the charity
  and the knell and asks no questions, and there are reasons below the fence why
  asking would be unwise.
- **The frost** is the wall the year runs into. When it is too cold and wet for
  cloth to dry, the tenters stand empty, the tubs slow, and the day-wage stops —
  and a day-paid trade has no cushion under it, so the cold months are eaten as
  debt, the pot goes thin, and the widow courts bury their weakest. The boatmen
  call ruin-by-debt *a dry drowning* — quietly, on land, in full view, nobody
  putting out a hand — and a fuller drowns dry every hard winter, a little.

> *A leaf of a fulling-master's day-book, the kind of page the name lives
> inside:*
>
> > Fulled this week at the Cinder tubs — six hands.
> > **Rud** (T.), five days, dockt half a bell the grey broadcloth run
> > narrow at the list, one ell short of measure — paid two bells ten.
> > **Rud** (the widow), four days, well-water her own, no dock — one bell eight.
> > Owing to me for earth and lye, carried: **Rud** (T.), a bell and four.
>
> Nobody at that tub is *of* the name to the master; they are stock he keeps a
> book on, and the book is where "Rud" gets written this week, and next, whether
> the family likes it or not. *The master counts every ell* is not a complaint
> about arithmetic. It is a complaint about being counted.

## The name as a map of the poor streets

Because the Ruds are a labour-roll and not a house, the name's *spread* is its
truest portrait. It does not cluster round a workshop; it smears along the poor
streets between the wards, wherever cheap hands are wanted, and reading the
smear is reading the city's poverty from the inside. Use the ambient bearers of
the name exactly this way — **in aggregate, anonymous, and swappable** — a role,
a ward, a household marker, never a biography.

**Bell-and-Sluice — the household pot.** The name is rooted, as much as it is
rooted anywhere, in the crowded central ward around the Bellstand, the city's
mixing bowl (`overview.md`). Here the Ruds are not a workshop but a **pot**: a
shared work-and-lodging group where wages, barter, and scraps go into one
cook-pot and come out as everybody's dinner, and the pot is the nearest thing
the name has to a hearth. Around it are the ones who cannot earn a full share —
an orphan errand child of five whose apron carries three square patches; a
message-runner of fourteen with a sore heel; a housemaid held this winter in the
**Stone House** for something or other, fed on gaol rations and what the pot
sends in by a cousin's hand — and the ones who earn the pot's bread: a dung
carter, a militia file leader, and two old men on their pensions. The pot is how
the name survives at all. It is also why a Rud is never quite destitute and
never once ahead: there is always another mouth, and the pot is always exactly
empty by the next Kindling.

**Wick — in the shadow of the wax.** West into Wickmarket the name runs to a
garment-cutter and a wick-maker and their boys — a pilgrim of fourteen with a
scarred lip, an errand child of ten — poor cloth-and-light hands living under
the Vells, the risen chandlers whose wax goes daily into the Lanthorn
(`family_vell.md`). The nearness is the whole joke of it: a Rud dips cheap
tallow wicks as a hired journeyman a street from a Vell basket bound for the
high altar, and when the tallow ran in the gutters the year the Wickmarket
burned (F.407) it was hands like these that scooped the warm wax while the
chandlers let them. A Rud wick-maker makes light for a living and cannot afford
to burn it.

**Cinder — the soot streets.** South to the glaziers' quarter, rebuilt after its
fire (F.171), the name runs to the furnace trades that are *not* glass: an
apprentice smith with soot in her knuckles, a tile-maker who chatters when she
is nervous, a cobbler with a broken-handled basket, a widow at the fulling tubs
at the Cinder end of the Cut. They work beside the Sparrs and the glaziers and
are not of them; a Cinder Rud watches the glass procession go up to the Gradine
at Ambrellestide from the wrong side of the guild line, soot on her hands and
none of the standing that a fired trade carries.

**The Reed edge — thinnest of all.** And a thread of the name reaches the water
quarter, though no Rud is *of the water*: a furnace hand lately arrived and not
yet trusted, a fuller or two at the tubs below Maren's Green. The
[boatmen's doc](../the_dry_boatmen.md) has the Ruds exactly right —
*ward-resident, not boat-blood; they keep no pole to lay over a door* — a name
that works beside the boat-houses and marries into none of the argument, present
on the Green the way the poor are present everywhere: doing the wet work and
holding no share.

**How to spot a Rud.** The name has no crest, no livery, no colour of its own —
but it has a look, and the look is *mending.* A brass button where a good clasp
used to be; a loop of cheap blue cord holding a child's hair, the one dye the
cloth-poor can come by; one shoe with a new wooden sole and its fellow worn
through; an apron with three square patches; a horn spoon through the belt; a
basket carried by its one whole handle. Nothing a Rud wears was bought new by a
Rud. The whole name mends rather than buys, patches rather than replaces, and
keeps a thing going three owners past its death, which is the household habit
that the shared pot makes and the frost enforces. If you want to mark an ambient
Rud in one detail, give them a careful repair — and remember it is a role and
not a person: the button, the cord, the wooden sole are the name's uniform of
poverty, worn by whoever the pot is feeding this year, and swappable for the
next.

## The three the name lets you name

Out of seventeen Ruds, exactly three stand solid enough to name and build a
scene on; the other fourteen are the aggregate above, to be used at role-level
and never pinned. And the three are not a household — they are in three
different wards, in three different trades, and would not all know one another's
faces — which is the name in miniature: not a family you can gather, but three
people the same sound happens to reach. Each holds a different answer to *is it
worth being a Rud?*, and none of the three would put it that way, because none
of them has time to.

### Aldith Rud — the city's circulatory system

Aldith (56, dung carter, Bell-and-Sluice) is the name's institution, if a name
with no institutions can be said to have one. Read her sheet: irregular work and
the neighbours' small alms barely keep her fed; she answers strangers in clipped
practical phrases; a brass button replaces the clasp at her neck; she sets the
crew's route from the market gutters toward the gate carts and argues for full
wages; and *people in this ward return to her because she remembers agreements
and faces.* That last is the whole of her, and it is the whole of the family's
answer to its own question rendered in one working life.

Her trade is the one nobody wants and the city cannot survive a week without.
Sanitation crews clear the dung, the privies, the gutters, and the refuse, and
carry it *outward* — from the market gutters where it fouls the trade streets to
the gate carts that take it beyond the walls
(`../core_lore/occupation_descriptions.md`). Aldith sets the route: which yards,
in which order, to which gate, at what hour, so a crew of the ward's poorest
gets through a day's filth and is home before the Snuffing. She is, in the
plainest possible sense, a part of the city's circulation — the ward's waste
moves because a fifty-six-year-old woman decides each morning which way it will
go — and she carries the knowledge that comes with it, which is enormous and
entirely unglamorous: **she knows every yard in the ward by what it throws
out.** Who is eating meat and who is not; whose privy says there is sickness in
the house; which shop's sweepings say the trade is better than the shopkeeper
admits; who has a lodger they have not declared. A dung carter reads a household
the way the washerwoman Nan reads its linen and the sexton reads its lintel, and
Aldith has read this ward for forty years.

Her standing fight is with the **ward hand**, the part-time officer who tallies
the crew's days for the Bench's thin coin
(`../core_lore/secular_government.md`). The hand wants to count the crew short —
a light day, half a crew, a route not finished — and Aldith wants a *full crew
day* counted for full wages, because the crew did the work and the filth does
not care what the tally says. Both are partly right: the Bench's purse is real
and thin, and the crew's day is real and done, and the fight is remade every
reckoning and never won for good. She fights it with memory, not heat — *you
counted us four last Waning and the gutters were as full; count us five* — and
she wins as often as she does precisely because the ward hand knows she
remembers, and knows the ward believes her over him.

Keep her **flat, dry, and unheretical.** Aldith holds nothing of what is fenced
below; her sheet's own line settles it — she *means to keep her place without
pretending that every local quarrel concerns the Great Rose*, which is her whole
politics and cuts against the heresy-hunters and the martyr-makers in one
breath. She is not warm and she is not cold; she is *reliable*, which in a widow
court is worth more than either. Her answer to the family's question — *is the
name worth carrying?* — is the one answer that has actually been tested and come
back yes: the name is worth being known by, and she has spent a life making the
being-known pay, one remembered agreement at a time. She is the proof that the
handle can be gripped from the good end. She is also fifty-six and a pauper, and
the being-known has bought her a place and not an inch more, and she would tell
you that too, in four words, and go back to her route.

### Petronel Rud — the climbing edge

Petronel (18, apprentice smith, Cinder Row) is the one place the name goes *up*.
Read her sheet: she meets strangers with guarded good manners; her knuckles are
stained with soot; she lives on wages, barter, and a place in the shared pot;
she needs, today, to borrow a handcart for an hour; and the visible condition
she lives with is a **speech impediment.** She is the youngest solid figure in
the name and the only one on a rising line, and everything about her is quietly,
gently governed by the naming law.

She works iron, and the smith — like the fuller — has a panel in the Great Rose,
*at a black-red fire with yellow sparks* (one of the twelve trades of Ring III;
`../core_lore/occupation_descriptions.md`), which she has as little leisure to
go and see as Tam has to see the fuller. The trade suits her, and the suiting is
not an accident of the design's kindness: a smith is judged by the work of her
hands and not the speech of her mouth, and a young woman for whom words come
hard has found the one trade where the iron does the talking and a true weld is
a true weld however you'd say it. She is guarded because she has learned to be —
a stammer in a market is a thing people take for slowness, and she has spent
eighteen years proving the anvil otherwise — and she remembers agreements and
faces for the same reason Aldith does: it is how the poor keep credit without a
ledger.

Here is the gentle engine. **If Petronel finishes her articles and makes
journeyman, the street will begin to call her *Petronel Smith*** — a byname of
trade, earned and not chosen, exactly as the law mints them — and the *Rud* will
fall off her the way scale falls off cooling iron, without anyone deciding it,
without a quarrel, without a word said. She is not trying to shed the name; she
is just becoming a smith, and becoming a smith is how you stop being a Rud. So
she is the family's live illustration of its own slow fall, and she cuts two
ways at once, which is why she belongs at the centre of the house and not the
edge:

- She is the name **rescued** — a Rud who climbs out of the fault-line of
  poverty into a trade with standing, the one bright thing the name has done in
  a generation, and the pot will be proud of her in its wordless way and send
  the handcart round when she needs it.
- She is the name **leaving** — because the way up is the way out, and every Rud
  who becomes a Smith or a Cobbler or a Wright is one fewer Rud, and a name
  already fraying loses a strand each time it succeeds. The Ruds cannot climb
  without climbing away from the name, and the name cannot be kept without
  keeping people down in it.

Do not settle which she is. Her articles are not finished; a smith can be turned
out, an apprenticeship can fail, a handcart borrowed for an hour can be the
whole of a day's ambition. Leave *Petronel Smith* a possibility on the anvil,
and let the family hold both feelings about her — the pride and the small grief
— without ever saying either, because saying either would name the thing the
whole house is trying not to look at.

### Tam Rud — the one exception

Everything in this section is what the city could see if it looked, and any of
it may go into any Rud's prompt or Tam's own. What Tam *is* underneath — the
oath, the faction, the list, the danger he shares with his cousin — is fenced at
the foot of this document and belongs to his context and the designers alone.
Render the fuller freely; render the heretic nowhere but there.

Tam (29, journeyman fuller, the Cut below Maren's Green and the tenter-yards) is
the one Rud the paragraph above ends on — the single man whose anger did not
stay ordinary — and *where* it went is fenced at the foot of this file. Above
the fence, even that is entirely deniable and entirely common. He is **angry
the way wet wool is heavy — all through, and slow to dry** — the
family temper at its purest, a fuller's whole day of beating something clean
turned into a disposition. He is blunt with strangers and worse with anyone in a
good coat; he says far more than he should after two pots at **Renna
Tapster's**, the Bell and Ladle on the Bellstand, where the room holds the
year's wagers on whether the doors will be barred again; and he carries two
wounds that the whole city could name and half the city shares:

- **The dead teacher.** An old fuller taught Tam his letters — a rare gift among
  the cloth-poor, and the reason Tam can *read*, which sets him a little apart
  at the tubs and matters more than it looks — and that old fuller died of the
  damp in the Custody's keeping after the Whisper Arrests (F.428). This is
  common knowledge in its bones: *a fuller died in keeping, of the damp*, is a
  thing the street says (`../second_sun/07_what_everyone_knows.md`). That the
  fuller was Tam's teacher is Tam's own grief, and injectable as grief; who the
  fuller was, and how he came to die, are not Tam's to know and not this
  document's to say (see the fence, and the fuller stays nameless everywhere, as
  canon leaves him).
- **The child's arm.** At last year's Concurrence the Chapter barred the nave
  doors at the moment of the Passing, the crowd crushed on the Gradine, and a
  child's arm was broken (F.436, the Shut Door). Tam was in that crush. The
  child whose arm broke is not a Rud — she is Ede of the Needle, who runs
  messages still with a crooked arm and says it does not hurt
  (`../second_sun/05_dramatis_personae.md`) — and Tam did not know her and does
  not need to; the arm is his because he was there and because it is the exact
  size of everything he cannot forgive.

Out of these Tam wants **this year's Concurrence loud** — the forbidden verse
sung, the doors open or made open — and he says so, in taverns, in the deniable
half of the sentence. *They'll bar the doors again this year; the Chapter never
unlearns a thing it learned in fear* is a thing half the Bellstand says and no
grey coat can cite (`../second_sun/06_rumor_pool.md`, R-011); it is the *other*
half of that sentence that hangs a man, and where the other half lives is below
the fence. His two lines, both canon, both injectable, both doing the same trick
of hiding a blade in a fuller's plain speech:

> **"Cloth needs beating to come clean. So do some institutions."** Said over
> the tubs, it is a fuller's sour joke and nothing a court could touch. Said
> with his eyes somewhere else, it is the whole of the Breach in eight words,
> and the room decides which it heard.

> **"Ask me about the doors last year. Go on. Ask me about the child's arm."**
> The safe wound, offered like a dare. Everyone in Ombreval has a version of it;
> Tam's is only louder, and the loudness is common, and being loud about the
> Shut Door is not a crime, which is exactly why it is such good cover for a man
> who is something more than loud.

Hold the line hard: above the fence, **Tam is a fuller with a temper, a dead
teacher, a crooked-armed reason, and a reading habit** — findable in every ward,
and safe. Half the city is Shut-Door-angry. The neighbours know Tam as one of
the half, and that is all they know, and it is all they should ever know.

## The cousin across the lines

The single most-charged fact the name carries, and the one that says the central
question best, is a cousinship: **Tam Rud the fuller and Sible Mott the grey
clerk are cousins** (`../second_sun/05_dramatis_personae.md`, and both their
sheets). A fuller at the tubs on the Cut and a clerk in the Grey Press with a
wage the Provost-Custodian signs — the lowest work in the city and one of the
quietest seats of its power — are kin, at a remove, back through the same
fulling family.

Above the fence, the cousinship is simply the naming law caught in one
bloodline, and you may render this much anywhere. A couple of lives back there
were a Rud brother and sister of the fulling streets, both long dead; the
brother stayed at the tubs, and his line kept the name and comes down to Tam;
the sister — **Idonea** — married a [Mott](family_mott.md) and went up out of
the wet trades, and her line took the Mott and the standing that came with it
and comes down to
Sible and her grey coat. Same starting blood, and the law sorted the two
branches — one up into a fixed name and a dry wage, one down into a labour-roll
— exactly as it sorts everyone, only here you can see the seam, because the two
ends of it are cousins and both still living. It is *is it worth being a Rud?*
asked by blood: the Mott branch is what the name does when it climbs, and the
Rud branch is what it does when it stays.

And above the fence, that is nearly the whole of it, because the two of them are
not close. A fuller and a grey coat do not share a bench; Tam is *worse with
anyone in a good coat* and his cousin wears the greyest coat there is, and the
distance between them is the ordinary distance between a poor cousin at the tubs
and the cousin who got the good wage and the dry shoulders — a distance of coat
and of wage and of which end of the city you answer to, cordial enough at a
burying and cold enough the rest of the year. Sible's own mother is the
washerwoman **Nan** of the Cut, who wishes the whole world good morning and owes
the Ruds nothing; her people are the Cut's people, and Tam grew up a street from
all of it. There is no feud here and no warmth to speak of — just two cousins on
opposite sides of the grey wall, and a naming law that put them there.

**What passes between these two cousins in the dark — what each of them knows
about the other and is not saying — is the sharpest thing in this whole
document, and it is fenced.** It is not street knowledge; nobody in the ward
knows it; and it must never reach an ambient Rud's prompt or Sible's public
face. Above the fence, they are cousins across a coat. Below it, they are
something much worse and much sadder, and it lives at the foot of this file.

## Anger as the family trade

If the Ruds inherit anything down the generations — and a name with no property
has little else to hand down — they inherit a **temper.** The stub's phrase for
Tam, *angry the way wet wool is heavy — all through, and slow to dry*, is not
one man's weather; it is the family register, and a writer can set it on any Rud
and be in canon. It is not a hot anger. It is a *soaked* one: heavy, patient,
everywhere in the cloth at once, and it takes a long time to dry out and never
quite does. It is the anger of people who work wet and cold for a wage that does
not move, under masters who count every ell, in a name that the city keeps a
book on. It has every reason to exist and almost nowhere lawful to go.

So watch where it goes, because the whole point of this house is that it goes,
overwhelmingly, into ordinary channels — and the channels are the map of the
poor's permitted rage:

- **Into the work.** Most of it is beaten into cloth. Fulling *is* controlled
  anger, six days a week, a man treading and pounding something until it submits
  and comes clean, and a Rud at the tubs is a Rud spending his temper lawfully
  and to a master's profit. The family's own grim word for what most of them do
  with their anger is *beat it into the cloth.*
- **Into drink.** The rest, or a good part of it, goes to Renna Tapster's
  counter, two pots past sense, into a room that holds every opinion and settles
  none. It is where a Rud says the thing he should not and where the ward learns
  which Ruds are merely loud, which is most of them.
- **Into the city's own service.** The city, cannily, *hires* the family temper.
  A Rud soldiers in the Wall Muster and leads a file at eighteen; a Rud kept the
  ward watch thirty years and rings its errands still; the militia and the watch
  are the channels a poor man's anger is *paid* to run in, pointed at the city's
  enemies instead of the city. The name has two old men in one Bell-and-Sluice
  pot who are this exactly, and between them they are the whole span of where a
  Rud's temper ends up: one wore the watch's badge for a working lifetime and
  kept the peace, and the other, a militiaman past seventy, still goes out in
  the dark and quietly wrecks a warehouse's stock for reasons he keeps to
  himself. One badge that held the law and one hand that breaks the stock, fed
  from the same pot, sleeping under the same roof, and the family asks neither
  of them to explain.
- **And, once, somewhere else.** Out of the whole angry name, one man's anger
  left the lawful channels and the drink and the watch and went to a green sun —
  and even his did not go there for the sun. That is Tam, and it is fenced, and
  it is *one*.

The stub keeps a fact this section is built to hold in its right place: **a Rud
has already put his anger into a warehouse.** Keep it exactly as the stub does —
nameless, ordinary, unresolved. Do not make the old warehouse-wrecker a saboteur
with a cause; he is an old man with a grievance and a grudge and a temper the
trade gave him, doing a small, mean, illegal thing in the dark, and the watch in
his own household does not look too hard, and nobody asks whether it is politics
or spite or just the anger finding a wall to spend itself on. The dread of this
house is not a cell. It is a name full of tired, angry, decent people, most of
whom put the anger somewhere it does no harm, and one or two of whom do not, and
no way from the outside to tell which is which until it is done.

## The dead, and the ones who left

A family treatment is mostly a treatment of its dead, and here the rule bites
strangely, because a name that keeps no pedigree keeps its dead the way it keeps
everything else: badly, in the heads of the old, in a chalk mark that washes
off. The Ruds have no bede-roll of their own and no tomb; what they have is a
handful of names the pot still says and a great many it has already lost. Name
the dead freely — they are yours to invent within the naming law — and use them
to carry the shape of the house, which is the shape of a trade that kills slowly
and a poverty that scatters.

**The fuller line, half-remembered.** Behind Tam stands what little the name
keeps of a lineage, and it is cloth all the way down and thin as a chalk line.
**Ansel Rud**, a fuller of the Cut two generations back, is the oldest name the
pot still offers, and the pot offers little else about him than that he trod
cloth and had a temper — the family's whole memory of a man is his trade and his
anger, which is all the name has ever had to hand down. His son **Corin Rud**,
Tam's father, was a fuller too and died as fullers die, of **the damp** — not in
any Custody's keeping, not for anything, just the slow lung-rot the trade lays
on men who work wet and cold for thirty years — while Tam was still a boy, which
is part of why an *old* fuller, not a father, taught Tam his letters. There is
nothing dramatic in Corin's death and that is the point: the trade does not need
arrests to kill a Rud; it does it quietly, by the lungs, and the family's
grimmest superstition is built on it. **"Fuller's luck"** is the worst luck
there is — *the luck of the damp* — and it is said low, and never within earshot
of a grey coat, because the phrase brushes too close to a fuller who died of the
damp in a cell nine years ago, and the poor have learned which words the grey
coats listen for.

**Idonea Rud, who married a Mott.** One name the pot half-keeps and half-buries
is the woman who is the reason a fuller and a grey clerk are cousins: **Idonea
Rud**, Ansel's sister, a Rud of the fulling streets who married a Mott a couple
of lives back and went up out of the wet trades into a house with a name that
stayed. She is
spoken of rarely and without much warmth — the poor do not always love the kin
who climb — and what the family remembers is mostly that she *left*, and that
her line kept the Mott and theirs kept the Rud, and that is how the blood
forked. She is the dead hinge of the cousinship (below, and the fence), and she
is the family's proof that the way out of the name is marriage or trade, and
either way it is *out*.

**The Hammering, in the widow courts.** The name's great wound is the city's
great wound, and the cloth-poor took it in a particular shape. When the
Hammering fell on a crowded Highmarket (F.415), the hail broke the cloth-halls'
tiles and the chronicle boasted that *the Draper's Reach kept the wool dry* —
and a later hand cut it to the bone: *the wool was dry; the people under the
western loft were not* (`../second_sun/04_chronicle_of_the_city.md`). The
cloth-poor die that death worst, because the cloth-poor **lodge high and lodge
cheap** — a loft, a garret, an upper room in a widow court let by the ell of
floor — and a roof that comes down comes down on the top storey first. The Ruds
lost a court's worth that day, up under a Bell-and-Sluice roof, and got for it
the common mourning toll and no more: their dead are **Unknelled**, folded into
the city's belated grief, *one bell is not a name*. The widow courts took their
name from exactly this — courts where nearly every household lost someone — and
the Ruds are a widow-court name, thick where the roofs fell.

**The Long Departure — a fuller can leave.** And the name lost as many to the
road as to the roof, because the ugly freedom of the cloth-poor is that they own
nothing to hold them. A mason is tied to his lodge, a chandler to her vats, an
Alder to the Moorings; a fuller is tied to nothing but a wage he can get in any
wool town on the Serle, and after the Hammering the ones who could walk, walked.
**Dunstan Rud**, a fuller, went down the Serle in the year of the departures
(departed F.416) and did not come back, and left behind him the one thing a
fuller *does* own, which is what he owed — so that the family's flat word on him
is the trade's whole bitter economics in a breath: **a fuller can leave; a
fuller's debt cannot.** A letter came once, signing itself well, or none did;
the pot is not sure, and it comes to the same thing. The ones who stayed feel
loyal and stupid in the usual proportion, and the name is thinner for every
Dunstan, and this is the same Long Departure the [Crakes](family_crake.md) are
made of, seen from lower down, where there was never a boat to sell — only a
debt to skip and a road that went somewhere.

**The trade's ghost.** The fullers keep one dead the whole city keeps with them,
though the city has moved him. In Gaunt Passage after the last bell, they say,
you can hear cloth being wrung — the slow twist, the dripping — on nights when
the stones are dry as a sermon, and the air goes river-damp and smells of wet
wool where no wool is: **the fuller**, dead of the damp before ever he was
tried, come back to wring his cloth at the door they took him from
(`../second_sun/08_folk_culture.md`). Say *good night to the trade* and walk
quick, and **never guess at his name**, for nobody living is sure of it and the
dead hate being guessed at wrong. It is a good ghost for a passage the fullers
would otherwise cut through, and most tell it for the shiver. A Rud fuller does
not tell it for the shiver, and if you set a scene where one is asked to, let
him decline and change the subject — never gloss the last thing the old women
add about the ghost, that he is *listening for one voice*; that line brushes a
true thing nobody living is allowed to resolve, and a Rud is the last person who
should be made to try.

## Burying a Rud

The great houses each have a rite to bury their own by — the Alders lay a pole
across the coffin, the Marles sew the last hem of the winding-sheet by the
family's own needle, the Vells burn a whole taper. **The Ruds have no rite,
because a rite is a thing you can afford,** and the absence is the truest thing
the house does over its dead. What happens when a Rud dies is what happens to
the poor everywhere in Ombreval, and the family's only mark on it is small
enough to break your heart if you let it, so do not — handle the grief at arm's
length, the way the corpus does.

**The quiet purse.** A pauper's funeral with a knell costs eight bells, near
three days' work, and a Rud who dies poor cannot pay it, so a Rud who dies poor
is buried on charity: *some quiet purse pays the paupers' funerals, and nobody
says whose* (`../second_sun/07_what_everyone_knows.md`). The family takes it —
the knell, the chalked name, the plain rites Sexton Noll Fitch gives every soul
the same — and asks no questions, because you do not interrogate the hand that
buries your mother, and because (below the fence) there are reasons a Rud in
particular is wise not to ask whose the purse is. Above the fence: the Ruds are
exactly the kind of poor the quiet purse exists for, and it has almost certainly
buried more than one of them, and the family is grateful and incurious in the
ordinary way of people who have learned not to look a charity in the mouth.

**The chalk that washes off.** The newest buried name is chalked on Saint
Maren's charnel-door lintel until the next burial writes over it
(`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §1). For a name that anchors nothing, there is a
hard aptness in this: *Rud* goes up on the lintel in chalk, is read for a few
days, and is washed off by the next rain or the next name, exactly as the family
name itself is being slowly washed off the living. *Chalk washes off; names
don't*, the sexton says — and the whole question of this house is whether he is
right, because the Rud name is a thing being tested against precisely that hope.
A Rud *chalked on the lintel* is doubly what the phrase means citywide — *as
good as dead, he just hasn't heard the bell* — for a family the lintel is a fair
emblem of: written in the softest medium there is, and read by people who will
not remember it long.

**The scrap of blue.** Here is the family's one mark, and it costs almost
nothing, which is why it is theirs. The Rud children go with a loop of cheap
**blue cord** in their hair — the one dye the cloth-poor can come by, the
near-worthless blue that a fuller's or dyer's leavings will give — and when a
Rud is buried, a length of that blue cord goes with them: tied to the bier on
the way up Maren's Slip, or cut from a living child's hair and put in the ground
with a dead one, a thread of the only colour the family owns. It is not a rite
and it would not be recognized as one outside the pot; it is just the poorest
possible grave-gift, a scrap of blue given because it is the one thing that is
*theirs* to give, in a burying otherwise entirely made of other people's
charity. Somebody always weeps at a Rud burying and it is never who you expect —
not the widow, who has done this before, but the child who is made to give up
her hair-cord, and cannot say why it is the cord and not the death that undoes
her, because she is six and the death is too big and the cord was hers.

## The year

The Candor's calendar rules the Ruds as it rules everyone
(`../core_lore/calendar_and_history.md`), but a day-paid, six-days-worked,
cloth-poor name lives it thin: most feasts are, to a Rud, either a day the wage
stops or a day the bread is given, and the difference between a holy day and a
hungry one is who is handing out the loaf. The thread through the Rud year is
that they are *in the working half of every feast* — carting, treading, dipping,
mending — and *in the receiving half of every charity*, and they see the sky
less than almost anyone, because the strong hour finds them at the tubs.

**Bellday** is the one morning the tubs are still. The weekly holy day fills the
nave, and a Rud who is not too tired stands in it — at the back, in the one good
coat the pot keeps for whoever needs it — and may, like anyone, speak a dead
name quietly into the green beam, Corin or the Hammering dead, affirming
nothing. But Bellday is also the day a pauper's burying is most likely to fall,
and a Rud is as apt to spend it standing at Saint Maren's over one of the pot's
own dead, counting the slow strokes of the name-knell, as kneeling in the
Lanthorn. The two places are a quarter-mile and a whole world apart, and the
poor go where the day sends them.

**Colm's Night** (early spring) the Ruds keep the way half the city keeps it — a
single green-dipped candle burned at home for a drowned poor man — and they keep
it with a carelessness the respectable cannot afford. A climbing house lights
that candle nervously, glancing at the shutters, because a lawful green flame
looks too much like the thing a grey coat cites; a Rud lights it without a
second thought, because a Rud has nothing to lose that a candle could cost, and
the pot is not watching itself the way a house of standing watches itself. And
that carelessness has a small cost, because Colm's Night is a night the grey
coats are quietly about — a clerk somewhere on the dark end of the street, being
seen, citing nothing — and a green flame in an unshuttered poor window is the
easiest thing in the ward to notice and the hardest to forget. Nothing is ever
cited. Everyone is noticed anyway. The Ruds do not know to mind it and could not
afford to mind it if they did, because you cannot be careful on a Rud's budget.

**Vhairestide** (spring) turns the year and walks the boat-families out through
the Reed Postern to bless the boats (`../the_dry_boatmen.md`); the Reed-edge
Ruds stand at the margin of a feast that is not theirs, ward-resident and not
boat-blood, and walk back in when it is done. **Ambrellestide** (early summer)
is the glaziers' day, and the Cinder Ruds — the smith, the tile-maker, the
cobbler — watch the glass go up from Cinder Row to the Gradine from the wrong
side of the guild line, furnace trades with no glass and no standing, near
enough to the procession to smell the hot glass and not near enough to be in it.

**Marenstide** (late summer) fills Maren's Green with eel smoke and the roll of
the drowned, and here the Ruds stand where the
[boatmen's argument](../the_dry_boatmen.md) touches them: their Hammering dead
are exactly the kind the parish now reads onto the roll of the drowned, so a Rud
at the Green at Marenstide hears their own roof-crushed kin argued over by
boat-families who say *you cannot drown under slate* — and mostly keeps the flat
peace, because a fuller does not win an argument with a boatman on the boatman's
own Green, and a name got by charity does not raise its voice.

**The Hammering anniversary** (late summer, the mourning toll at the Waning) is
the family's plainest grief. When the common toll sounds the Ruds hear the widow
courts under it, and they answer as the traditional poor answer — *one bell is
not a name* — and mean the thing every under-buried house means by it: give me
back the name of my dead. They have meant, for twenty-two years, to pay the
belated name-knells the family owes its loft-crushed dead, and every year the
pot has eaten the bells, and the dead stay Unknelled, and will, because eight
bells is three days' work and there is always a living mouth ahead of a dead
name.

**The Concurrence** (the fortieth day after Coswaldstide) is the great feast and
the live fuse of the whole city (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §4). Last year the
Chapter barred the doors at the Passing — the Shut Door, the crush, the child's
arm — and this year the doors are the question the city cannot stop asking, and
everyone watches the sky, for a clouded Concurrence answers nothing. The Ruds
have the least say in it and a sharp exposure to it: the cloth-poor mostly
cannot get into the nave at the strong hour anyway, being at the tubs or shut
out at the back of the crowd, and the Shut Door shut a door on people already at
the bottom of the press. Tam wants it loud (above), and the ward's fury runs hot
in Renna Tapster's room where the wagers are laid; the rest of the name wants
what the poor always want of a great feast, which is for it to pass without
costing them a day's wage or a child's arm.

**Wet Alms** (winter) finds the Ruds on the receiving side of the bread at the
nave door, where a name of standing gives and the poor take, and the Ruds take.
And **Gaudry's Audit** (late winter) proves the ell of Ombreval and the sealed
weights against the Tallage stone — the [Marles](family_marle.md) and the
[Vells](family_vell.md) watch it keenly, because a house that sells by the ell
or the pound has a stake in an honest standard. The Ruds do not go, because the
Ruds have no stake in the standard: they are paid by the *day* and docked by the
*master's* count, and an honest city ell does not help a fuller whose master
swears his cloth ran an ell short. Their quarrel is never with the Tallage
stone. It is with the book at the tub, and no audit proves that.

**The frost** is the season with no feast, and it is the one that governs the
year. When cloth will not dry, the wage stops, the pot goes thin, the credit
tightens, and the widow courts bury their weakest, and a Rud drowns dry a
little, quietly, on land, in full view, with everybody watching and nobody
putting out a hand.

## How they talk

A Rud is marked in one line by two things: **the trade in the mouth** — a speech
soaked in fulling and refuse and iron, the way the boatmen's is soaked in the
river — and **a blade kept in plain speech**, because a name the city writes
down learns early to say the dangerous thing in words a court cannot hold. The
idiom below is direct prompt fodder, injectable on its own, and the sharp ones
cut two ways, because a poor mouth cannot afford a saying that only means one
thing.

- **"Beat it into the cloth."** What most Ruds do with their anger: spend it
  lawfully at the tubs, on the wool, to a master's profit. Said as advice to a
  young hothead it is real counsel — *put it in the work* — and said of a man
  gone quiet after a wrong it is a warning, because a temper beaten in all day
  comes out somewhere at night. The whole family's relation to its own anger is
  in the phrase.
- **"Cloth needs beating to come clean."** Tam's line, and the trade's, and the
  perfect example of the blade in plain speech: over the tubs it is a sour joke
  about the work, and with the eyes elsewhere it is the Breach entire, and the
  hearer decides which was meant. Any Rud fuller can say the first meaning; only
  one means the second; and that is exactly why it is safe to say.
- **"In the stocks."** At the fulling tubs — and, said dry, in trouble, because
  the word is the punishment-frame's too, and a fuller stands in his trough the
  better part of a life. *He's been in the stocks since the Kindling* is a plain
  report and a small complaint at once.
- **"On the tenters" / "stretched on the tenterhooks."** Held in suspense;
  pulled tight to the edge. The fullers gave the city this one off their
  drying-frames and use it for a debt pulled taut and a wait that will not end,
  which is most of a poor life.
- **"Came off the frame."** Stolen, and quietly — the cloth-quarter's twin to
  the Reed Ward's *come by barrow.* A length that came off the frame is a length
  nobody can prove was ever on it; say a thing *came off the frame* and you have
  called it thieved without the word.
- **"A piss-penny."** The smallest coin there is, the price of a pot of stale
  urine at the Kindling: a thing worth almost nothing that you still cannot
  afford to give away for free. *Not a piss-penny in it* is the bottom of the
  purse; *he'd skin a piss-penny* is said of a master who docks the poor.
- **"Buying your own water."** Paying for a thing that should be free — the
  fullers' grievance at a dry city, and by extension any arrangement where the
  poor are charged for what was taken from them. A boatman *lifts it twice*; a
  fuller *buys his own water*; both mean the world has been arranged against
  them and calls it their private cost.
- **"Fuller's luck."** The worst luck of all — *the luck of the damp* — said low
  and never within a grey coat's hearing, because it brushes a fuller who died
  of the damp in a cell, and the poor know which words are listened for. Wish a
  man *fuller's luck* and you have cursed him about as far as a careful mouth
  will go.
- **"Beat clean and hung out to dry."** Used and then abandoned: a man worked
  hard for another's profit and then left to the frost. The trade's own life in
  five words, said with a shrug that is worse than bitterness.
- **"There are more Ruds than there is Rud."** The house's truest line (above):
  most who carry the name have nothing it was ever for — consolation and threat
  in the one breath.
- **"Known to the ward."** Findable; a face the ward keeps. Said warmly it is
  credit — *he's known to the ward, he'll get the work* — and said flat it is
  the cold fact that a name written in enough books can be found in the dark by
  anyone holding one. Being known is the name's whole worth and its whole
  danger, and the Ruds use the one phrase for both.
- **"One shadow to your road." / "And one light over it."** The city's blessing
  at doors, said by the Ruds like everyone
  (`../core_lore/sayings_and_customs.md`) — and worth marking here because it is
  one of the few things a family with nothing has to *give*: a Rud at a door can
  send you off with the same blessing a merchant can, and it costs the same,
  which is nothing, which is why the poor say it most.

Aldith's register is its own mark and the plainest in the house: **clipped,
practical, and built on memory.** *Count us a full crew day.* *I know your yard
by what you throw out.* *You said the Waning; it's the Waning.* One line of it
and you have a Rud who deals in remembered facts and not in heat, and who will
be believed over the officer because the ward has learned she remembers. What
one line reveals, across the name: a fuller's flat speech with something sharp
folded inside it marks Tam and his kind; a clipped account of a remembered
agreement marks Aldith's kind; a careful repair and a loop of blue cord marks
the ambient rest; and any of them saying *known to the ward* is telling you,
whether they mean to or not, that the name is the only property they have and
that it is held in other people's hands.

## The arms — there are none

The Ruds bear no arms, and the blank at the top of this page is not an oversight
but the plainest thing the heraldry says. Arms follow standing (`overview.md`):
a house gets a shield when it has property, an office, or a workshop worth
naming, and the Ruds have never had any of the three. The crest register reaches
for a wittier reason — *cloth-poor; the Unwalled do not display arms*
(`crests/README.md`) — and it is a good dry line, but it flatters the family by
half, because the Ruds could not blazon arms if every one of the seventeen were
sworn Greensick tomorrow. There is no charge for *a labour-roll*, no tincture
for *lye-scald*, no field on which to marshal seventeen poor hands scattered
across four wards under a name that anchors nothing. A shield says *here is a
thing worth defending and a family solid enough to defend it*; the Ruds have
neither the thing nor the solidity, and the empty space where the arms would go
says so more honestly than any charge could. If you want the family's device, it
is the one they wear: a brass button where a good clasp should be, and a loop of
blue cord — mended, cheap, and nobody's grant.

## Quarrels worth playing

Every one is live in F.437, and none has a right answer. Unlike the great
houses' quarrels, these are rarely fought across a table, because the Ruds have
no table; they are worked out in the street, at the tub, at the gaol gate, one
poor decision at a time, and written for designers to drop a scene onto.

1. **Petronel's name.** If she finishes her articles she becomes *Petronel
   Smith*, and the *Rud* falls off her — the name rescued by her climbing and
   lost by her climbing, both at once. Is a Rud who becomes a Smith a triumph or
   a defection? The pot holds both feelings and says neither, and her articles
   are not finished, so play the pride and the small grief and never resolve
   which the family is entitled to.

2. **The crew day.** Aldith fights the ward hand every reckoning to have a
   *full* crew day counted for full wages; the hand counts the crew short. Both
   are partly right — the Bench's coin is real and thin, the crew's day is real
   and done — and the fight is remade and never won for good. Play the
   memory-duel: she wins by being believed, and loses when a new hand does not
   yet know to believe her.

3. **The master's count.** A fulling-master docks a Rud for cloth run short at
   the list; the Rud swears the count is rigged; the master swears the cloth was
   skimped. It is the boatmen's quarrel over the crept tun in a cloth key, and
   like that one it has no honest answer available, because the only book is the
   master's and the only word against it is a poor man's. Keep the master
   anonymous and the quarrel open.

4. **On every side of the frame.** Tenter-theft is the ward's petty crime, and
   the Ruds are on all three sides of it at once: a Rud loses cloth off his
   master's frame and is docked for it; a Rud shadow lifts a length off somebody
   else's; and a Rud on the ward watch walks the tenter-grounds at night meant
   to stop both. Set a scene where the thief, the victim, and the watchman could
   all answer to the same name, and let no one be simply in the right.

5. **The warehouse in the dark.** An old Rud, a militiaman past seventy, quietly
   wrecks a warehouse's stock at night, and *a Rud has already put his anger
   into a warehouse* is the stub's own fact. Do not resolve why. Is it
   grievance, grudge, politics, or just an old temper finding a wall? The family
   does not ask; the retired watchman under the same roof does not look; and the
   interesting scene is the not-asking, played across a household that has
   decided some things are better uncounted.

6. **The careless candle.** On Colm's Night a Rud lights a lawful green candle
   without the glance at the shutters a respectable house gives it, because a
   Rud has nothing a candle could cost — and Colm's Night is a night the grey
   coats are about, and a green flame in an unshuttered poor window is easy to
   notice and hard to forget. Nothing is cited. Everyone is noticed anyway. Play
   the ordinary carelessness of people too poor to be careful, and the quiet
   cost of being easy to note.

7. **The basket to the gaol.** A Rud is usually held in the Stone House for
   something — a housemaid this winter — and the pot carries in food and
   bedding, which is lawful and ordinary. But the gaol gate is a public place,
   and being seen going in is its own small thing to manage. Who carries the
   basket, how openly, and whether it is a shame or just a Tuesday: the family's
   whole habit of managing embarrassment, played over an embarrassment it
   actually knows how to handle.

8. **What we owe the purse.** A quiet purse buries the family's dead, and the
   family takes it and asks nothing — but the poor know that free things have a
   price, and there is a low, unresolved unease under the gratitude about what,
   exactly, is owed for a knell nobody would name the payer of. Above the fence
   this is only the ordinary wariness of people who have learned that charity
   keeps a book; the reason a Rud is right to be wary is below it, and stays
   there.

9. **Is it worth being a Rud?** The house's central question, live for the
   young: shed the name for a trade-byname and vanish into the safer anonymous
   poor, or keep a name that anchors nothing and is written in a grey book. The
   errand children will face it; Petronel is facing it on the anvil now. It is
   never settled, because it is remade by every child born poor into the name
   and every Rud who stands close enough to a trade to be renamed by it.

## Secret tier — designers only

Everything above this line renders the Ruds as the city sees them and as they
see themselves, and the descriptive sections may be injected into any Rud's
context without harm — with one carve-out. The lede, "The second thing to get
right," and the writer-guidance asides in Tam's and the cousins' sections name
or gesture at Tam's oath so that no writer spreads the heresy across the
family; those framing passages are for authors, never for prompts. What the
ward may know is only this: Tam is, to the whole ward, an angry fuller with a
dead teacher and a loud opinion about the doors, which describes half the
Bellstand. Everything below is the true shape of the one
thing the name carries in the dark, and it belongs to **one actor only — Tam's**
— and to the designers; and two items below are not even Tam's, and are the
designers' alone. It restates canon fixed elsewhere (`../second_sun/00_canon.md`
§5, §10 #26; `../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §2–3, §7–8, §12; Tam's and
Sible Mott's own sheets; the corrected
`../second_sun/03_the_church_and_the_office.md`) and adds only the Rud-internal
shape of it. It **imports no secret from the [Vells'](family_vell.md) fenced
tier**, names no informant, and hangs the dead fuller on no one. It resolves
nothing the canon leaves open.

- **Tam Rud is sworn Unwalled, of the Breach faction** — one of the six who came
  in after the Shut Door, the angry intake, *joined against the Church rather
  than for the sun*, whose seam the cell can feel
  (`../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §2). The stub's *raw new muscle to
  the patient old head* names this seam and nothing else — the cell's
  post-Shut-Door **intake** against its **old believers**, who came for the sun
  (`../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §8); it is a contrast of the cell's
  own generations, never of the Rud, Vell, or Marle *houses*, whose ordinary
  members know nothing, and the "old head" is never pinned on a family nor its
  old believers named. And even that overstates his
  faith: **he did not come for the green sun and does not really believe in
  it.** He half-believes the Church is lying, which is true, and wholly believes
  it owes for a fuller dead of the damp and a child's arm, which it does, and
  the cell is simply the only room in Ombreval where his anger has a door to go
  through. The Breach whisper he leans to — *the building itself is the wall,
  and walls come down the way they went up* — is **talk, and only talk: no plan,
  no tools, no map of the fabric, no key, no schedule, no capability of any
  kind**, only a sentence that could hang all nineteen if the wrong ear caught
  it whole (`../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §7). Never make the Breach
  competent. Never give Tam a device or a way in. The dread this house is built
  to deliver is not a plot against the cathedral; it is **a tired man with a
  list**, angry the way wet wool is heavy, whose most dangerous possession is a
  sentence. This oath is the thing that must never leak into an ambient Rud's
  prompt or the street; above the fence, Tam is Shut-Door-angry like half the
  city, and that is all the ward may know.

- **The cousins' mutual silence — the sharpest thing the name holds.** This
  restates both cousins' sheets and stays fenced to Tam (and, in her own
  context, to Sible, whose prompt is not this document's to write). Tam **knows
  his cousin Sible Mott wears a grey Custody coat** and copies the funeral
  watch-lists, and he has **told the cell nothing** — which makes him, in his
  own words, a liar in one direction or the other every hour of every day: a
  heretic hiding a grey coat from his neighbours, or a Rud hiding his neighbours
  from a grey coat, depending on which loyalty you weigh. And from the other
  side of the wall, unknown to him, Sible has **his face on Clerk Tilman Rue's
  charnel-door watch-list** — faces, not names — and she is the one clerk who
  could write *Tam Rud* into it, and she **has not**, and the not-writing grows
  heavier in her every week. **Neither knows the other is holding back.** Tam
  does not know his name is being kept off a list by his own cousin's hand;
  Sible does not know her coat is being kept off the cell's tongue by her own
  cousin's silence. Two cousins on opposite sides of the grey wall, each alone
  in the dark protecting the other, each certain they carry it by themselves.
  That is the whole engine, and it is unbearable exactly because it is lonely:
  do **not** write the scene where either learns what the other is doing. The
  moment one of them knows, it stops being two solitary treasons and becomes a
  pact, and the design wants the solitude. Keep it fenced, keep it silent, keep
  it unresolved.

- **Tam's list** (his sheet; `../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §8). He
  keeps his own quiet tally of the neighbours' habits — who lingers, who comes
  and goes, whose comings do not add up — hunting the informant of F.428 who was
  never found. It is futile and it is dangerous, and both must stay true on the
  page: **the informant is never to be found** by Tam or anyone in the cell
  (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §3f, §10 #26), and the cell's own leadership
  forbids the hunt, holding that a hunt would finish what the arrests began — a
  forbidding the intake resents and Tam ignores in his private book. Render the
  list as a brooding, never a plot: a grieving man writing down his neighbours
  because he cannot write down the one thing he wants, which is a name he will
  never have. **Do not let the list find anything.** It is the shape of his
  anger, not a thread to pull.

- **The dead teacher, and the fuller of F.428** (designers'; not Tam's to
  resolve). The old fuller who taught Tam his letters and died of the damp in
  the Custody's keeping is the fuller of the Whisper Arrests, and he **stays
  nameless everywhere, and is not a Rud**, as canon leaves him
  (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §7). His empty place in a light is the cell's one
  monument (`../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §3) — that is cell knowledge,
  not street knowledge, and not to be surfaced as street knowledge through any
  Rud. The Gaunt Passage ghost is this fuller; the last thing the old women say
  of it — that he *listens for one voice* — brushes a true thing
  (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §10 #26) that **no NPC, document, or system may
  ever resolve.** This document adds nothing to who he was, how he died, or who
  gave the word; it leaves the man exactly as nameless and the death exactly as
  unattributed as the canon does, and it takes no step, at any tier, toward the
  informant the [Vells'](family_vell.md) fenced tier keeps.

- **The pass-name irony** (designers' alone; the family never knows, and neither
  does Tam unless a scene puts it in his hands, and even then he must not
  understand what he holds). The Ruds are precisely the paupers the cell's quiet
  purse buries, and the pass into a meeting is the newest given name chalked on
  Saint Maren's charnel door of a burial the cell's coin paid for
  (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §5). So it is all but certain that a **Rud's
  given name has served, for a season, as the key to a heretic door** — the
  family's own poor dead unknowingly turning the cell's lock — and it is
  possible that Tam has stood at the grate and given a dead neighbour's name, or
  a dead kinsman's, as the pass, without the rest of the name ever learning
  their dead had a second life as a key that washes off the lintel with the next
  rain. Keep this as canon irony, handled with the same light hand the
  [Marles](family_marle.md) keep for Nan's linen: **do not build it into a plot,
  do not resolve it, do not let the family discover it.** It is here so no later
  writer trips over it, not as a hook to pull.

- **The canon this doc must not tip.** This treatment adds **no evidence about
  the Emblem** — a cloth-poor name is at the tubs at the strong hour, the wrong
  witness entirely, and a Rud would be embarrassed to have a theory about the
  sky. The cell's glass-memory doctrine is FALSE-BUT-BELIEVED and refuted by a
  fact it will never see (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §10 #15, #10). The green
  sun's nature, the F.428 informant's identity to anyone in the cell, and the
  last Sparr page stay AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN (§3f). Whether the dead hear a name
  spoken into the green beam is never confirmed and never mocked (§3f, #29): a
  Rud may speak Corin, or the loft's dead, into the beam on Bellday, and nothing
  answers on the page.

## What this document does not settle

Deliberately open, per the canon rules and the house's own design, and forbidden
to later writers to close:

- **Whether the name survives.** The Ruds are a name in the losing, and this
  document does not declare it saved or doomed. The naming law is doing its slow
  work; Petronel may climb out of the name and Dunstan's road may take another;
  a child may be born into it next winter and hold it forty years. Leave "Rud" a
  slow fall the family is living inside, never a fate resolved on the page.

- **What "Rud" first anchored.** The family does not agree — a lost
  fulling-house, a raddle trade, or nothing at all — and the not-agreeing is the
  point: a name one stage past the Hawsers has forgotten not only its property
  but the memory of it. Do not invent a definitive origin for the name; leave
  the three histories standing and none of them proved.

- **Petronel's name.** Whether the apprentice smith becomes *Petronel Smith* —
  the name rescued by her climbing or lost by it — is not a quest to complete.
  Leave the articles unfinished and both feelings in the pot.

- **The informant, and Tam's list.** Never resolved, on pain of undoing the
  corpus's central ambiguity. The fuller stays unnamed and not a Rud; the
  informant is never anyone, to anyone; and Tam's list finds nothing, ever, on
  the page.

- **The Breach's capability.** There is none, and none may be invented — no
  plan, no tools, no map, no key, no schedule. Do not escalate the Shut Door,
  add casualties to it, or turn the Breach's talk into a plot. The dread is a
  sentence, not a scheme.

- **The cousins' silence.** Do not write the scene where Tam learns Sible is
  holding his name back, or where Sible learns Tam is holding her coat back from
  the cell. Each protecting the other alone, in the dark, unknowing, is the
  whole of it, and it dies the instant either finds out.

- **The warehouse-wrecker's reason.** Grievance, grudge, politics, or an old
  temper finding a wall — the family does not ask and neither may the page.
  Leave the old man's motive exactly as uncounted as his own household leaves
  it.

- **What the light is, and whether the dead hear it.** A Rud speaks the family's
  dead into the green beam like anyone; whether the beam hears is
  AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN, and this house — at the tubs at the strong hour — is the
  last one that should be made to settle it. Never confirm, never mock.

Somebody in this name always weeps and it is never who you expect: not the widow
at the tub, who has buried before; not Tam, who is too angry to weep where
anyone can see; not the old warehouse-wrecker, who spent his grief on a wall
years ago. It is the six-year-old made to give up her blue hair-cord to a
coffin, undone by the loss of the one thing that was hers in a burying made of
other people's charity — or it is the grey clerk two wards off, alone at her
desk with a good pen, not writing a name. The Ruds are a name the city keeps
track of and the family cannot let go of, a labour-roll the naming law has
already begun to erase and has not finished, worth nothing to hold and
everything to be found by, mending its one blue thread and beating its anger
into the cloth six days a week — and the saddest thing the name carries is not
the heresy, which is one man's, but the ordinary arithmetic that a knell costs
three days' work, so the poor are buried by a hand they dare not name, and their
dead go up on the lintel in chalk, and wash off, and are gone.
