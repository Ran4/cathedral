## The thing to get right first

**The Vells are not a settled house.** This is the mistake every writer and
every prompt-builder makes on first contact — filing them beside the Sparrs, two
centuries in one window, or the Alders, one boat and one yard held past all
reason. The roster calls the Vells a family of standing, marks them **S**, hangs
a crest at the top of this page, and the eye slides on. It is wrong, or rather it
is half-right at one end of the name and dead wrong at the other, which is worse
than being simply wrong, because it hides the one fact the family is *for*.

A fixed name in Ombreval is not a possession. It is a lease. The naming law says
so in as many words: a family name is *held, not owned; it lasts only as long as
the thing that anchored it — a boat, a yard, a warehouse, a bench of trade.* Lose
the anchor and nobody strips the name from the living; the street simply stops
giving it to the children, and inside a generation the line falls back to bynames
of gear and place like anyone's. Most houses hold their lease so quietly you
forget it is a lease at all. The Sparrs have paid theirs in the same glass for
two hundred years. You cannot see a Sparr's name moving.

You can see a Vell's. The naming handbook uses this family, by name, as its worked
example — *the Vells show the rule mid-motion: a name still on the roster of
standing, and a fallen warehouse branch whose last bearer sells pilgrim badges in
the shadow of somebody else's loft.* That is the whole house in one sentence. The
name is **rising at one end and falling at the other at the same time**, and the
point of the Vells is that you are watching it happen.

- On the **Wickmarket**, in the west, the name is being paid up. Osanne Vell holds
  the Lanthorn's candle contract in her own name, has held it thirty years, and
  a Vell basket goes into the cathedral daily and nobody looks twice. Here the
  lease is current. Here the crest means something.
- On **Maren's Green**, in the Reed Ward, the name is lapsing. The Vells had a
  loft on the water once; the roof came down in the Hammering and the loft went
  after it, and what is left is Averil Vell, a widow of fifty-nine selling
  stamped pilgrim badges from a booth, the last of the Reed branch to carry the
  name at all. She has no children. When she dies the name dies with her *on that
  side of the city*, and the Wickmarket end will not even hear of it.

Everything in this document follows from that one fact: **the Vell name is a
lease held at both ends, paid up on one side and running out on the other, and no
Vell alive can stop thinking about which end they stand on.** The wax-house is
not a family business. It is the thing that holds the name up. Ask what happens
if the contract is lost and you have asked the only question the Vells are really
afraid of, and they are afraid of it in the specific, ledger-keeping way of
people who have already watched the other half of the name come down.

The second thing to get right, smaller and just as load-bearing: **the trade is
the name and the name is the trade.** Wax, tallow, wick, lamp, and the pilgrim
badge that is a picture of the light — the Vells work every stage of *light* the
city has to sell, from rendered fat at the low end to the candles that burn on
the high altar, and the pun runs so deep it stopped being a pun generations ago.
A house that lives by making light, in the city of the impossible light, under a
Church whose every oath is sworn *by the light that does not lie.* The Vells will
tell you the trade came first and the joke was noticed later. Do not entirely
believe them.

## The wax-house, stated plainly

The Vell name of standing rests on one building and one piece of paper.

The building is the **melt-house** on the north row of the Wickmarket — a stone
ground floor with a tiled hot-work island, a wax-loft above it kept dry and cool
because wax that has sweated is wax half-spoiled, a tallow shed off the yard
because you do not render fat under the same roof you keep good wax, and a booth
on the square where the finished work is sold. The whole of it stands around a
small paved court behind the north row that the Wickmarket calls **the Melt
Yard**, after what the family rebuilt first on the burnt ground (F.407;
below). Osanne Vell was born in the loft over the melt-house and means to die
there, and between those two facts is the entire
history of the family's standing.

The paper is the **Lanthorn contract**: the Vell wax-house supplies the
cathedral's candles, and has done for thirty years, in Osanne's own name. A
cathedral is a furnace of wax. It burns the high altar, the Rose chapels, the
quire stalls, the vigils, the night offices, the funerals of the rich, the
name-days of the saints, the Concurrence when the nave is packed to the west
doors — the Lanthorn eats candles the way the Tallage eats tolls, steadily and
without gratitude, and somebody has to make them to weight and deliver them to
time or the office of the Rite comes looking. That somebody is a Vell. It is the
single largest standing candle order in Ombreval, and it is the anchor under the
name.

What "of standing" buys the Vells is exact and small. It buys the fixed surname,
which the children keep. It buys the crest — **Azure, a candle argent enflamed
Or**, a single lit candle, silver on blue, the plainest charge any house in the
city bears and the one the family is least ashamed of, because a candle is what
they are. It buys a pew of their own at the Wickmarket's parish altar, a place in
the chandlers' feast processions, the right to take apprentices under articles,
and the assumption of credit — a Vell's word at the Tallage is good for more than
a Quern's, and both families know it. It does not buy leisure, land, or safety. A
Vell works. Osanne is sixty-three and still smells of hot fat no matter how she
scrubs, and says so, and means it as a boast.

What it costs is the constant knowledge that all of it hangs on the melt-house
and the contract, and the melt-house is timber above the stone and the contract
is renewable. The Sparrs cannot lose their name without losing a two-hundred-year
window; it would take a catastrophe. The Vells could lose theirs in a bad year —
a fire in the loft, a lost order, a Concurrence delivered late, a son who could
not keep the pound — and every Vell over the age of ten knows it, because every
Vell over the age of ten has seen the Reed branch do exactly that. Standing, for
the Vells, is not a possession they rest on. It is a debt they service, quarter
by quarter, in wax.

### The pound, honoured to the grain

The most important number in the wax-house is the weight of a candle, and the
most important fact about that weight is that it is dishonest by law and the
Vells keep it honest anyway.

The **chandlers' pound runs heavy by charter — "for the wick's sake."** This is
old trade custom, sealed into the guild's charter and proved, like every other
standard, against the Tallage stone and the brass Gaudry weights at Weighday: a
chandler's pound is a few drachms over the common pound, the surplus notionally
allowed because a wick and its char are not wax and the buyer should not pay
candle-price for ash. That is the charitable reading. The other reading, which
every householder in Ombreval holds, is that the heavy pound is a licensed way
for chandlers to sell you less light than the scale says, and that a chandler
weighing "honest" is weighing to his own advantage and calling it charter.

The Vells' whole reputation is built on refusing the second reading. Osanne's
rule, drilled into Ewart and drilled by Ewart into every hand who has ever held a
dipping-rod in the Melt Yard, is that **the Vell pound is the charter pound to the
grain and not one grain over.** They take the heavy pound the charter allows —
they would be fools not to; it is lawful and the guild would resent a house that
undercut the standard — but they take *exactly* it, weighed against their own
proved beam, and a candle that comes light gets made again on the Vells' wax, not
the buyer's coin. It is why the Lanthorn's office of the Rite gives them the
contract and keeps giving it: a verger can count a Vell basket by weight and not
by trust and find it right, and a cathedral that must account for every stub to
the Custody's fabric-clerks would rather deal with a chandler who is boringly
exact than one who is cheap. Ewart weighs wax to the drachm and wicks to a thread
and takes a cold pride in it that the square mistakes for hardness. It is not
hardness. It is the family's one real capital, and he is guarding it.

> *"Honour the pound"* is the whole of the Vell catechism, and it means more than
> weight. It means: be exact where exactness is watched, because exactness is the
> only thing standing between this house and the Reed Ward. A Vell says *honour
> the pound* to a child who has told a small lie.

## Wax against tallow

Inside the one wax-house run two trades, and the line between them runs through
the family like a seam through stone.

**Tallow** is rendered animal fat — beef and mutton suet bought from the shambles
and the Weigh, boiled down in the shed off the yard, drawn into dips on rush or
tow wick. It is the light of the poor: cheap, smoky, soft in summer, guttering,
rank while it burns and ranker while it renders. The tallow shed is the worst
work in the Melt Yard and it is where apprentices start and where the family's own
origin lies (the Vells were renderers before they were chandlers; see the dead,
below). Tallow is sold by the dip and the poundweight to anyone with two sparks,
and it is most of what the wax-house actually moves, because most of Ombreval
cannot afford better.

**Wax** — beeswax, bought in the block from the honey-keepers, bleached in the
sun on the loft leads, worked into cast and dipped candles of a hundred sizes —
is the good work. It burns clean, holds its shape, keeps a still flame, and
smells faintly of honey and nothing else. It is the light of the altar, the rich
man's table, the wedding, the lying-out of a corpse that can pay for dignity. Wax
is the Lanthorn contract; wax is the crest; wax is the reason the Vells are of
standing and the renderers on Tanners' Slip are not. A pound of good white wax
candles costs many times a pound of tallow dips, and the difference is the
difference between the two ends of the family's own name.

The seam this cuts is not a quarrel exactly — nobody in the Melt Yard proposes
giving up the tallow, which pays the daily bread while the wax pays the name —
but it is a permanent, low, sorting pressure, and it sorts people.

- The hands who work the **tallow shed** are the poorest of the house's people —
  apprentices, day-labour, a cousin at the vats — and they carry the smell of the
  work on them into the square, where it marks them. You can tell a tallow-hand
  from a wax-hand at ten feet in still air.
- The **wax-loft** is the clean work, the trusted work, the work done where the
  good stock is kept, and it is done by family and by the one or two hands the
  family has decided to keep.
- **Ewart** works both and weighs both and will not let anyone forget that the
  tallow is what keeps the roof on, which is his way of defending the people who
  do it. He came up through the shed himself. He has strong opinions about
  chandlers who have forgotten the smell.
- The **badge branch** on Maren's Green is, in a sense, the tallow end of the
  name gone all the way down: Averil sells the very cheapest light the trade has,
  a stamped picture of light on a lead disc, to pilgrims who cannot afford a
  candle either. Nobody in the Melt Yard says this. Everybody in the Melt Yard knows
  it.

The city reads the seam as class, and it is class, but inside the family it reads
as *memory*: the Vells climbed from tallow to wax within living record, and the
tallow shed is where they came from, and a Vell who sneers at the shed is
sneering at his own great-grandfather. Osanne will put a wax-loft hand back in the
shed for a season for exactly that, and call it "remembering the pound."

> **"Wax or tallow?"** — asked of a person, not a candle. It means *which end of
> things are you*, and it is asked across the whole city now, not only in the
> Wickmarket; the Vells minted it and lost control of it, which they find they
> mind. Said kindly, it means *are you comfortable or are you scraping*. Said
> unkindly, it means *do you belong at this table*. A Vell asked the question
> about themselves will usually say *"tallow, and proud of the pound"* — claiming
> the low origin on purpose, because to claim it is to say you have not
> forgotten, and forgetting is the sin.

## The contract, in operation

The Lanthorn contract is the anchor, so it is worth knowing how it actually
works, bell to bell, because half the family's life is arranged around it and the
other half is arranged around resenting it.

**The count.** A cathedral does not order candles; it orders *light by the
season*, and the seasons of light are the calendar. The heavy weeks are the
Concurrence and the great feasts, when the nave burns full; the quire and the
daily offices burn steadily all year; Lent-of-the-Rose and the fasts burn lean;
and **Belwyn's Dark**, at midwinter, burns nothing at all — the one office the
Candor sings without lights, in honour of the saint who went blind, and the one
day of the year the largest candle-buyer in the city buys no candles. A wax-house
that lives by the Lanthorn learns the shape of the church's year as a shape in
its own ledger: the family's fat months are the city's bright feasts, and the
family's lean week is the week the cathedral goes dark on purpose. The Vells find
Belwyn's Dark funny in a way that takes explaining. It is the day their whole
trade is, liturgically, refused, and they keep it like a small private sabbath —
the melt cold, the shed banked, the house idle — and call it *the chandler's
rest*, and nobody outside the trade gets the joke.

**The basket.** Finished candles go from the Melt Yard to the cathedral in the
Vells' own baskets, carried by a Vell or a Vell's hand, taken in at the fabric
door and counted by a verger against the delivery-tally by weight. This is the
ordinary, dull, daily motion the whole contract comes down to: a Vell basket
goes into the Lanthorn, is counted, is emptied, comes back. It has gone in so
long and so regularly that it is furniture; the vergers know the baskets, know the
weight, know the woman and her son, and wave them through the way you wave through
a thing you have stopped seeing. *A Vell basket goes in* is a Wickmarket phrase
for a thing so trusted it is invisible, and a basket nobody looks at twice is
worth more, to the house that carries it, than the square ever stops to reckon.

**The oath.** The contract, like every contract in Ombreval, is sworn *by the
light that does not lie* — the city's universal oath, the load-bearing wall of
all civic dealing, the sentence a mason and a heretic and a chandler all say with
the same face. The Vell wax-house is bound to the cathedral's light by an oath on
light. There is no document in the family more ordinary, and none a careful
reader could find quietly stranger: an oath on the light that does not lie,
binding a house of light to the light of the Church, sworn by a chandler every
quarter without a flicker.

**The counterpart.** Osanne deals, for the contract, with the office of the
**Rite** — the Provost of the Rite commands the liturgy and therefore the
candles, and the standing arrangement passes through his clerks and the senior
vergers. In F.437 the Provost of the Rite is **Hamel Crake**, a careful old
cleric of a scattered name, and the two of them have kept the candle account
between them for years with the dry courtesy of people who have never once
disagreed about a weight. The office of the **Custody** touches the contract only
at its edges — the fabric-clerks who account for wax as they account for lead and
scaffold, the grey coats who are simply present in a cathedral the way damp is —
and the Vells deal with grey the way the whole city does: civilly, briefly, and
without volunteering anything. (On what the grey coats make of a chandler, see
*Being watched*, below.)

**The loss of it.** Ask any Vell what happens if the contract goes and you will
get a shrug and a changed subject, because it is the family's death and they do
not discuss their death. What would actually happen is mundane and total: the wax
trade would shrink to the Wickmarket's own custom and the odd private order, the
tallow would become most of the house instead of half of it, the credit at the
Tallage would tighten, the apprentices would not be renewed, and within a
generation the street would stop calling the children Vell — because the anchor
would be gone, and the name follows the anchor down. It has happened once already
to this family, on the other side of the city, and the Wickmarket branch has
watched it happen and drawn the obvious lesson and does not speak of it. The
contract is not a source of pride to the Vells. It is a source of fear that they
have agreed to call pride.

### A leaf of the wax-house ledger

*A quarter's delivery entry in the Vell book, in Ewart's hand, of the kind laid
open before Osanne each quarter-day. Innocent to the last grain — which is the
point of it.*

> **Coswaldstide quarter, F.436 — Lanthorn, office of the Rite.**
> White wax, high altar and quire, cast, iij baskets — weighed at the charter
> pound, proved on our beam — vj lantern, x bell.
> White wax, Rose chapels and the night offices, dipped — ij lantern, iiij bell.
> Paschal-work and the great tapers, to the Concurrence, part-delivered — held to
> account, weight noted at door.
> Tallow, fabric and the works, common dips, for the scaffold-men and the
> counting-rooms — xviij bell.
> Wick and tow, own drawing, to a thread.
> *Received and counted at the fabric door by weight, verger's tally agreeing.*
> *Sworn by the light that does not lie.*
>
> *(At the foot, in a heavier, older hand:)* **Osanne Vell, mistress, her mark.**

There is nothing in the leaf but candles, and that is exactly what a leaf of this
book should contain, and the fact that Ewart lays it open every quarter and his
mother signs it and says nothing about whose name stands at the top is the whole
grief of the household in a line of accounts. He thinks the silence is about him.

## The households

The Vell name is a lease held at both ends, and each Vell of consequence holds a
different answer to the same question — *what is the name worth holding, and who
should hold it?* The answers are livable and chronic. Nobody in this family is
about to do anything drastic; that is not the register. They are simply, each of
them, standing at a slightly different distance from the wax that holds the name
up, and the distances do not change, and everyone can feel them.

### Osanne Vell — the anchor

Osanne (63) is mistress of the wax-house, holder of the contract, and the still
point the whole family turns on. Read her sheet and the first word is *motherly*:
she feeds people, she lets a silence sit, she asks after your children by name and
remembers the answers, she is unhurried in a trade that runs on time. The second
word, underneath, is *iron*. She has run the melt-house alone since the fire took
her husband thirty years ago, raised a son to master through it, held the largest
candle account in the city through a diversion's aftermath and a Hammering and a
Long Departure, and buried more of her own than she will name at one sitting. The
warmth is real and the iron is real and the warmth is the older of the two.

Her failing eyes are the fact everyone in the Melt Yard works around and nobody
mentions. She can no longer read a fine wick or a far face; she weighs by the
feel of the beam now, judges wax by smell and thumb, and knows the melt-house by
sixty years of walking it in the dark. She hides it well and hates that she must.
For a chandler — a woman whose whole trade is other people's sight — going blind
is a particular cruelty, and she meets it the way she meets everything, by
refusing to discuss it and quietly rearranging the world so the discussion never
comes up.

Her answer to the family's question is the plainest and the hardest: **hold the
name, and sign nothing away.** The contract stays in her name while she lives.
She lets Ewart run the floor, keep the books, weigh the wax, take the credit of
the trade and the blame of it — she has made him master in every particular but
the one — and every quarter she signs the leaf he lays open and says nothing.
The square reads this as an old woman who will not let go, a hard mistress keeping
a grown son a boy, and the reading is cruel and, on the evidence the square has,
fair. Ewart reads it that way himself, on his worse days. She has never explained it —
not to the square, not to her son, not once — so the explanations are all other
people's: pride, control, a widow's grip on the one thing that was ever hers in
her own name. Any of them will serve, and she lets them serve, and adds nothing.
What she is, on the surface and as far down as the Wickmarket can see, is a warm,
iron, half-blind old chandler who guards her contract past all reason and feeds
everyone who comes to her door.

Her sayings are famous in the Wickmarket and injectable on their own:

> **"Be a candle."** Her whole philosophy, offered to the frightened, the grieving,
> the young about to do something rash. *A candle doesn't argue with the dark. It
> just keeps burning. Be a candle.* She has said it so often it is nearly the
> family motto; children of the square can finish it. It is warm, and it is true,
> and she offers it most readily to people who are about to break, which is
> perhaps why it never sounds like a platitude in her mouth.

> **"I have neighbours enough to see for me."** Said of her eyes, lightly, to turn
> the pity — the Wickmarket is a close square, and an old chandler-woman has
> indeed spent sixty years knowing everyone in it. She leans on the word
> *neighbours* the way a person leans on a good stick.

### Ewart Vell — the heir who runs everything but the name

Ewart (38, master chandler) is the best hand in the Wickmarket and the second
name in his own house, and he lives in the gap between those two facts like a man
living in a draught. He runs the melt-house floor, keeps the books, weighs to the
drachm, cuts wick to a thread, honours the charter pound to the grain, and makes
every candle the Lanthorn burns — and the contract that pays for all of it stands
in his mother's name, and every quarter he lays the ledger open in front of her
and she signs it and says nothing about the name at the top, and he has stopped
asking and never stopped minding.

He talks the way he keeps books: short, exact, cold enough that the square takes
him for a hard man. He is not a hard man. He is a man with one wound and good
manners about it. The proof is the thing he hides: **in the week before Wet Alms
he goes out after the Snuffing with a full basket to the lodging-houses off the
Needle and leaves light at the doors of neighbours who cannot afford it, and tells
nobody which neighbours, not even his own house.** He gives away more candles at
the poor door than the ledger he guards so exactly will ever balance, and he
keeps that unbalanced with the same care he keeps everything else, because the
charity is his and the ledger is hers and the one thing that is purely his own is
the giving he does in the dark. It is the tallow-shed boy still in him: he came up
through the worst work in the house and he knows exactly who in the lodging-houses
is choosing between bread and light, and he will not let them choose.

His answer to the family's question is **the name is an inheritance, and it is
owed to me.** He does not want to sell anything or change anything or leave; he
wants his own name at the top of the leaf before he is forty, and to be something
in the square other than *Osanne Vell's boy*. It is not greed. It is a
craftsman's plain sense of desert: he does the work, he should hold the paper.
That his mother will not give it to him he takes as a judgment on his fitness,
and it corrodes him quietly, and nothing in the way she does it corrects the
reading, because she offers no reading of her own. **Whether there is more behind
her refusal than the square can see, and whether Ewart suspects it, this document
does not decide and forbids you to decide: he does not ask where she goes on
quarter-feast nights, he does not ask why the name stays at the top of the leaf,
and the not-asking is canon — the only kindness his sourness has ever done his
mother.** Leave it exactly there.

His second wound is his son. Tobin took a lamplighter's ladder instead of the
vat, and Ewart has not forgiven him — and walks the west lamps most nights to see
the boy trimmed them properly, which is the whole of Ewart in one habit: the
refusal on the surface, the care underneath, and no words connecting the two.

### Clemence Skep — honey to his wax

Ewart's wife is **Clemence Skep** (36, a honey-seller of the Wickmarket), and by
the naming law she keeps her own name in a house of Vells, runs her own trade
under it, and is nobody's Vell — a fact the family states plainly and the square
respects. She is Tobin's mother, and she *sees straight through him*, and she is
the one person in the Melt Yard who married in with clear eyes and no illusions
about what she married.

The match is better trade than the square first thought. Wax comes from the
honey-keepers, in the block; a wax-house that marries the honey-seller has married
its own supply, and the beeswax that becomes the Lanthorn's altar candles comes,
some of it, across Clemence's own counter. Wax and honey are one animal's two
gifts, and the Vell candle and the Skep comb come off the same hives, and the
household jokes that Ewart married the front half of his own stock. The Skeps are
a rougher, jumped-up house than the Vells — muscle and service and a crooked
assumed crest nobody granted them — and a Vell of standing marrying a Skep raised
eyebrows a generation back; but Clemence is shrewd, exact with a sum, and warmer
than her husband, and the marriage works the way a good weight works, without
drama. She keeps her honey books as tight as Ewart keeps his wax books, and
between them the two ledgers of one household balance where the family's own two
ledgers — Ewart's and Osanne's — never quite do.

### Tobin Vell — the ladder, not the vat

Tobin (16) is the third generation and the first to refuse the wax. He is
apprenticed to the lamps — a **lamplighter**, which under Ombreval's trades still
falls beneath the Wickmarket chandlers, so he has not left the family trade so
much as chosen its lowest and most public branch over its highest — and he did it
to get out of the melt-house, and he wanted bronze instead. He talks about
the Bellfounders' Yard, out east under the wall, where they sink the casting
pits, the way other boys talk about girls; he wants to be at the casting, in
the metal, and instead he carries his
father's taper-can up the western round from Lamplight to the Snuffing and
whistles the three-note wick-call at every corner so the figure on the ladder
reads as lawful.

He is a hopeless liar, goes red, answers questions nobody asked, and keeps two
secrets badly. The first is a superstition: **he leaves one lamp dark each night
at a different corner**, never the same one twice, because ill luck learns a
man's habits if you let it. The custom itself is ordinary — every lamplighter
leaves one lamp dark as *Belwyn's lamp*, the saint's due — but Tobin has made it
his own private ward against fate and moves it nightly, which is the sort of thing
a sixteen-year-old does with an inherited rite. The second secret is a girl:
**Rohese Crake**, a lamplighter of his own age who brings her mother's ladder to
the last corner of his round, which is why the last stretch takes him so long and
why he cannot say so to his father or his mother, who sees through him anyway.

His answer to the family's question is the one nobody wants to hear: **the name is
a weight I did not choose.** He is not against the Vells; he simply does not want
the melt-house, and the melt-house is the name, and to refuse the one is read as
refusing the other. His father takes the ladder as a rejection and his mother
takes it as a phase and his grandmother — who chose her own hard road young and
knows the look — takes it as a boy trying to be a candle in his own way and says
nothing, and walks a little of the western round herself now and then, on the
nights her eyes will let her, to see the lamps.

### Sible Vell — the name that was never only wax

Here is the household that breaks the tidy story, and the family keeps her exactly
for that. **Sible Vell** (79, mistress locksmith of the Fabric ward) is a Vell of
standing whose standing has nothing to do with wax. She holds a fine-metal
workshop, makes locks the householders of the Fabric trust and the Common Bench
sends for when a forced one needs reading, ranks mistress in her own right, and
has carried the name upright for sixty years on a trade the Wickmarket has almost
forgotten is theirs. She is kin to Osanne at some remove — of the same name and,
they both suppose, the same great-grandfather, back before the branches sorted
themselves into wax and river — and she is the living proof that **the Vell name
was never only the wax-house.**

She is dry, blunt, asks one hard question before she offers help, and thinks the
Wickmarket makes far too much of itself. In her view the wax branch mistakes its
contract for the family, and she has watched them treat the loss of the Reed loft
as a tragedy and the holding of the candle account as a triumph and privately
finds both a little vulgar: a name, she will tell you, is held up by *work and
rank*, by a mistress's mark on a good lock as much as on a candle account, and a
house that thinks its whole soul is in one piece of paper has already half-lost
the paper. She is not warm about it. She is right about it, which is worse, and
the wax branch knows she is right and finds her tiring, and she finds them
sentimental, and this is the family's most comfortable quarrel because nothing is
at stake in it but pride.

Keep her **mundane**. Sible makes locks, examines forced ones for the Bench, and
knows nothing of any of the things this document fences below; her workshop is a
workshop and her locks are locks and the echo between a locksmith's trade and the
family's — both of them, in the end, about what holds a door — is a resonance for
the reader and not a fact in her head. She would be insulted by the comparison.
She would also, if you brought her a forced Vell lock, find the file-marks and
tell you who made it, and be right, and charge you for the telling.

### Sibbe Vell — the poor branch, keeping her place

**Sibbe Vell** (17, oven-keeper's apprentice, Bell-and-Sluice) stands at the far
poor edge of the name where standing thins to nothing. She keeps an oven in the
central streets, is deaf in the left ear, meets strangers with guarded good
manners, and is trying only to get an employer to count her a full day's work and
wage. The wax-house is a rumour to her — a great-something's cousins in the west
who are *of standing*, which has never bought her a loaf — and she has the
sense, unusual and hard-won at seventeen, to know that not every quarrel in the
city is about the Great Rose and not every Vell is the wax-house. She remembers
agreements and faces; people in her ward come back to her for it; she means to
keep her place without pretending to a grandeur that was never hers.

Her answer to the family's question is the one the ambient mass of the name holds
in aggregate: **the name is a sound, and the sound does not feed you.** She is not
bitter about it. Bitterness is a luxury of the branch that had something to lose;
Sibbe never did, and she keeps her ear to the good side of the room and her name
to herself and gets on. She is the register in which most people who are called
Vell actually live, and the wax-house does not know she exists, and she is better
off for it.

## The dead, and the fire that made the house

A family treatment is mostly a treatment of its dead, and the Vell dead are the
argument the living are still having. The Wickmarket branch remembers three
generations and a fire, and the fire is the hinge of all of it.

**The renderers.** Before the Vells were chandlers they were renderers — boilers
of fat, drawers of tallow, keepers of a low riverside shed where the shambles'
suet and the wharf's spoiled fat became dips and grease. This is the family's
true origin and it is kept, not hidden: the Vells came up out of the tallow, and
the tallow shed in the Melt Yard is a deliberate monument to it. The oldest Vell
anyone in the Wickmarket can name is a renderer, and the family says his trade
before it says his virtues, because the point of him is that his grandchildren
sell candles to a cathedral and he boiled fat by the water.

**Aubin Vell**, Osanne's father, is the one who moved the weight. He is the riser
— the Vell who took the family's tallow up from the river to the Wickmarket,
bought the melt-house on the north row, learned to bleach and cast good wax, and
turned a rendering line into a chandlery of standing. He won the family its first
regular candle custom at the Lanthorn — small, a quire order, nothing like the
contract — and he died before it grew into anything, leaving the house to his
daughter because a daughter who can weigh is worth more than a son who cannot, and
Osanne could always weigh. Aubin is the family's proof that the name can *climb*,
and the whole Wickmarket branch's confidence is really Aubin's confidence,
inherited: he changed the family's trade and quarter in one lifetime and the name
survived the change, which is the opposite of what the Reed branch learned.

**Corin.** Osanne married a wick-drawer from the next lean-to — a man of the
trade and no standing, so the children took her name and the house stayed hers.
The ledger records his death and not his father's name, which tells you exactly
what he was: a good hand, a kind man by the little that is said, and a footnote in
a house whose name and whose weight both ran through his wife. Osanne does not
speak of him much, and when she does it is briefly and without arm's-length
grief and then a changed subject, which in her is the deepest grief there is.

**The fire — F.407.** The chronicle keeps it in one dry line: *a tallow fire at
the Wickmarket; six stalls burned, and the wax ran in the gutters, and the poor
scooped it while it was warm, and the chandlers let them.* The Vells keep the rest
of it, and it is the making of the house.

The fire started in hot fat — it always does; a rendering vat left too high, a
tallow shed on a dry night — and it took the north row, and the Vell melt-house
was among the six that burned. Corin went back in. Accounts differ on whether it
was for the vats, the stock, or a hand who had not got out, and Osanne has never
settled which because settling it would mean saying more about him than she can;
he went back in and the loft came down and that was the end of him. Osanne was
thirty-three, with a boy of eight and a burning house, and the thing she is
remembered for that night is not grief but the wax: **the good wax ran out of the
melt-house into the gutter and she let the poor of the square scoop it while it
was warm** — did not fight them for it, did not count it, let a fortune in white
wax go into the aprons of the Wickmarket's paupers because it was going to be lost
anyway and they were cold. *The chandlers let them* — she was the chandler who
let them, and the phrase in the chronicle is, in part, about her, and the poor of
the Wickmarket have not forgotten it, and it is the root of the whole family's
charity: Ewart's poor-door round in the dark before Wet Alms is the son repaying,
without knowing he is repaying, the debt his mother chose to owe on the worst
night of her life.

And out of the fire came the contract. The row was disordered; the chandler who
had held the Lanthorn's quire order was burned out and broken; the cathedral
needed a reliable house and needed it in weeks, and here was a widow rebuilding
fast, honouring the pound to the grain, delivering through the ash — and the
office of the Rite gave her the whole account, in her own name, because there was
no man to put it under and because she was, plainly, the best chandler left
standing. **The contract is thirty years old because the fire is thirty years old.
The night that took her husband gave her the name's anchor, in her own name, and
she has held it in her own name ever since**, and the square reads that as a
widow's hard pride, and the square is not wrong, only incomplete. The Melt Yard
has its name from that year too: the melt-house was what the family rebuilt
first on the burnt ground — before the dwelling, before the booth — and the name
stuck, exactly as the naming law says names grow from use and damage. Whatever
the court behind the north row was called before the fire, nobody now remembers,
which is its own kind of renaming. Every day the family walks in and out of a
court named for the worst thing that ever happened to it, and thinks nothing of
it, because that is what a name is: a wound you have agreed to answer to.

**The Hammering.** Like every house in the city the Vells count dead from F.415,
though the Wickmarket, west and away from the cloth halls, came off less murdered
than the Reed Ward and the centre. The melt-house held; the loft cracked and was
patched; the family's dead that year were kin at the edges and, above all, the
whole other half of the name, on Maren's Green, which the fire had spared and the
hail did not. The Wickmarket Vells gave candles to the parish for the belated
name-knells of the Unknelled and gave them, characteristically, at cost and off
the books, and Osanne stood the family's Hammering grief the way she stands
everything, upright and unspeaking, and it is the one wound in the house she
shares fully with the fallen branch, because the hail did not care which end of
the name you were.

## The fallen — Maren's Green

The other end of the name is a widow in a badge-booth, and she is the most
important thing in this document after the wax-house, because she is what the
wax-house is afraid of, walking around in the same surname.

**Averil Vell** (59, badge attendant, widow, Reed Ward) is the last of the Reed
branch to carry the name. She sells stamped pilgrim badges of the Emblem from a
booth near Saint Maren's — lead discs the Custody licenses and forbids anyone to
describe, the cheapest and lowest form the trade of light takes — courteous,
direct, hard to hurry, in a coat too large and belted twice, remembered by her
neighbours because she remembers agreements and faces. She is a warden of her
booth and a pauper in her purse, and she has the particular dignity of people who
have come down a long way and decided not to perform it.

**The loft.** The Reed Vells had a warehouse once — a dry-goods loft behind
Maren's Green, off the brine-damp lane of **Tanners' Slip**, where the family's
old riverside trade had gone: storing wool, hides and rendered fat carted up from
the wharves, the warehouse end of the same tallow business the Wickmarket branch
climbed out of. It was never grand — a loft, a hoist, a counting-corner, a name
over a door — but it was an anchor, and while it stood the Reed Vells were a
family of standing too, the lesser and older half of the name. It did not survive
the century.

**How it went**, dated, internal, and nobody's crime — which is the cruelty of
it:

- **F.363–369.** The diversion turned the loft dry, like every loft on the old
  water. The trade did not end but it doubled its handling — the dry carry, every
  load lifted twice, the second lift another man's — and the loft's margin thinned
  the way the whole ward's did. It limped. It limped for two generations.
- **F.415.** The Hammering brought the loft roof down. Averil's brother, **Gile
  Vell**, the heir who kept the books and would have held the name, died under the
  slate with it. Her father, old **Bertran Vell**, lived a broken year in a
  patched shell of a warehouse whose stock had spoiled where it lay.
- **F.416.** Bertran died, and the loft went. Not to an enemy and not in a
  scene — to arithmetic. The repair-shares on a Hammering roof were more than the
  spoiled stock was worth; the credit was gone; the lease lapsed and the ground
  went to whoever could pay the shoring, and the Vell name came off the door
  inside a year. Averil, widowed already — her husband **Dunstan**, a wharf-hand
  of no name to leave her, was some years dead of the damp — could not hold what
  two dead men could not, and did not try to perform a fight she could not win.
  She took the badge-booth to live, and the booth is what she has.

So Averil sells the licensed picture of light **in the shadow of somebody else's
loft** — the boatmen's document has the phrase, and it is exact and merciless and
must stay unpinned: the loft she works beneath is *someone else's*, and the
someone is nobody in particular, because the point is precisely that it does not
matter whose it is now; it was Vell and it is not, and the name that mattered is a
fact about her grandparents. She has **no children**. When she dies the Reed
Vell name is simply over; the street will not give it to anyone, because there is
nothing left for it to name. She knows this with complete clarity and mentions it
to no one and sells her badges and remembers her faces, and it is the quietest
tragedy in the family and it is not performed, which is why it lands.

**The two ends of the name.** How do the risen Wickmarket Vells and the fallen
Reed Vell treat each other? Carefully, and at a distance, and not often. There is
no feud — a feud would require the fallen branch to have a grievance with a
target, and Averil's grievance is with a roof and an arithmetic, not with her
Wickmarket kin. There is instead something worse than a feud, which is
*embarrassment on one side and pride on the other*. The wax-house sends candles
to Maren's Green at the belated name-knells and at Marenstide, correct and quiet
and a little too correct; Averil takes them, correct and quiet and a little too
proud; and both of them know that the candles are the wax branch looking at its
own possible future and flinching, and neither of them says so. Osanne, who
flinches at nothing, does not flinch at Averil, and the two old women — kin at
some remove, of the same name and, they suppose, the same loft four generations
back — have a plain regard for each other that neither branch entirely
understands, because it is the regard of two people who both know exactly what a
name costs and exactly what it is worth, from the two opposite ends of finding
out. Averil is the only person in the city Osanne will let see her count on her
fingers because her eyes have gone — an intimacy with no visible cause, which the
family has long since stopped trying to explain.

## The scatter

Between the wax-house and the badge-booth runs the rest of the name — the thin,
poor, un-anchored mass of Vells that a fixed surname sheds as it moves, and that
the roster shows scattered through the working trades of half the city. This is
the name gone to a sound. It clusters, as nearly every ambient name does, in the
**Bell-and-Sluice** streets, the crowded central mixing-bowl where the city's
overflow settles: the Vell name there runs to a court usher, a measure clerk, a
wall guard, a privy cleaner, and a pauper who begs a pitch and sells what she
can — a whole small ledger of poor Vells who have never seen the melt-house and
would get nothing at its door if they went. It runs a seamstress and a master
glazier into **Cinder Row** among the furnaces; a porter — a newcomer, measuring
the local speech before he trusts it — into the **Wallwright** streets by the
wall; a day-labourer into the **Reed** Ward near the fallen branch, though
whether he is kin to it or merely shares the sound, nobody has troubled to work
out. Keep every one of them role-level and swappable: the name hangs no biography
on them, and that weightlessness is the whole point of them.

What the name *means* to a poor Vell is worth stating, because it is the aggregate
answer to the family's central question and it is not what the wax-house imagines.
To a Vell in the Bell-and-Sluice streets the surname is, at best, a story about a
great-aunt with a warehouse or a great-something with a candle contract in the
west — a thing that proves the family was *someone* once, produced on occasion for
a scrap of dignity and worth exactly that scrap and no bread. Most of them do not
produce it. A fixed name with no anchor under it is a slightly awkward thing to
carry among the truly nameless poor, who wonder, not always kindly, what a
labourer is doing with a surname of standing; it can read as a pretension the
bearer cannot back. So the poor Vells mostly let it lie, answer to their given
names, and are Vells only on the rare day it is useful, which is the naming law
doing its slow work in real time — the sound outliving the thing by a generation
or two before it fades. There is no drama in it. There is just a name spreading
thin over people who have troubles of their own and no melt-house to inherit, and
the occasional Vell among them who still says the surname with a straight back, and
gets, for it, a straight back's worth.

**A master glazier on Cinder Row** deserves one line, because the family has an
opinion about him even though he is nobody to them. There is a Vell at a furnace
on the Row — a widower, a master of his craft — and the wax-house, when it thinks
of him at all, thinks the way the whole city thinks about Cinder Row: that it is
the hottest, oddest, most watched ward in Ombreval, that the glaziers keep to
themselves and their guild and their venerated dead, and that a Vell firing glass
among the Sparrs is a Vell the family lost track of a long way back. What the
Wickmarket thinks about the Row is texture and no more; the glazier is unnamed
here on purpose and stays swappable, and the family's view of the glass quarter is
a working household's ordinary mixture of respect, superstition, and relief that
the furnaces are somebody else's problem.

## The trades of light

Stand the whole name in a row, top to bottom, and you notice the thing the family
pretends not to: **every Vell works light.** Not most; every branch of the name
that works at all works some stage of the one trade, and the trade is the making
and tending and selling of light, and the name and the trade have been the same
asset for so long that the family can no longer say which held the other up.

- **Wax** — the altar, the rich table, the good death. Osanne and Ewart. The high
  end. The contract. The name of standing.
- **Tallow** — the poor man's dip, the daily light of most of the city. The shed
  in the Melt Yard, the apprentices, the origin. The low end that pays the bread.
- **Wick** — drawn in the house, to a thread, the small exact craft inside the
  large one; a candle is only as good as the thing that burns down its middle,
  and a Vell will tell you a badly wicked candle is a lie you light.
- **Lamp** — the public light of the streets, tended by lamplighters who fall
  under the Wickmarket chandlers; Tobin on the western round, and Rohese Crake
  beside him, carrying the city's lit hours up a ladder from Lamplight to the
  Snuffing.
- **The badge** — light reduced to a stamped picture of itself, sold to pilgrims
  who cannot afford a candle; Averil on Maren's Green, the lowest rung the trade
  of light has, and still, unmistakably, the trade of light.

The city's lit hours are, in a real sense, a Vell product. When the office of
Lamplight rings and the shutters close and the public lamps come up along the
five squares, that is chandlers' work being tended; when a household lights a dip
against the dark, most likely it is a tallow dip off somebody's Wickmarket
counter; when the high altar burns at the Concurrence with the nave packed to the
doors, that is Vell wax. The family that makes the light does not often stop to
find this remarkable, because to the people inside a trade the trade is just the
work. But it is why the name has the weight it has, and it is why losing the trade
would be losing the name and not merely the income: a Vell who does not work light
is a person the street has no particular reason to keep calling Vell.

There is one more thing to say, and the Vells do not say it, because saying it is
neither their habit nor their interest: a house that lives by light, in the one
city where light itself is in question, lives very close to the thing the whole
city is careful about. The Vells make the
lawful light and sell the picture of the questioned one and tend the lamps that
push the dark back nightly, and they do it in the shadow of a cathedral whose
whole doctrine is that there is only one light and it does not lie. None of that
is a secret and all of it is a pressure, and the family absorbs the pressure the
way it absorbs everything, by being exact, being useful, and changing the subject.

## Being watched

Every citizen of Ombreval lives under the Custody's grey eye a little; the
Wickmarket lives under it more than most, and the Vells more than the Wickmarket,
for the simple reason that they supply the cathedral. A house whose baskets go
into the Lanthorn daily is a house the grey coats know by name, and this section
renders that — the Custody as the wax-house experiences it, from below, all of it
ordinary, all of it public, none of it the fenced material at the foot of this
document.

**The grey coats in the square.** The Custody's clerks — *the grey coats,* whom
the street does not greet — are simply present in a cathedral quarter the way
damp is present in a cellar. They order the pilgrim hours, license the glass, keep
the Great Rose's fabric, stamp the badges, and note things. A Wickmarket
chandler deals with them civilly, briefly, and without volunteering, because that
is how the whole city deals with grey and because a house with a contract to
protect volunteers nothing to anyone. The Vells have no quarrel with the Custody
and want none; the Custody is a fact of the weather, and the family's whole
posture toward it is the posture of people who have something to lose and intend
to keep it: correct, quiet, dull on purpose.

**The metered light.** What the Custody actually does with the Emblem is *meter*
it — the licensed viewing hours, the sightlines kept open for paying pilgrims,
the badges sold at the wicket — and the Vells sit at the paying end of that trade
without ever being asked their opinion of the thing itself. Above the rank of
wick-priest nobody wants opinions; below it nobody's opinion is safe to give. The
Edict of F.244 makes it a citable offense to call the Emblem a sun, a second sun,
or the green sun; the Custody Doctrine absolves the sight and punishes only the
saying — *to see is no sin; to affirm is* — and a chandler learns young to say
*the Emblem*, agree with the priest, and change the subject. Most Vells, like
most working people, have seen the thing once or twice in a lifetime and no more;
a wax-house at the strong hour is at its vats, not in the nave.

**The green candle.** Here the family sits closest to the grey eye, and does it in
the open. The Vell wax-house dips and sells the **green-dipped candles of Colm's
Night** — the spring candles burned at home for the drowned labourer Colm
Attergate — and Osanne sells them off her own stall, and says the wax line
herself, deliberately mundane: *it is a dye and a season and a poor drowned man,
neighbour, nothing in it but tallow and a little verdigris, buy two.* The candle
is lawful to make and lawful to sell and lawful to burn; only affirming the
forbidden thing is the offense, and a green candle affirms nothing. So the Vells
sell them, and a grey coat stands on the sluice road on Colm's Night being seen,
and notes who buys, and the whole city finds the arrangement slightly funny and
slightly cold, and buys the candles anyway. That the grey coats keep a tally of
green-candle buyers is common knowledge, and the Wickmarket lives with it the way
it lives with all the grey coats' counting: by noticing, saying nothing, and
buying the candles anyway.

**The clerk at the funerals.** Everyone on the poor side of the city knows that a
grey clerk attends the paupers' burials at Saint Maren's now — presently a mild
man who watches the mourners instead of the grave — standing at the back of the
churchyard, greeting no one, noting who lingers at the charnel door. It is not
a secret; it is a presence, like the grey coat on the sluice road, meant to be
felt. The fallen
Vells on Maren's Green live beside it and think nothing of it, the way you think
nothing of a thing that is always there. The wax-house, west and away, hears of it
the way it hears of all Reed Ward news, at second hand and without much interest.

**The folded letter.** When the Custody wants a word with a citizen about the
tongue, it sends a **grey letter, folded but unsealed, so any neighbour may read
it on the way** — the mundanity is the method, and the neighbours' knowing is half
the fine. No Vell has ever had one; the family's whole art is to be the house that
never gives the grey coats a reason to fold a letter. This is not innocence
exactly. It is discipline, bred in by three generations of having more to lose
than a poorer name would, and it looks, from outside, exactly like having nothing
to hide.

## The year

The Candor's calendar is the wax-house's order book, so the Vells live the year
twice — once as feasts, and once as candle-weight — and the two readings do not
always agree about which day matters.

**Highmarket**, third of the week, is the Wickmarket's market day and the
family's steady pulse: the stall on the north row open, the dips and tapers out,
Osanne feeling the beam, the honey and wax and rendered fat of the whole square on
the air. It is not a feast; it is the ground under the feasts, and a Vell measures
an ordinary week by it.

**Colm's Night** (early spring) is the family's oddest working day — the green
candles, the grey coat on the sluice road, the small lawful trade in a thing the
city half-fears — handled above in *Being watched*. To the Vells it is a season's
line in the ledger and a plain man drowned; they dip the candles, sell them, and
keep their faces mild, which is the correct face for Colm's Night and the correct
face for most of what the Vells do.

**Vhairestide** (spring, the year's turn) is the city's, not especially the
family's, though the wax-house feels it as the Reed Ward walking out through the
postern to bless the boats, which is the fallen branch's country and the
Wickmarket's only once-a-year thought of it.

**Perrinstide** (late spring) — bells washed and greased, alms for the deaf — is
Sibbe's, quietly, if it is any Vell's: the oven-keeper deaf in one ear, at the
one feast that remembers people like her. It is also the one day Tobin is
lawfully near the trade he wants: Saint Perrin Halfbell is the founders' saint,
and a founder will suffer a boy's questions on Perrinstide who would chase him
off the yard any other day. Ewart lets it happen, once a year, and says nothing
when the boy comes home talking of bell-metal.

**Marenstide** (late summer) is the fallen branch's feast and the family's shared
wound. The roll of the drowned is read at Saint Maren's and disputed — *you cannot
drown under slate,* the traditional voices say of the Hammering dead added to it —
and Averil is there, and the Wickmarket sends candles, correct and quiet, and the
two ends of the name stand in the one churchyard and do not say any of the things
they both know. The eel fair fills the Green; the Dry Race runs the dry Cut; and a
Vell of the west, if one comes at all, comes for the candles and the kin and goes
home before the drink.

**The Hammering anniversary** (late summer) is a wound for every house and a
double one for this name, which lost the Reed loft's people under the slate. The
common toll sounds at the Waning; the Vells answer it the way the traditional
households do — *one bell is not a name* — and the wax-house gives candles at cost
for the belated name-knells of the Unknelled, off the books, because it is the one
grief the risen branch and the fallen branch own together and neither will charge
the other for it.

**Coswaldstide** (autumn) is the masons' day — the scaffold-blessing, the
yearly brawl over the Glaziers' Trial — and no particular temptation to anyone
in the wax-house; Tobin's feast is Perrinstide (above), and Ewart is quietly
grateful the founders' saint falls in spring, half a year from the Concurrence
rush. The family sells its candles and takes stock, because forty days on
stands the Concurrence, which is the year's whole reckoning.

**The Concurrence** (fortieth day after Coswaldstide) is the wax-house's heaviest
delivery and the city's greatest feast: the nave packed to the west doors, the
great tapers burning, every doubled shadow fusing in the eye of the Rose if the
sky is clear and the whole feast failing if it clouds. For the Vells it is
candles, and candles, and candles — the year's largest single burning, weighed
and delivered to the office of the Rite against the day — and it is also, since
last year's **Shut Door**, a dread: the barred nave, the crush on the Gradine, a
child's arm broken, and the question of the doors unanswered again this year with
the tavern fury still hot. A chandler who has spent a season making the light for
a feast does not want the doors barred on it, and the Vells, who have no vote in
it, watch the sky like everyone else.

**Wet Alms** (winter) is Ewart's feast, though he would deny it has anything to do
with him. Bread at the nave door for the city's poor; and in the dark week before
it, the son of the house out after the Snuffing with a full basket, leaving light
at the doors of neighbours who cannot afford it, telling no one which. The family
gives at the poor door all year, but Wet Alms is when the giving has a name, and
Ewart makes sure his own name is nowhere near it.

**Belwyn's Dark** (midwinter) is the chandler's joke: the one office the Candor
sings without lights, the one day the largest candle-buyer in the city buys none,
and so the one day the Vell melt cools and the shed banks and the house sits idle
by liturgical decree. They call it *the chandler's rest* and take it as one, and
find the whole thing funnier than anyone outside the trade ever will — a wax-house
given a holiday by the feast of darkness.

**Gaudry's Audit / Weighday** (late winter) is where the family's pride is put on
the stone. The standards are proved against the Tallage stone and the sealed
Gaudry weights, the chandlers' heavy pound among them, and a Vell is always there
to see the pound proved and to have the house's own beam checked against it,
because *honour the pound* is not a boast a Vell will let go untested. It is the
one civic day the family attends not as chandlers but as citizens with a stake in
an honest weight, and they attend it seriously, and come home satisfied or come
home to re-true a beam, and either way say little about it.

## How they talk

A Vell can be marked in one line, and the line is almost always about weight,
light, or the name — the three things the family cannot stop measuring. They talk
like people who keep books and dip candles: exact, understated, warm at the
edges and cold in the middle, with a trade-metaphor for every human situation
because the trade is the only frame they entirely trust. The idiom below is direct
prompt fodder; each is injectable on its own, and the good ones cut two ways.

- **"Honour the pound."** The family's whole catechism, said to a child who has
  told a small lie, a hand who has weighed short, a kinsman about to cut a
  corner. It means *be exact where exactness is watched*, and underneath it means
  *exactness is the only thing between us and the Reed Ward.* Said as
  instruction, and as a warning, and once in a while as an accusation, depending
  entirely on the weight the speaker puts on *honour.*
- **"Wax or tallow?"** Asked of a person: *which end of things are you — altar or
  gutter, comfortable or scraping, of this table or not.* The Vells minted it and
  the city took it; the Vells mind that it got away from them. A Vell asked it of
  himself says *"tallow, and proud of the pound,"* claiming the low origin,
  because to claim it is to prove you have not forgotten it.
- **"Whose name's at the top?"** In the mouth of the city, a plain question about
  who really holds a thing — the contract, the lease, the say. In the mouth of a
  Vell it is Ewart's wound and everyone in the Melt Yard knows it, so it is not said
  lightly in the house, and when it is said in the house it is a blow.
- **"A Vell basket goes in."** Of a thing so trusted it has stopped being looked
  at. Said with pride by the Vells and with a shrug of *lucky them* by everyone
  else; a phrase about reputation, and about how much a plain, dull,
  never-questioned thing is quietly worth.
- **"Green-dipped."** Of a thing that looks like one thing and is understood to be
  another — lawful on its face, freighted underneath, sold without a word said
  about what it means. From the Colm's Night candles. *He gave a green-dipped
  answer* is a Wickmarket way of saying a man told you the truth and told you
  nothing.
- **"A badly wicked candle is a lie you light."** Of anything sound to look at and
  rotten at the core — a marriage, a contract, a man. The wick is the middle you
  cannot see until it burns; a Vell trusts the middle of things least, and says so
  in candles.
- **"Weighed to the grain"** / **"not a grain over."** Praise for honesty and, in
  the wrong tone, a complaint about a pedant. A Vell means it as the first; the
  square sometimes hears the second; the Vell does not care, which is itself very
  Vell.
- **"Off the books."** Of a kindness done unrecorded — the poor-door candles, the
  Hammering knells given at cost. In this family a good deed is *off the books* by
  definition; a charity that balanced would not be charity, and a Vell who let his
  left hand tell his right would have missed the point of being a Vell.
- **"It went to the gutter."** Of a loss let go on purpose — a thing you could
  have fought for and chose to let the poor have, or the flood take, because
  fighting for it was beneath the moment. From the wax that ran in F.407. Said
  without regret, which is the point: some things you let go to the gutter, and a
  Vell knows which.
- **"Born to the loft, born to the shed."** Which end you started — the clean wax-
  loft or the reeking tallow shed. Nobody in the family was born to the loft; they
  all say so; it is the family's way of keeping level with itself.
- **"The chandler's rest."** Belwyn's Dark, the idle day; by extension any forced
  holiday you decide to enjoy rather than resent. *Take it as the chandler's rest*
  is Vell for *the thing you cannot change, sit down inside it.*
- **"Trim it."** From the lamps — to do a thing properly, quietly, and get down
  the ladder without being seen to. The lamplighter's virtue and, in the family,
  a compliment: *he trimmed it* means a hard thing done well and without noise.
- **"In the shadow of somebody else's loft."** The fallen branch, and by extension
  any come-down worn with dignity: to work small where your people once worked
  large, and not to perform the difference. The whole city can use it now; the
  Vells try not to, because in this family it has an address.

Two blessings, both said over light, both older than anyone can source. The
city's own — *one shadow to your road,* answered *and one light over it* — is said
at doors and moorings everywhere and *not* inside the Lanthorn; the Vells say it
like everyone, but a wax-house says it with the small extra weight of people who
made the light in question. And the family's own, said over a candle newly lit at
a table or a bedside, half-grace and half-charm:

> **"One light, and honest."** — The Vell grace. *Una lux* is the Church's creed
> and *honest* is the family's amendment to it, and a Vell says the two words
> together without hearing any tension in them at all, which is either the most
> innocent thing in this document or the least, and the family has never once
> stopped to ask which.

What one line reveals: *honour the pound* marks a speaker trained in the Melt Yard;
*wax or tallow?* marks someone thinking in the Vells' own class-terms even if they
have never met a Vell; *whose name's at the top?* in a family mouth is a wound
being pressed; and a mild, exact, subject-changing warmth over anything to do with
the Emblem marks, precisely, a Wickmarket chandler — which is the most ordinary
thing in the world, and which the family wears like the cover it does not know it
is wearing.

## Funerals and rites

The house that makes the light for every rich man's lying-out and every altar in
the city has to bury its own, and how it does it is the family's most private
custom, because it is the one time the Vells burn the good work on themselves.

**The whole taper.** At a Vell death the house takes one of its own best white wax
candles — not a dip, not a tallow light, the good work, the altar grade — and
burns it entire beside the body, from the lying-out to the carrying-out, never
snuffed, and lets it go all the way down. *A whole candle for a whole life.* The
family that sells light by the weighed pound spends one candle unweighed and
unpriced on each of its own dead, and it is the only wax in the year that leaves
the Melt Yard without a figure beside it in the book. The stub, if a stub is left,
is kept. Osanne kept Corin's — burned beside him at the lying-out, after they had
brought him out of the burnt loft — thirty years on, in a box in the wax-loft, and
has never said so to anyone, and it is the one thing in the melt-house she could
find blind.

**The cold melt.** On the day of a house death the melt goes cold and the shed
banks — no rendering, no dipping, the fires out — exactly as at Belwyn's Dark, so
that the wax-house keeps a private day of darkness for its own the way the
cathedral keeps one for its saint. A neighbour who passes the Melt Yard on a
working day and finds the melt cold and no smoke off the shed knows, without being
told, that a Vell has died, and knows not to knock.

**The name-knell, and the last of a name.** The Vells bury at their parishes with
the city's ordinary rites — the risen branch at the Wickmarket's altar, the fallen
branch at Saint Maren's, where the sexton chalks the newest name on the charnel
lintel and Maren Smallvoice rings the name-knell, one slow stroke for every year
of the life, and everyone in hearing counts. For most families a name-knell is a
grief and a comfort both. For the Reed Vells it will one day be something sharper:
when Averil dies, the knell will count out her years and the sexton will chalk
*Averil* on the lintel until the next burial rubs it away for the next name, and
that will be the last time the given name of a Vell of standing is chalked on
Maren's Green, because there is no Reed Vell after her to bury or to be buried by.
The name-knell for the last of a branch counts a life and tolls a name out of a
quarter in the same strokes, and the only people who will hear both meanings in it
are the ones who know what the lintel is worth — which, on Maren's Green, is more
people than you would think, and none of them will say so.

**The green candle and the dead.** The city says you may speak your dead's name in
the green beam and they will hear you; the Church absolves the sight and forbids
the saying; the folk keep the practice and the doctrine will neither bless it nor
break it. The Vells make the green candles and sell them and, for their own dead,
do — what? Something mundane, and kept private, and never explained: a green
candle among the white at a Vell lying-out is a thing you might see in the Melt Yard
and might not, and if you asked, you would be told it is a dye and a season and a
poor drowned man, neighbour, nothing in it. Whether a Vell speaks a name into any
light, and whether anything hears, this document does not say, because the canon
does not say, and a family treatment does not get to answer what the city itself
is forbidden to answer. The candle burns down. The name is said or not said. The
melt is cold that day either way.

## Quarrels worth playing

Every one of these is live in F.437 and none has a right answer. They are written
for designers; all of them sit above the fence and can be handed to the Vells
themselves.

1. **The contract.** Whose name stands at the top of the leaf. Ewart does the work
   and wants the paper; Osanne signs and says nothing; the square calls it a
   widow's grip and Ewart, on bad days, calls it a judgment on himself. There is
   no scene coming and no resolution available: it is a chronic ache the whole
   household arranges itself around, and the moment it *is* resolved the family is
   a different family. Play the ache, not the answer.
2. **Wax against tallow.** Chase the high trade — grow the wax, feed the contract,
   let the tallow shrink toward the name and the altar — or keep the tallow strong
   because it pays the daily bread and it is who the Vells are. Ewart defends the
   shed and the hands who work it; the market pulls the other way; Osanne lets the
   argument run because a house that settled it would lose either its income or
   its soul, and she is not sure the family could tell you which was which.
3. **Tobin at the ladder.** The heir who took the lamps and wants the bronze. The
   trade needs him back at the vats by the Kindling; the boy needs to be a
   founder's apprentice at the Bellfounders' Yard; and behind both stands the
   real question nobody says — *who takes the wax-house when Ewart is old, if
   not Tobin?* — because there is no second son. Ewart plays it as discipline, Osanne
   plays it as patience, and the succession sits under the whole thing unspoken.
4. **The pound to the grain.** The family honours the charter pound exactly and
   takes not a grain over, and it costs them margin every single day against every
   chandler who quietly weighs to advantage and calls it charter — the Querns two
   ovens down certainly do. Is the Vell exactness their capital or their vanity?
   It has kept the contract for thirty years, which argues capital; it has also
   kept the family poorer than it needs to be, which argues something else. Never
   settle whether honesty is strategy or virtue in this house; it is visibly both
   and the Vells cannot separate them either.
5. **What the risen owe the fallen.** The Wickmarket sends candles to Maren's
   Green at the belated knells and at Marenstide, correct and quiet. Is that
   charity or condescension? Should Osanne do more for Averil — and would more be a
   kindness or an insult to a woman whose whole remaining dignity is that she asks
   for nothing? Averil would refuse a rescue and the wax-house knows it, so the
   help stays candle-shaped and careful and slightly cold, and everyone involved
   can feel exactly how careful it is.
6. **The green candles.** Colm's Night is lawful trade and honest coin and a poor
   drowned man's due, and it is also the one day of the year a grey coat stands on
   the sluice road counting who buys — and the Vells sell them off the family
   stall, in the open, every spring. Keep selling, as Osanne does, on the ground
   that a lawful candle affirms nothing? Or is the house drawing an eye a house
   with a contract to protect should never draw? A cautious Vell would stop. Osanne
   does not, and her reason, as she gives it, is simply that the trade is lawful,
   the man is dead, and the poor want their light.
7. **The Skep marriage.** Ewart married the honey — good trade, a supply secured,
   a shrewd wife — but he married it out of a rougher, jumped-up house than his
   own, and the alliance ties the wax to one comb-and-hive interest and keeps the
   Vells in Skep company. Was it sense or was it a leash? Clemence is the best
   thing in it and the whole question embarrasses her, which is its own reason to
   raise it carefully or not at all.
8. **Sible and the soul of the name.** The Fabric locksmith says the wax branch
   mistakes its contract for its name and has half-lost the name already by
   thinking so; the wax branch finds her sour and a little vulgar for saying it. Who
   is right about what actually holds a Vell up — a workshop and a mistress's mark,
   or a paper and a pitch? Nothing is at stake in the quarrel but pride, which is
   why it is the family's most comfortable one, and why it never ends.

## Secret tier — designers only; never inject into any rank-and-file Vell's context

Everything above this line renders the Vells as the city sees them and as they
see themselves, and any of it may be injected into any Vell's context without
harm. Everything below is the true shape of the family's centre and belongs to
**one actor only — Osanne's** — and to the designers. It must never reach the
prompt of Ewart, Tobin, Clemence, Sibbe, Sible, Averil, or any ambient Vell. This
section restates canon fixed elsewhere (00_canon.md §7, §10; 02_the_heretic_cell.md
§12) and adds only the Vell-internal shape of it; it resolves nothing the canon
leaves open.

- **Osanne Vell is the Tracer** — the hidden head of the Unwalled, the heretic
  cell of about nineteen. She holds the whole tracery of it in her head: the
  lights and the leads, the pass rule, the counter-sign rotation, the chalk signs
  at the Needle. Her identity is hidden even from most sworn members; she is
  counted by no one, which is why the cell's tally is always *about* nineteen.
  Motherly, unhurried, iron-cored — and the horror the design intends is that none
  of that is a mask: the warm chandler-woman and the Tracer are the same woman all
  the way down, and there is no seam to find.
- **Her failing eyes** are the private terror under the public one. She can no
  longer make out the Emblem through the Rose at all, and she leads a cell whose
  entire faith is a matter of *seeing* — on the strength of other people's sight.
  *"I have neighbours enough to see for me"* is the literal truth of her
  leadership, said aloud in the square as a joke about old age. *"Be a candle"* is
  the doctrine she leads by. Neither is a slip; both are hidden in plain view,
  which is where she hides everything.
- **The contract is the cell's front door, and that is why she will never sign it
  away.** A Vell basket goes into the Lanthorn daily and no verger looks twice: the
  thirty-year candle contract in her own name is unquestioned access to the one
  building that matters, held by the one person who most needs it unquestioned.
  Above the fence her refusal to sign the contract to Ewart reads as pride or
  control or a widow's grip; the truth is protection. **Ewart holding the contract
  would be Ewart carrying the cover — and Ewart, who knows nothing, exposed to
  everything.** She keeps the name at the top of the leaf to keep her son off it.
  Her son experiences this as a wound and a judgment. She lets him, because the
  wound is survivable and the truth is not.
- **Osanne was the F.428 informant.** One anonymous tip to the Custody removed a
  Breach hothead who was going to get all nineteen hanged; the man was taken, and
  a fuller died in custody of the damp instead of the whole cell dying on the
  Gradine. The fuller's death is hers to carry. It is known to no one living but
  her. Her leadership since is the penance she set herself, and the cell's angry
  post-Shut-Door intake, who want the old informant hunted, are asking — without
  knowing it — for their own Tracer's head, and she forbids the hunt, and the
  forbidding is remembered and weighed by one or two. **Do not resolve the
  informant's identity to anyone in the cell, ever** (00_canon.md §3f).
- **The green-candle tally.** On Colm's Night she sells the green-dipped candles in
  the open and then **pads the Custody's tally of who bought them** — softens the
  list, salts it, protects the buyers — using exactly the trusted, dull, invisible
  standing that a thirty-year contract-holder has with the grey coats. The lawful
  trade above the fence and the padded list below it are the same act seen from two
  sides.
- **Her two hands are cell offices.** Above the fence, Osanne the chandler knows a
  seamstress named **Betriss Marle** and a salt-merchant named **Grigor Ashe** as
  ordinary Wickmarket-and-Tallage acquaintances. Below it, Betriss is the
  **Namekeeper** and Grigor the **Wicket**, and they are the Tracer's two hands.
  Betriss's public recantation on the Gradine in F.428 was planned with the cell to
  shield the Tracer; she remains sworn, and she alone reliably carries word to and
  from Osanne. None of this is renderable in any Vell context but Osanne's; render
  the acquaintances, never the offices.
- **Ewart must never learn any of it,** and *whether he suspects is not to be
  decided* — his never asking where she goes on quarter-feast nights is canon (her
  sheet), and it is the load-bearing ambiguity of the whole household. Do not
  resolve it toward suspicion or toward innocence. He does not ask. Leave the
  not-asking exactly as it stands.
- **No other Vell knows anything.** Tobin, Clemence, Sibbe, Averil, the whole
  scatter — none carry a grain of it. **Sible especially must stay mundane:** the
  locksmith of the Fabric ward has no cell knowledge, the resonance between her
  trade and the family's centre (locks, keys, what holds a door) is a resonance for
  the reader only, and she would be the last person in Ombreval to be trusted with
  a secret, being blunt, civic, and on speaking terms with the Bench. Do not make
  any other Vell a cell asset to give the family more heretic texture; the cell
  touches the Vells through exactly one person, and the loneliness of that is the
  point.
- **The canon this doc must not tip** (restated so no later Vell writer forgets):
  the green sun's nature is never resolved; the glass-memory doctrine the cell
  holds is FALSE-BUT-BELIEVED and refuted by a fact the cell will never see (new
  glass leaded into the Rose shows the Emblem too); the last page of the Sparr
  deposition stays AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN; and this family treatment adds no evidence
  about the phenomenon whatsoever, because a working wax-house is at its vats at the
  strong hour and not in the nave.

## What this document does not settle

Deliberately open, per the canon rules, and forbidden to later writers to close:

- **Whether Ewart suspects.** He does not ask where his mother goes on quarter-
  feast nights or why the name stays at the top of the leaf, and the not-asking is
  canon and must remain a not-asking. Do not write the scene where he finds out.
  Do not write the scene where he is revealed to have always known. Leave the ache
  chronic and the question open; it is the household's whole tension and it dies
  the moment it is answered.
- **What the light is.** The Vells make the lawful light of a city whose light is
  in question, and this document adds not one grain of evidence about the Emblem's
  nature, for or against any theory — the rival-theory rule holds, and a chandler
  at her vats is the wrong witness anyway.
- **Whether the dead hear a name in the green beam.** The Vells make the green
  candles; the folk speak names into the light; the Church absolves the sight and
  forbids the saying; the canon calls it AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN. Never confirm it,
  never mock it, and do not let the family that makes the candles be the family that
  settles what the candles do.
- **Whether the warmth is cover.** Below the fence the contract is cover and the
  tally-padding is protection; above it the warmth and the charity are entirely
  real. Do not resolve these into a single motive. Osanne's charity is genuine
  *and* it is camouflage, exactly as the cell's funeral-purse is piety *and*
  camouflage, and the family — and the cell — know it is both and cannot separate
  the strands, and neither should you. The horror is not that the warmth is fake.
  It is that it is real.
- **The fallen branch's loss has no villain.** The Reed loft came down in the
  Hammering and went to arithmetic after; no family took it, and Averil blames no
  one, and no later writer may hang the loss on another house to give the Vells a
  feud. The grievance is with a roof and a repair-share, and that is the whole of
  it, and it is worse than a feud precisely because there is no one to be angry at.
- **Sible Vell knows nothing and holds no key but iron ones.** The locksmith is
  mundane, the trade-echo is the reader's, and no writer may promote her into the
  family's secret. If the wax-house's centre ever needs a second Vell who
  understands it, the answer is that there isn't one, and the not-having is the
  point.
