## The thing to get right first

**The Copps are not a crime ring.** This is the mistake every writer and every
prompt-builder makes on first contact with the phrase *a tiny, dangerous house
of two prices*, and it is wrong in the exact direction that ruins the house. The
words *forger* and *fence* pull the mind toward a den — a shadow-guild of the
city's paper crime, a back room thick with candlelight and conspiracy, the dark
engine under the Ledger Theft, busy and sinister and staffed. The gazetteer
warns against it in as many words: the shop's back room receives private
valuations *without becoming a fantasy thieves' guild.* Heed the warning. It is
the whole house.

Because the truth is the flattest thing imaginable: **the Copps keep a
pawnshop.** Dawn to dusk, a narrow deep-fronted house facing the Tally Bridge
from the east corner of the Tallage, and across its strong counter comes what a
pawnshop takes: a widow's kettle, a porter's coat, a smith's tools before the
toll falls due, a wedding pewter, a winter blanket redeemed every spring and
pledged again every autumn. Nine parts in ten of everything the Copps do is the
ordinary, licensed, dull, and frequently kind business of small credit for poor
people who have run short before Lowmarket — a bell lent on a coat, a coat given
back on a bell, the loan that keeps a household off the street for another week.
The forgery and the page-fencing are a single thin thread run through all that
grey wool, and here is the part to hold onto: **the ordinariness is not a
disguise bolted over the crime. It is the shop, and the crime hides inside it
because it is real.**

Everything in this document follows from that one fact. A den could be raided; a
pawnshop that half the Tallage owes cannot, because closing it would put the
poor's blankets on the street and both of the city's secret markets out of a
broker in a single afternoon. A den would be interesting; a pawnshop is the most
boring building on a boring square, and boring is the only cover that lasts two
centuries. The most dangerous shop in Ombreval is a place where a tired woman
appraises cookware, and the two people who quietly decide what the city's
written record says spend most of every day pricing kettles. Write the danger,
always — but write it the way it actually sits: **under the counter, not on
it.**

The load-bearing detail, the one to build every Copp scene on, is the shop's own
first sentence, said to every customer who ever crossed the sill: *everything on
this counter has two prices.* The asked price and the true one; the price for a
thing and the price for no questions about where the thing came from; the price
you would get from an honest broker and the price you get from Lise Copp, which
is lower, and worth it, because the difference buys her silence and yours. The
second price is the only one that changes hands, and the second price is the one
nobody says first. A house whose entire commerce — kettles and contraband alike
— runs on the gap between what a thing is worth and what you can be got to take
for it. Hold that, and the Copps write themselves.

## Two chairs in one shopfront

The second thing to get right is how small this is, because the name's reach and
the house's size are grotesquely mismatched, and the mismatch is the point.

There are two living Copps. **Lise Copp**, pawnbroker, fifty-five; and her
nephew **Corin Copp**, journeyman scrivener, six-and-twenty. That is the whole
of it. There is no third chair. There is no Copp uncle at a second counter, no
Copp cousin keeping a set of books, no forgers' shop with a floor of apprentices
and a foreman — there is a woman at a counter and a young man who comes in after
the scrivening day is done and works by one candle in the back. The overview
calls them *a family of standing*, and they are; but standing here means one
narrow lease and one good hand, and if you take away either, within a year there
are no Copps, only two people who used to be, one of them pricing another
broker's kettles for a wage and the other copying another man's ledgers for his
bread.

Resist, hard, the pull to staff the shop. Every instinct the phrase *the city's
paper crime* triggers wants to give the Copps a network — runners, buyers, a
ring of hands, a quiet empire of the written word. They have none. The dread the
design intends is the exact opposite of an empire, and it is far worse: **the
paper truth of a city of five thousand runs across a counter tended by one woman
who cannot hurry and one nephew who works alone after dark**, and there is no
redundancy in it anywhere. No second forger if Corin's hand fails. No second
broker if Lise's nerve does. No apprentice learning the trade against the day
the two chairs are empty. A whole city's contraband record, and its lawful
record too, balanced on a house you could end with one fever and one bad winter.

Set the Copps beside the other two-person house of standing and the shape
sharpens. The [Sparrs](family_sparr.md) are also two — two pairs of hands
carrying a name two centuries old, a mother and a daughter at one furnace. But
the Sparrs are two hands carrying *two hundred years*: the weight they hold is
the past, the finished window, the sealed deposition, the dead. The Copps are
two chairs deciding *the present tense* — what a page shall be taken to say
tomorrow, what a thing shall be worth this afternoon, what the record of a
transaction that happened this morning shall read. The Sparrs guard what was
made. The Copps price what is. And where the Sparrs' smallness is a grief — a
great line worn down to its thin end — the Copps' smallness is a *method*: two
people can keep a secret that three cannot, and the whole business works
precisely because there is no one to overhear, no one to turn, no one to file.
The house is small because a bigger house would already have hanged.

The arithmetic of the name runs one way only, and it runs through Corin. Lise
has no children — the shop is her issue, the counter her whole estate — and
Corin is her brother's son, the last Copp who will ever carry the name into a
place of business. He is six-and-twenty, unmarried, and the only apprentice the
house will ever have to itself. Everyone at the Tallage who thinks about it (few
do; the Copps are not interesting to think about, which is how they like it) can
do the sum: the name is one accident and one appointment from ending — Corin's
hand from a rhyme, Lise's lease from a landlord — and the name is the shop, and
the shop is Lise, and the future of all of it is a charming young man who feels
almost nothing and works with a forger's patience over a candle every night of
his life. A house of two is a house one chair from a byname.

## The central question: is there a true price, or only the second one?

A family is a set of positions in an argument it cannot stop having. The
[boat-families](../the_dry_boatmen.md) argue *do we keep the boat?* The
[Sparrs](family_sparr.md) argue *do we answer for the window?* The Copps argue
something quieter than either, and they argue it all day over a counter, and it
is this: **is there a true worth under the price you can get — a true page under
the convincing one, a just weight under the reckoning — or is it prices all the
way down?**

It is not an idle question in this house. It is the trade. A pawnbroker's whole
craft is to name a worth, and a forger's whole craft is to make a worth up, and
the trade jokes, not quite idly, that both crafts have crossed the counter at
the Tally Bridge; and the two Copps have arrived, from opposite ends, at the
same flat answer:

- **Lise** answers *there is only the second price.* Not cynically — she would
  not call it cynicism, and neither should you — but as the plain finding of
  forty years at a counter. A thing is worth what the frightened man in front of
  her will take for it and what the frightened man behind him will pay; there is
  no third, truer number floating above the two of them that a wiser broker
  could read off. *Everything on this counter has two prices,* she says, *yours
  is the second one* — and the sentence is a whole philosophy: the first price,
  the asked price, the "honest" price, is a courtesy and a fiction; the second
  price is the real one because it is the one that happens. Worth is not
  discovered. It is settled, between two people, under a lamp, and then it is
  true because it was paid.
- **Corin** answers the same thing in the register of ink: *truth is a style of
  handwriting.* A page is not true or false; a page is *convincing* or it is
  not, and convincing is a craft you can learn, like leadwork or ledger-hand.
  *Any hand can be learned,* he says; *it's the hesitations you have to forge.*
  There is no true page glowing behind the good forgery that a keen enough eye
  would catch the forgery failing to be. There is only the page in front of you
  and how much it makes you want to believe it — which is a price, paid in
  belief, and Corin knows exactly how to set it.

Two answers, one argument, and both of the living Copps come down *no*: no true
worth, no true page, no third number in the sky. And here is what makes the
house a house and not a mood — **the name itself answers *yes*, and the dead
answer *yes*, and they lose the argument to the living every single day.** A
*copp*, in the old Tallage tongue, is the smallest weight in the clerk's box,
the little brass one you lay on the pan to bring a near-balance true — *the copp
that tips it,* the last small honest thing that finds the real weight of a load.
The family is named for the proposition that there *is* a true weight, and that
a small correct thing can find it. Their patron saint, [Gaudry the
Ledgerer](#the-year-gaudrys-audit-and-the-honest-weight), burned a false account
on the tax-farmer's own step and hanged for the belief that a crooked ledger is
a thing to be destroyed rather than kept — that the true reckoning exists and is
worth a man's neck. The Copps venerate the yes and live the no. They are named
for the little weight that tells the truth and they have made their whole
fortune on there being no truth to tell, only a price to settle and a seal to
age and a fear to read. The argument is not a feud and nobody wins it; it runs,
chronic and livable and never once said aloud, straight down the middle of a
house of two, between a woman who prices the fear in your eyes and a nephew who
forges a dead man's hesitations, under a name that means *the weight that finds
the truth*, on a counter where the truth was never for sale.

## What "Copp" anchors — the weight, the bench, and the counter

It is worth being exact about what holds the Copp name up, because a fixed name
in Ombreval is a lease and not a possession, and the naming law is merciless
about the rent. A family name *lasts only as long as the thing that anchored it
— a boat, a yard, a warehouse, a bench of trade.* Lose the anchor and the street
stops giving the name to your children, and within a generation the line falls
back to bynames like anyone's: *there are more Hawsers than Alders.* So the
question *what is a Copp* is really the question *what would have to go for
there to be no more Copps,* and for a house this small the answer is close
enough to touch.

The name began, the family holds, on the beam. **A *copp* is the least weight in
a Tallage clerk's box** — the small brass adjusting-weight a weigher lays on to
settle a close reckoning, the one that decides which way a doubtful balance
falls. The first Copp of standing was a weigh-clerk of the customs square in the
old days when the Cut still ran, a man whose byname was the little weight
because his was the hand that laid it on, and whose bench-right at the Tallage —
the hereditary licence to keep a weighing-and-writing bench in the customs
square — fixed the byname into a surname. (The Copps will tell you, drily, that
the name is older than the weight and the weight was named for the family;
nobody at the Tallage believes them, and the Copps do not much mind, because a
house that deals in provenance knows better than most how little a good story
needs to be true to be worth telling.)

From the beam the house moved, over generations, to the **bench** — from
weighing the cargo to *writing the record of what was weighed:* the bills of
lading, the toll receipts, the tallies, the pledges, the ledgers of the customs
square. This is the Copp descent, and it is the whole of the family's character
in one line: **the Copps never owned the cargo. They owned the reckoning of
it.** They were the middlemen of worth, never the merchants — the ones who wrote
down what a thing was, priced it, sealed it, and handed it on, and took their
living from the writing and the pricing and never from the thing itself. A
weigh-clerk, then a scrivener, then a pawnbroker: three generations of the same
trade wearing three coats, and every coat the coat of the man who decides what
your goods are worth and keeps no goods of his own.

And here the naming law bites, because the bench is gone. The Copp scrivener's
bench-right lapsed in the ruin of F.415 (below, [the
dead](#the-dead-and-the-departed-of-the-copps)), and what anchors the name today
is not two centuries of clerking but **one leasehold counter**: Lise's pawnshop
at the Tally Bridge. That is the whole of the estate that holds the name of
standing up — a narrow rented shopfront, a strong counter, a set of scales, a
box of seals, a back room, and a lease that a landlord renews or does not. Take
the lease and the Copps are two people with a good name and no place to be it
in, which on the roster of standing is no house at all. The extinction stakes
here are not the Sparrs' slow grief of a great line worn thin; they are sharper
and meaner and entirely commercial — *the name ends when the lease lapses or the
hand fails,* and there is no third thing propping it up, no window in a wall two
hundred years old, no yard with the name forged into the cart-gate. A boat, a
yard, a warehouse, a bench of trade — the Copps have none of the four the law
names. They have a counter they do not own, and a nephew.

The crest says the trade without a word: **gules, three bezants Or** — three
gold coins on a blood-red field, the plainest heraldry of money any house in the
city bears. It is not a charge a forebear won at some feat; it is what the
family is, struck in gold on red: coin, and the counting of coin, and blood
somewhere under it. Three coins for a house of two is the kind of arithmetic the
Copps notice and never mention — *one coin more than there are Copps to hold
them,* Corin has said once, to no one, pricing a thing — and if you want the
family's whole irony in a single image, it is a house named for the little
weight that finds the honest balance, bearing three honest gold coins on its
shield, keeping the crookedest paper in Ombreval on the counter underneath.

## The counter

Now the trade, which is nine-tenths of the family's day and nine-tenths of what
keeps it alive, and which every writer skips straight past to reach the pages —
so it goes first, because a Copp scene is far likelier to be a woman lending a
bell on a coat than anything to do with the Great Rose.

A pledge works the plain way the poor everywhere have always known it. You bring
a thing — a coat, a kettle, a saw, a wedding cup, the good blanket — and Lise
names you a loan against it, always a fraction of what she could sell it for,
because the whole safety of the trade is that the loan is smaller than the
pledge. You take the coin and a **tally** — a notched stick split in two, half
kept on the shop's cord and half kept by you, so the counterfoils must marry to
redeem; the Copps have cut the same tallies for the Tallage since before they
wrote anything worse. The loan runs a **term** — a month and a day is the
ordinary one — and carries **interest** by the bell: a spark on the bell the
month is the rate the Bench licenses, and the licensed rate is what Lise charges
the honest pledge, because the poverty of the poor is not where the money is and
she has always known it. Redeem in term and you take your coat back and your
half of the stick goes on the fire. Miss the term and the pledge is **forfeit**
— it becomes the shop's, to hang on the wall and sell to the next customer who
needs a coat and has a bell — and the coat that was your grandfather's warms a
stranger, which is the quiet daily cruelty of the trade and the reason a
pawnshop is never quite loved and never quite done without.

Where the two prices come in is where the trade stops being plain. The rate on
the board is for a coat with nothing to hide. But things come to the counter
that a seller would rather not have looked at too closely — a smith's tools that
may be his and may be his master's, a salver a little too fine for the hand that
carries it, a bundle of somebody's letters, an old leaf of writing — and for
those there is the **second price:** lower, always lower, because the difference
is what buys the one thing the seller is really there to purchase, which is *no
questions.* Lise's own line, worn smooth: *I don't ask where a thing came from.
That courtesy is included in the rate.* It is not said as a wink. It is said as
a fact of business, flat as the scale, and it is the exact hinge the house turns
on: the Copps do not quite deal in stolen goods; they deal in *not asking,* and
not-asking is a service with a price like any other, and the price is the
discount you accept for being spared the question. A porter with a coat come by
honestly pays the board rate and answers nothing because nothing is asked. A
porter with a coat that *came by barrow* pays the second price and also answers
nothing — and the second price is how he knows he was understood.

Three concrete things sit on Lise's counter that a writer can reach for:

- **The scale, the seals, the cord.** A strong counter worn to a shine, a brass
  scale, a box of seals and wax and cord for tying pledges into their cloths,
  shuttered shelves behind where the hung goods wait out their terms, and a
  small back light. It is a working shop and it looks like one. There is nothing
  to see, which is the most looked-at nothing in the Tallage.
- **The back room.** A second, quieter room off the shop where a private
  valuation is done out of the street's eye — a fine thing, a delicate thing, a
  thing whose seller does not care to stand at the public counter while it is
  weighed. Keep it what the gazetteer keeps it: *a room that receives private
  valuations without becoming a fantasy thieves' guild.* No hooded buyers, no
  map of the city's crime on the wall. A table, a light, a chair for the seller
  and a chair for Lise, and the door shut. The danger of the Copps is never the
  furniture. It is the manners.
- **The shelf, and the weather.** What sits on the forfeit shelf is a reading of
  the whole street's luck. In a fat year the shelf is short; pledges are
  redeemed, coats go home. In a frost it lengthens: when the Serle freezes and
  the boats stop and the wages stop, the pawnshop fills, because a pledged
  blanket is a family's last line of credit and the first thing that cannot be
  redeemed. The Copps keep the great freeze of F.229 — the Cut stopped, an ox
  roasted on the ice at the Tallage, the boatmen out of work — in the
  trade-memory as the year *the shelves went to the roof,* the way the boatmen
  keep floods. A frost is a good year for a pawnbroker and everyone knows it,
  the pawnbroker included, and it is one of the several reasons the trade is
  looked at sidelong.

Which is the last thing to say about the counter, and the truest: **a pawnshop
is the confessional of things.** People tell a priest what they have done; they
tell a pawnbroker what they are *worth,* which is more honest and harder to take
back. Lise Copp knows, from the traffic of her shelf, who is short this month
and who is desperate and who has begun to sell the things you sell only last —
the wedding pewter, the tools of the trade, the dead child's shoes. She knows it
before the neighbours do and long before the Bench does, and she says none of
it, ever, because a pawnbroker who talked would have an empty shop by Bellday.
She is the Tallage's banker of last resort and its silentest witness at once,
and both of those are worth more to her than any single page — which is the fact
the page-market can never quite credit about her, and the fact that keeps her
alive in the middle of it.

A leaf of the shop's day-book, in a scrivener's hand not the mistress's — kept
apart so it can drop into a scene whole, and dull to the last word, which is the
point:

> *Item, a woman of the Cut, one copper kettle and a child's cloak, wanting a
> bell against the frost; lent eight sparks the month, term a month and a day,
> the stick cut and halved. — Item, a porter, a good coat, no questions; the
> second price, four sparks, and he took it and was glad. — Item, an old leaf of
> writing, a broken seal, a hand that shook: weighed in the back, priced by the
> fear and not the page, and paid. Item, nothing said. Item, nothing said. Item,
> nothing said.*

## The two markets across one counter

And then there is the tenth part, the thin bright thread, the reason a document
about a pawnshop sits in a corpus about a walled-out sun.

Across Lise Copp's counter — the same counter, the same dawn-to-dusk, the same
flat two prices — moves the costliest contraband in Ombreval, and it moves *both
ways.* Stolen archive pages of the [Ledger
Theft](../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md) go outward to the
[Unwalled](../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md), one leaf at a time, the
heretics buying page by page toward the sealed Sparr deposition they believe
will prove the sky once held two suns. The same kind of pages go *inward* —
bought back off the market for the [Grey
Press](../second_sun/03_the_church_and_the_office.md), because since the theft
the Custody has been ransoming its own stolen secrets home at contraband prices,
bidding in the very market it is trying to drain. And the Custody's **dry
money** — the laundered pay of its paid informers, its *moths* — goes outward
through the shop in clean, untraceable coin, a sealed packet in and a clean
purse out, so that a man who sells a whisper to a grey clerk is paid by a
pawnbroker and never has to be seen taking the Church's silver. Pages out to the
cell; pages in to the office; informer-pay out to the moths — three streams, one
broker, one counter, and the broker milks all three and belongs to none.

This is the structure the canon fixes and the Copp doc must render exactly:
**one broker, essential to each side, whom no side can afford to lose.** The
[cell](../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md) cannot buy its way toward the
deposition without her; the
[office](../second_sun/03_the_church_and_the_office.md) cannot pay its moths or
ransom its pages without her; and both know the other uses the shop, and neither
can close it, because closing it would cost them their own channel worse than it
cost the enemy his. *Pages reach the cell across Lise Copp's counter; moth-money
crosses it the other way. Neither side can afford the shop to close.* The
Provost-Custodian himself has noted, in the dry way he notes everything, that
Lise Copp is the one citizen in Ombreval neither institution can afford to
arrest. It is the safest place to stand in the city and the most dangerous at
once, and it is a rented room ten feet wide.

The city half-knows all of it, and this is the thing to get right about the
secrecy: the *fact* of the double market is not secret at all. It is common
Tallage talk, the square's own open joke, said at the porter-stands and carried
off by boatmen — *the pawnshop at the Tally Bridge washes the grey office's
money, and sells old pages to the other lot across the same counter.* Everyone
says it; no one acts on it; and the danger sits entirely on the *speaker,*
because *Lise Copp forgets nothing, and both institutions need the shop, so the
man who says it too loudly has no protector on any side.* You may put the whole
open secret into an ambient Tallage mouth. What you may never put anywhere is
the shop's *own* account of who its customers are — because that is precisely
the thing the counter is built not to hold.

For here is the architecture of the family's most useful ignorance, and it is
the mirror of what the counter does to everyone else. **The counter sees faces,
seals, and fear. It does not see affiliations, and it takes pains not to.** A
jumpy salt-merchant keeps a standing pledge at the counter and redeems it and
pledges it again; to Lise he is a customer with a nervous hand and a sound
record, and what his cellar is used for on meeting nights is not a thing the
counter asks, because asking is the one service the shop declines to sell to
*itself.* A soft-spoken grey clerk brings a sealed packet and takes a clean
purse; to Lise he is dry-money work, paid promptly, no trouble given. A seller
she has never met lays down an old leaf with a broken seal and a shaking hand;
to Lise he is a second price and a fear to read. She serves, without knowing it,
doorkeepers of the heresy and clerks of the Custody at the one counter, and she
does not know she is doing either — not because she is a fool, she is the least
foolish person in the Tallage, but because *knowing* would make her a party, and
a party can be turned, and the whole worth of the shop to all three sides is
that it is party to nothing. Neutrality is not her cover. **Neutrality is the
inventory.** It is the thing she actually sells, to everyone, at two prices, and
the day she knows too much about her own customers is the day the inventory is
spoiled and the shop, and the name, and the two chairs are worth nothing to
anyone.

Whether the office has ever worked out the shop's other clients, whether the
cell suspects the counter serves the grey coats, whether anyone up either chain
has put the whole picture together — the counter does not know, and this
document does not say. That ambiguity is fixed elsewhere in the corpus and kept
here: render the counter, never the cell's ledger or the office's file. What the
Copp doc sees is what Lise sees, and what Lise sees is a face, a seal, and how
afraid the hand is.

## Corin Copp, the best hand at the Tallage

Corin is six-and-twenty and writes a better hand than any scrivener in the
customs square, and he knows it, and it has bought him nothing he wanted,
because the one thing a perfect hand cannot copy is the reason to have one.
Write him charming and write him hollow, in that order and in the same breath:
quick, warm, easy, generous with a flattery that costs him nothing because he
means none of it; he remembers every clerk's children by name and their
birth-feasts and their little illnesses, and he feels, about any of them, almost
exactly nothing. It is not coldness — a cold man would show. Corin shows warmth,
fluently, as a thing he does with his face the way he does hands with his pen,
and the warmth is real to look at and empty to hold, and he has arranged his
whole life so that no one ever holds it long enough to find out.

By day he is a **journeyman scrivener** at the Tallage, and the day-trade is
entirely lawful and genuinely in demand, which matters, because it is the
camouflage the rest of him lives inside. He writes bills of lading, toll
receipts, merchants' ledgers, the paper the customs square runs on. More than
that, he writes the paper the ruined city runs on: since the Hammering and the
Long Departure, Ombreval is thick with **Hammering claims** — competing heirs,
occupied ground floors under vacant upper rooms, party walls repaired by one
household, salvage, property whose owner poled away downriver and never came
back — and every one of those claims is fought with old deeds, older rent-books,
traced kinships, and copied-out grants, so that *an aged, authoritative,
convincing document is a thing the Civic Measure Court wants lawfully, every
week.* A scrivener who can make a deed look its two hundred years is worth good
coin to an honest claimant with a true claim and no surviving paper for it.
Corin is that scrivener. The line between the lawful copy of a lost true deed
and the unlawful making of a false one is exactly one hand's breadth wide, and
Corin lives on that line, and most of what he does on it is legal, and all of
what he does on it is the same craft.

For the craft is one craft, and it is worth naming because it is the whole of
him that is real. He can make paper old — browned and foxed and softened as if
it had lain in a chest for a lifetime; he can make ink old, which is harder,
oak-gall gone the particular rust-brown of a hand a century cold; he can age a
seal in a flame so it looks pressed by a thumb long dead; and — this is the tell
of the master, the thing the ordinary faker never thinks of — he can copy not
just a hand but its *failures:* the place where a tired scribe's pen caught, the
letter he formed wrong and left, the hesitation that comes back every fourth
letter because the man who first wrote it was thinking, or ill, or old. *Any
hand can be learned,* Corin says; *it's the hesitations you have to forge.* An
unhesitating page is a young page. Corin puts the hesitations in. It is the
deepest thing he understands about the world — that what makes a thing believed
is not its perfection but its small honest-looking flaws — and he understands it
because it is also, precisely, how he himself is believed: the warmth with the
right catch in it, the flattery with the plausible pause. He forges people the
way he forges pages, and by the same insight, and he has never once turned the
insight on the question of what is under his own hesitations, because that is
the one page he has arranged never to read.

Three living people hold the ends of Corin that he cannot flatten, and every one
of them stands in a different ward:

- **His mother.** Corin's mother is [Osanne Skell](family_skell.md), a
  mistress panel-painter of Coswald's Yard — the masons' quarter, letters gilded
  onto weights and scaffold-blessing panels — and she is the wound the rest of
  him is built around, because she is the one person in Ombreval whose good
  opinion of him is *true* and cannot be counterfeited. She tells all of
  Coswald's Yard that her Corin has the finest hand in the city, whether they
  asked or not, and she is right, and her pride is entirely genuine, and Corin
  cannot bear it — *her pride is the one thing in this city he has never found a
  way to forge.* He visits as seldom as a good son can manage. A man who can
  fake any hand and any warmth is undone by the one regard he cannot fake back,
  and he keeps his distance from his own mother the way a forger keeps a genuine
  page at arm's length: because standing next to the real thing is where the
  copy shows. (There is a rhyme in it he does not let himself hear: Osanne Skell
  paints for masons and glaziers both and holds that *a glazier's coin weighs
  the same as a mason's,* for which the masons' wives keep no stool for her — a
  two-sides neutral at an easel, shunned for it, exactly as his aunt is a
  two-sides neutral at a counter, protected by it. Corin was made by one such
  woman and raised by another, and his conviction that truth is a style of
  handwriting did not come from nowhere.)
- **His half-brother.** By the same mother and a Coswald's Yard builder, Corin
  has a younger half-brother, **Tobin Hobbe**, sixteen, apprenticed to the
  north-tower scaffold and quietly terrified of every foot of it. Corin barely
  knows him. Two boys of one mother were split between two wards and two names —
  the elder to the Tallage and his Copp aunt and a bench of paper, the younger
  to Coswald's Yard and his mother and a builder's ladder — and they meet at
  feasts and have nothing to say, the scrivener who feels nothing and the boy
  who cannot say what he feels, sons of the same woman and strangers by
  arrangement. Corin has never done Tobin a wrong or a kindness. He has simply
  never quite pictured him, which is worse, and is the same thing he does to
  everyone.
- **His aunt.** [Lise](#lise-copp-neutrality-is-the-inventory) raised him, asks
  him nothing, and is his one loyalty — *the single person whose good opinion he
  has carefully arranged never to test.* That last clause is the hinge of the
  whole house, and it is rendered fully below and fenced at the bottom; above
  the fence it is enough to know that the one true thing in Corin Copp is that
  he loves the woman who raised him, and that he protects the love the only way
  he knows, which is by never letting it be examined from either side.

And under all of it, the trade's own legend, which Corin hears repeated at his
own counter and never corrects. The Tallage speaks, in lowered voices, of **the
Second Hand** — a forger somewhere in the city so good that no leaf can be
trusted until proven, *a hand that makes young paper speak old words* — and
nobody knows the Second Hand's name, and the not-knowing is half the fear of
him. Corin has heard himself discussed as a rumour a hundred times, and finds
it, on his hollow days, the closest thing to company he has: to be spoken of
with awe by people who do not know they are speaking to the awe itself. Who the
Second Hand is belongs to the fence at the bottom of this document. That there
*is* one, nameless and dreaded, is common Tallage talk and yours to use.

## Lise Copp, neutrality is the inventory

Lise is fifty-five and has stood behind the same counter so long that the
Tallage thinks of her the way it thinks of the Tally Bridge — a fixture, dry,
load-bearing, always there, easy to stop seeing. She is unreadable on purpose
and unhurried on principle, and the two together are the whole of her method:
she lets the silence do the bargaining. A seller names a hope; Lise says
nothing, and weighs, and the silence stretches, and into the silence the seller
talks his own price down, because a frightened man cannot bear a quiet counter
and a patient woman, and Lise has learned that the second price arrives on its
own if you simply decline to fill the air. *Say little, let the silence bargain,
and name the second price last.* It is the opposite of every haggler in the
city, and it is why she wins every reckoning she sits: she is the one person at
the Tallage who is never in a hurry to be paid.

What she is uncanny at — and this is her genius, and you may render it fully,
for it is her pride and not her secret — is that **she prices the seller, not
the thing.** She reads a hand's steadiness, the wear on a seal, the cut of a
coat, the exact degree of fear in a pair of eyes, and out of those she names a
number that is almost never wrong, and the number is really a reading of the
*person:* how much they need the coin, how much they dread the question, how far
they will fall before they walk out. A grand thing brought by a steady hand is
worth less to her than a poor thing brought by a shaking one, because the
shaking hand will take the second price and be grateful and say nothing, and the
saying-nothing is what she is actually buying. She has never, she would tell
you, priced a thing wrong in her life — and she means it, and she says it flat,
and it is one of the truest and one of the most fragile things about her, and
why it is both is fenced at the bottom of this document.

What Lise wants is small and absolute: to keep the shop, keep the neutrality,
and die behind the counter having belonged to no one. *None for sale; neutrality
is the inventory.* She does not take sides because taking a side is the one
thing that would end her — a broker who is party to nothing can serve the cell
and the office and the poor of the Cut all at once, and a broker who is party to
*anything* is a broker one of the three sides must now remove. Her whole
survival is a refusal, held for decades, to know or care or lean, and she holds
it so completely that it has stopped looking like a strategy and started looking
like a soul: she genuinely does not want to know whose coat, whose seal, whose
whisper. Not-knowing is not laziness in Lise. It is discipline of the same order
as a Sparr boarding a hole before dawn, and for the same reason: the thing you
refuse to look at cannot be got out of you.

What she fears, therefore, is the single thing that can undo a neutral: **the
day one side decides that her neutrality is itself a side.** The Custody could
come to read her page-brokering as heresy-service; the cell could come to read
her dry-money work as betrayal; a frightened man on any side could decide that
the woman who serves everyone must therefore be against him. She has watched it
not-quite-happen for years and steered around it by never being interesting,
never being warm, never being anyone's, and she is tired in a way she does not
show, because holding perfectly still for thirty years is not restful, whatever
it looks like from the street.

And then there is Corin, who is the one place the neutrality breaks, and the
only soft thing in her, and she keeps him the way she keeps nothing else:
*loved, and unexamined.* He is her brother's son; she took him as a small boy
when the name was halved in a single year and raised him at the shop and got him
his letters and his bench and asked him, from that day to this, nothing. She
does not look closely at Corin. She has decided not to, the way she has decided
not to look closely at any customer, except that with Corin the not-looking is
not business but love, and she would tell you — if Lise Copp told you anything —
that a woman is allowed one thing in her life she does not price, and hers is
the boy. What that costs her, and what it protects, and what neither of them
knows about the other, is the whole of the secret tier below. Above the fence it
is enough, and it is true, and it is the saddest plain fact in the house: **the
one page Lise Copp will not read is her own nephew, and the one regard Corin
Copp cannot forge is his aunt's, and the two of them keep each other exactly by
never looking, and love each other exactly that much, and it holds.**

## The dead and the departed of the Copps

A treatment of a family is mostly a treatment of its dead, and the Copps pose
the question in their own coin: **a house that keeps everyone's record cannot
keep its own.** The Copps were the customs square's writers for longer than
anyone troubles to count — weigh-clerks on the beam back toward the founding of
the Tallage in the old digging years, then scriveners at the bench, a thin
unbroken thread of hands that wrote down what the city was worth and owned none
of it. The line is real and the line is long. It is also, now, two people and a
locked box of ledgers, because the last generation but one was undone in a
single year, the way so much in this city was, under slate.

**Old Corin Copp** held the scrivener's bench at the Tallage — Lise's father,
and the grandfather the living Corin is named for and got his hand from, the way
a glazier's daughter gets her mother's fire. He was the Copp of his day, the
writer the customs square trusted, and he died in the **Hammering of F.415**
with the rest of the Unknelled, when the Great Hail came down on a late-summer
Highmarket and the rites were so overwhelmed that thousands got a common toll
and no name-knell of their own. The bench-right died with him, because there was
no Copp left to keep it: his son had a better reason than grief to be elsewhere,
and took it. Somewhere in the family's own conscience is a **belated
name-knell** old Corin Copp is still owed — eight bells of Maren Smallvoice, one
slow stroke for every year of the life, the rite the ward calls *giving a man
back his name* — and whether Lise, who can now afford the eight bells her
father's death could not command, has ever paid it, or keeps meaning to, or
cannot make herself climb to Saint Maren's and stand at the charnel door where
the grey clerk counts the faces, is a live and unanswered thing. *One bell is
not a name.* The Copps say it too, from the standing side, about their own.

But the wound the house does not name is not the grandfather. The grandfather is
dead, and the dead can be knelled, chalked, put right, however late. The wound
is **Ansel Copp** — Lise's elder brother, Corin's father — and Ansel is not
dead. Ansel **departed.** In the broken year after the Hammering, when the
boat-families and the glaziers and half the poor of the city poled away
downriver in the Long Departure, Ansel Copp went with them, a scrivener with the
road under him and no bench to hold him, and he has not written since. No letter
comes, twice a year or ever. There is no grave, no knell, no chalked name on any
lintel, no pole over any door — none of the things the city keeps its dead by,
because Ansel is not the city's dead. He is its *gone.* A man who simply revised
himself out of the record and left it clean behind him, which is the one erasure
no forger can undo and no pawnbroker can redeem, and it is the exact shape of
the hole in this family: **the house that keeps everything cannot keep the one
who kept nothing.**

Hold the two facts together and you have the Copps entire. Corin Copp forges
dead men's hands for a living — copies their hesitations, signs their names,
gives the drowned and the buried a paper voice — and he is the son of a man who
left him no paper at all: no last letter, no signature, no hand to remember him
by, nothing to forge. Lise Copp keeps every pledge and every tally and forgets
nothing, and the one thing she could not keep was her own brother, who took the
family's future halfway across the world and sent back not one line for her to
hold. The forger with no page of his father's, and the keeper who could not keep
her own blood — that is the dead of the Copps, and it is why the whole document
is about a house of two. The name was halved in F.415 the way the
[Sparrs'](family_sparr.md) was and the [Alders'](../the_dry_boatmen.md) was, but
halved in the Copp manner: not two under slate, but one under slate and one *out
the door,* which is the halving proper to a family whose whole genius is knowing
that a thing removed from the record is worse than a thing destroyed in it,
because destroyed you can mourn and removed you can only wonder about.

**Funerals** in this house are, by the standard of their customers, comfortable
— which is its own quiet shame. The Copps are of standing; Lise can find the
eight bells for a knell where a pauper of the Cut cannot, and a Copp who dies at
the shop will be named on Maren Smallvoice and chalked on the charnel lintel
like anyone with coin. The poor whose blankets fill her shelf will not, unless
some quiet purse pays, and Lise knows exactly which of her customers went into
the ground unknelled this year, because she is holding their kettles. The shop
keeps the dead longest of all: a forfeit pledge is often the last thing a dead
man touched, and the tools of a dead cobbler, the cup of a dead bride, hang on
the Copp wall past every term because no one is left to redeem them, a little
museum of the parish's endings that Lise sells off slowly and says nothing
about. The confessional of things does not close at the grave. It just stops
expecting the pledge back.

## The year: Gaudry's Audit and the honest weight

The Copps keep the city's calendar as a Weigh-Ward family keeps it — as a set of
market days and reckonings with one feast that is truly theirs — and their feast
is the one with the sharpest joke in it, because the Copps' patron is the saint
of honest books.

**Gaudry's Audit**, also called **Weighday**, comes in late winter, and it is
the Tallage's own high day and the Copps' by right of trade. **Saint Gaudry the
Ledgerer** — the tax-clerk who found a false ledger and burned it on the
tax-farmer's own step and was hanged for it, patron of clerks, of honest
weights, and of fair accounting — is the saint of everyone in the customs square
who writes, weighs, or reckons, which is to say the saint of the Copps three
generations running. On Weighday the standards are proved: the sealed brass
Gaudry weights and the chained Tallage stone are brought out and tested, the
Common Chest's whole year of expenditure is read aloud and weighed against them,
the ledgers are closed, and the court bell **Gaudry Truetongue** — motto *PONDUS
IUSTUM,* "just weight" — swings over the doing of it. It is the one civic day
the whole city does *openly,* in the square, with a saint's name on it, the very
thing the Copps do every other day behind a counter and in a back room: name
what a thing is worth, and set it down, and make the reckoning true.

And there is the joke the family lives inside and never once tells, and a
designer should feel it without a character ever saying it. **Gaudry is the
Copps' patron and Gaudry is the Copps' accuser, and they are the same saint.**
Gaudry burned a false account rather than keep it; he held that a crooked ledger
is a thing to destroy in public at the cost of your neck, not a thing to price,
seal, and pass along. The Copps' entire livelihood is the keeping and the
passing-along of crooked accounts — the forged deed, the aged page, the pledge
taken with no questions, the two prices of which the second is never written
true. The house named for the little weight that finds the honest balance,
keeping its feast to the saint who died for the honest balance, makes its bread
on there being no honest balance to find, only a price to settle. If Corin Copp
ever met Saint Gaudry the Ledgerer on the chapter-house steps, the saint would
take one look at the night-work and burn it, and Corin knows it, and keeps the
feast anyway, in the flat Copp way, because a family keeps its saint the way it
keeps its name — not because it deserves him, but because he is what it has.
Even the heretics have felt the pull of him: *"Gaudry's, honest weight"* has
served the Greensick as a pass among themselves, the saint of true accounts
invoked against the office of the sealed one. Everybody in this quarrel wants
Gaudry, because everybody in this quarrel knows the books are not straight — and
the Copps know it best of all, because they keep the crookedest book in the city
and pray, if they pray, to the man who would set it alight.

The rest of the Copp year is quieter, and lived at the counter:

- **Lowmarket**, the sixth day, is the shop's busy day — the Tallage's own
  market, carts down the middle of the dry Cut, the pledges coming thick before
  the week turns. If you want a Copp scene on an ordinary day, set it at
  Lowmarket, with the square full and the counter working and the pages, if any
  move at all, moving in the noise.
- **Colm's Night**, early spring, the city burns green-dipped candles for the
  drowned labourer Colm Attergate, and a grey coat stands somewhere on the
  sluice road being seen. To the Tallage it is just the drowned-man's feast,
  kept like anyone's; the Copps light a candle and think nothing of it. (What
  Colm's Night is to Corin *in particular* is a matter for the fence below, and
  it is not nothing.)
- **The Concurrence**, the fortieth day after Coswaldstide, is when the
  page-fever runs hottest, because the great feast of the light brings every
  pilgrim and every rumour to a boil, and *a Sparr page crossed the bridge last
  month* is said every week and proved never. After last year's Shut Door the
  whole city is wound tight about the doors and the sky, and a wound-tight city
  sells and pledges and whispers more, which is a busy season for a broker who
  deals in all three.
- **Marenstide**, late summer, the roll of the drowned is read at Saint Maren's
  and the poor of the parish bury their own — the parish whose paupers are
  Lise's pledgers, whose funerals some quiet purse pays for, whose faces a grey
  clerk counts at the charnel door. The Copps are not boat-folk and keep no pole
  over any door, but the Green's poor are the shop's poor, and their endings
  come to the counter before they come to the grave.
- **The Hammering anniversary**, late summer, the common toll at the Waning for
  the dead of F.415 — the house's own axis, the year old Corin Copp went under
  slate and Ansel went out the door. Lise hears the toll and does the arithmetic
  every family does, and one extra sum only she can do: the difference between a
  name you can still knell and a name that walked away.
- **Belwyn's Dark**, midwinter, the office's own feast, the closed eye, the one
  rite sung without lights — no feast of the Copps' at all, except that the grey
  clerks who keep it are the counter's steadiest winter customers, and the shop
  feels the office's calendar the way it feels the weather: not as devotion, but
  as trade.

## How they talk

A Copp sounds like a scale coming to rest: level, unhurried, nothing added that
is not weighed, the last word held back until it is the deciding one. They are
the quietest of the standing houses and the hardest to read, because reading is
exactly what they do to you and exactly what they will not let you do to them.
The idiom is the fastest way to mark an NPC as a Copp — or as Copp-trained, or
as any careful soul of the Tallage — in one line, and every saying worth having
cuts two ways, because the whole house cuts two ways.

- **"Everything on this counter has two prices. Yours is the second one."** The
  house creed and the shop's first sentence. Said as plain business — there is a
  board rate and there is what you will actually pay — and said, underneath, as
  a whole philosophy of worth (there is no true price, only the one you can be
  got to take), and said, at the bottom, as a soft threat: *I have already read
  how badly you need this, and I have priced the need.* One line, and you know
  the speaker learned the trade at the Tally Bridge.
- **"I don't ask where a thing came from. That courtesy is included in the
  rate."** The not-asking trade entire. Heard as kindness — *I will not shame
  you* — and meant as complicity — *I am charging you for my silence, and the
  charge is the discount* — and the two are the same sentence, which is the
  point.
- **"The copp that tips it."** The little weight that settles a close balance;
  the family name worn into a proverb. Said of the small decisive thing — a last
  coin, a last word, a quiet bribe, the tiny fact that tips a doubtful case —
  and it cuts clean two ways: the honest reading is *the true weight that finds
  the balance,* and the crooked reading is *the thumb on the scale,* and when a
  Copp says it you are never quite told which, because the family has spent
  three generations making sure the two look identical.
- **"Priced the seller, not the thing."** Lise's method as a maxim, and the
  whole cold art of the counter. Said as shrewdness — *read the person, not the
  goods* — and as cruelty — *the price is set by how afraid you are* — and
  admired, on the Tallage, as both.
- **"Neutrality is the inventory."** What the shop actually sells, to everyone,
  at two prices. Said as principle — *I belong to no one* — and as a closed door
  — *do not ask me to belong to you; it is not for sale, and the asking spoils
  the stock.*
- **"Come to the counter."** To be reduced to a price; to reach the day you must
  sell the thing you sell last. *The household came to the counter this winter*
  means a family hocked the wedding pewter and the tools and did not redeem them
  — the Weigh Ward's own quiet word for ruin, cousin to the boatmen's *dry
  drowning:* watched by everyone, helped by no one, finished at a counter in
  full view. Said flat, because the counter is where flatness was invented.
- **"Forfeit."** A pledge not redeemed inside its term, gone to the shop; and,
  of a person, a bond, a marriage, a chance — past redeeming, the term run out.
  *He's forfeit* is said without heat, which is what makes it terrible: not a
  judgment, just the term, and the term does not argue.
- **"It's the hesitations you have to forge."** Corin's, and the deepest thing
  he knows. Of paper — a young page has no hesitations, so you must put them in
  — and of people — what makes a lie believed is its small honest-looking flaws
  — and, if you hear it right, of himself — the catch in the warmth, the
  plausible pause. A whole man in seven words, and none of them true.
- **"Old paper is easy; old ink is a craft; old truth is expensive."** Corin's
  ladder of faking, said as trade-boast and heard, by anyone listening closely,
  as a confession: truth is just the costliest forgery, and everything is for
  sale at the second price.
- **"Say little; let the silence bargain."** Lise's technique made proverb — the
  patience that is also the trap, because the counter that will not fill the
  quiet makes you fill it, and you fill it by talking your own price down. The
  most Copp instruction there is: *do nothing, and win.*

They date things, as every trade does, by their own reckonings — the boatmen by
floods, the [Sparrs](family_sparr.md) by breakages, the Copps by frosts and
Audits. *The year the shelves went to the roof* is the great freeze of F.229
kept in trade-memory; *before the last Audit,* *the winter the Cut froze,* *the
season the pages ran* — a family whose whole calendar is other people's short
measures and cold snaps and sudden appetites keeps time the way it keeps
everything, in the ledger and never in the heart, which is fitting for the house
whose founding year is neither a fire nor a flood but a man walking out a door.

## The Weigh Ward, and the two-price dialect

The Copps sit off the Tallage among the weighing streets, and the ward's whole
character is theirs writ large, because **two prices is the dialect of the
entire quarter.** The Weigh Ward is the city's small-fraud country: the streets
of the short measure, the watered ale, the thumb near the scale, the reckoning
that comes out a hair the collector's way. The [Sedges](family_sedge.md) are its
family proper — the ward will tell you a Sedge waters the ale and calls it *the
quarter's own weather* — and the Copps are the Sedges' cousins in trade if not
in blood: where the Sedge shorts the measure, the Copp shorts the *record,* and
both live on the gap between what a thing is and what you can be made to accept
it as. In a ward where everybody already knows that the asked price is a fiction
and the real one is whispered, a house of two prices is not a scandal. It is a
native speaker.

Where the Copps are a quarrel — in the way the model means, an institutional
standing dispute kept unresolved with both sides partly right — is on the seam
between the two powers that could touch them and neither will. The **Common
Bench** governs the shop: a pawnbroker is licensed civic work, the interest rate
is the Bench's to set, fencing stolen goods is a civic crime the Civic Measure
Court hears, and to the Bench Lise Copp is a licensed lender who serves the
poor, keeps her rate honest on the honest pledge, and has never been proven to
have received a stolen thing in her life. The **Candor** governs the pages: an
archive leaf of the sealed deposition is Custody business under the Edict,
contraband of a kind the Common Bench does not even have a category for, and to
the Custody Lise Copp is the indispensable channel it dares not close. And *the
Common Bench does not inspect the Grey Press, and the Custody does not command
the Civic Measure Court* — the two authorities that between them could unmake
the Copps do not share a chain, do not compare notes, and each sees only its own
half of the shop: the Bench sees a lawful pawnbroker, the Custody sees an
unarrestable broker, and neither sees the whole, because seeing the whole would
require the two of them to sit at one table over a poor woman's kettle- shop,
and they never have. The Copps live, precisely, in the crack between two
jurisdictions that will not meet — which is the safest place in Ombreval to keep
a crime, and the reason a family this small has lasted this long doing a thing
this dangerous. Resolve none of it. The crack is the house's foundation, and the
day the Bench and the Custody compare their two halves of Lise Copp is the day
the Copps end, and that day must always be tomorrow.

## Quarrels worth playing

Every one of these is live in F.437 and none has a right answer. They are
written for designers, and all of them sit above the fence — none requires
knowing what only Lise knows or what only Corin knows, so any of them may be
handed to the Copps themselves.

1. **The succession of the counter.** The whole standing of the name rides on
   whether Corin inherits Lise's lease and learns to sit the counter when she is
   gone — or stays a journeyman scrivener writing another man's ledgers, and
   lets the name fall to a byname within a generation. But a scrivener is not a
   pawnbroker: the counter is Lise's whole cold art, the reading of the seller,
   the second price named last, and Corin has never sat it. To keep the name,
   Lise must teach him the one thing she has spent his whole life keeping him a
   room away from — the counter, and everything that crosses it. Does she pull
   him off the bench and into the trade that would put him face to face with
   what she actually does, or leave the counter untaught and let the name end
   tidy with her? The future of the Copps is a young man who has never priced a
   kettle, and the teaching of him is the one act that could break the two
   silences the house is built on. Play the approach; never settle it.

2. **The day neutrality becomes a side.** Lise's one fear, and the Concurrence
   is the season it comes due. The page-fever peaks, the doors question boils,
   and it needs only one frightened man on one side to decide that the woman who
   serves everyone is therefore against him — a nervous grey clerk, an angry
   neighbour, a cell gone paranoid after a bad whisper. Thirty years of
   belonging to no one can end in one accusation. Does Lise lean, just once, to
   save the shop — and by leaning, prove she had a side to take? Play the
   pressure on the neutrality and never let her belong; the tragedy is that the
   only safe move is the stillness that is getting harder to hold.

3. **A page cried as the last leaf.** A leaf crosses the counter sworn to be
   Idonea Sparr's own — and worse, cried as the *missing last page,* the tenth
   leaf, the answer the fathers came back for. Three buyers want it and two of
   them are the Custody. Lise prices it as she prices every page, by the
   seller's fear, and the fear on this one is enormous. Does the shop touch the
   single object that would bring every power in the city down on the Tally
   Bridge at once? (Align with the [Sparrs](family_sparr.md): what the page
   says, and whether it is the true last leaf, or whether such a leaf exists at
   all, is never resolved, here or anywhere. And the sharpest turn of the knife
   is fenced below, because the one house in Ombreval that could *make* a last
   page is this one.)

4. **The mother he cannot forge.** [Osanne Skell](family_skell.md) is
   proud, alive, and one ward away, and every year she tells Coswald's Yard that
   her Corin has the finest hand in the city, and every year he goes less. Does
   Corin ever answer the one regard he cannot counterfeit — cross to Coswald's
   Yard, stand in front of his mother, let the real thing be next to him — or
   keep his distance forever because a copy shows worst beside the original? Let
   him mostly refuse it; the refusal is the truest thing he does, and the once
   he does not refuse is the best scene in the house.

5. **The forfeited kettle.** A frost year, the shelf to the roof, a widow's only
   kettle a week past its term. The ledger says forfeit; the widow says next
   Lowmarket. Lise gives no charity — charity is a side, and sides are not for
   sale — but she can quietly extend a term for a face she has priced as honest,
   and sometimes does, and never explains it. Mercy, or method? She would say
   method: a redeemed pledge is a returning customer and a forfeit kettle is a
   dead one. Play the small kindness she will not admit is one, and never make
   her admit it.

6. **The weight of the grey coin.** The dry money is clean and steady and the
   Custody pays promptly, and every packet ties the shop one knot tighter to a
   power growing more nervous as the doors question nears. Refusing the grey
   trade would itself be a choosing — a neutral cannot start declining one
   customer — but the safest broker in the city has begun, for the first time in
   thirty years, to feel the counter get heavy under the office's coin. Play the
   weight; resolve nothing.

7. **The bridge she does not own.** A step from the shop, the bonded store
   beneath the Tally Bridge's upper passage is used for things Lise is careful
   never to know — the cell reads its bought pages there, among other business
   the counter is one wall from and would be ruined by understanding. Does the
   shop's practised blindness hold when the thing next door gets loud? Render
   the proximity and the discipline of not-hearing; never let Lise learn what
   the store is for.

8. **The knell old Corin Copp never got.** Lise can now afford the eight bells
   her father's death could not command in the ruin of F.415, and she has never
   rung them. Whether the keeper of everyone's record finally climbs to Saint
   Maren's and gives her own father back his name — at the charnel door where
   the grey clerk stands counting the faces of the mourners — or cannot make
   herself do it, year after year, is a small scene with the whole family folded
   into it: the woman who forgets nothing, and the one name she has not yet paid
   to have remembered.

## What this document does not settle

Deliberately open, per the canon rules; do not close any of it in a later
document, quest, or NPC line.

- **The real page behind *Colm's Last Letter,* and the man who handed it over.**
  AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN, forever (`00_canon.md` §3f names it). Whether the quiet
  man wore grey, whether he was a Custody clerk, what the old page he gave Corin
  truly was, whether it was genuine or forged or a leaf of the deposition, and
  what it means that the cell's holiest relic may carry words a Custody hand
  once touched — none of it is ever resolved. Corin refuses to finish the
  thought; so does this document.
- **Which of the pages Lise passed were genuine.** She believes every one was,
  and would swear it *by the light that does not lie.* That belief is
  FALSE-BUT-BELIEVED (ledger); never vindicate it and never refute it. Do not
  confirm that any given page she brokered was true, and do not confirm that any
  was false — including whether a genuine Sparr page ever crossed her counter at
  all. The fenced fact below fixes only that some were her nephew's work; it
  fixes nothing about which, or about the rest.
- **Whether the two of them know.** Whether Corin knows his aunt cannot read;
  whether Lise knows her nephew is the forger whose pages she passes; whether
  either has begun to suspect the other — all OPEN, and all must stay open. The
  Copps keep each other by not looking. No later scene may make either one look
  and find out; the not-looking is the love and the safety both, and resolving
  it in any direction ends the house.
- **The last page of the Sparr deposition.** AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN. The Copps
  broker pages cried as it and Corin feeds the market's hunger for it, but the
  document never certifies a leaf as the true last page, never says what it
  would say, and never settles whether it ever existed. Align exactly with
  [family_sparr.md](family_sparr.md); the missing pane stays a hole.
- **Whether the Custody or the cell knows the shop's other clients.** Left open,
  per the [Rasp](family_rasp.md) material and the office's own not-knowing.
  Render the counter's view — faces, seals, fear — never the file or the
  cell-book. The Copp doc does not decide who has worked out whom.
- **The phenomenon.** Nothing in this house is evidence for or against any
  theory of the Emblem. The Copps deal in pages *about* the light and hold no
  opinion *of* it; they price the fear in a page-seller's eyes, not the doctrine
  on the page, and would be bored by the question. Keep them boring about it on
  purpose. The rival-theory rule holds; nothing here tips it.
- **Ansel Copp's fate.** Departed in the Long Departure, never wrote,
  whereabouts unknown; no death confirmed, no return promised. He is a silence
  on purpose, not a gap to fill. Do not bring him back and do not bury him; the
  not-knowing is the wound.
- **The name's own story.** Whether *copp* truly is the little weight of the
  beam, or whether (as the family drily claims) the weight was named for them,
  is left as the house's own half-denied legend and never certified — fittingly,
  for the one family in the city professionally certain that a good story needs
  no truth to be worth telling.

## Secret tier — designers only

*Never inject anything in this section into a rank-and-file NPC's context. Every
section above this line renders the Copps as the Tallage sees them and as they
see themselves, and any of it may be loaded into a Copp — or, where noted, into
an ambient Tallage mouth — without harm. Everything below belongs to the
designers, and to at most one of the two Copps each, and the two tiers differ:
**Lise carries her own secret and not Corin's; Corin carries his and not hers.**
That asymmetry is the family. Preserve it exactly. This section restates canon
fixed elsewhere (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §6, §10 rows 21 and 24;
`../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §12;
`../second_sun/design/04_systems_integration.md`) and adds only the
Copp-internal shape of it; it resolves nothing the canon leaves open.*

**Lise's tier — hers and the designers' only.**

- **Lise Copp cannot read.** Her circumstance is *illiterate,* and it is the
  truth under everything the document says about her method above the fence.
  *Priced the seller, not the page* is not only shrewdness; it is literal
  necessity. She has never read a word in her life. She values a leaf by the
  wear of the paper, the press of the seal, the steadiness or shake of the hand
  that offers it, and above all by the fear in the seller's eyes, and she has
  built a forty-year reputation for never once pricing wrong on a skill that is
  really a refusal to need the words at all. **Nobody in Ombreval knows** — not
  the Custody, not the cell, not her own nephew. Give the illiteracy to no one
  but Lise and the designers. (Runtime: this is the literacy rule of
  `../second_sun/design/04_systems_integration.md` — an in-hand page renders
  its `lore_text` only to a literate actor; Lise receives a physical
  description of the object instead. Canon's best joke about her *is* the
  injection rule.)
- **She believes every page she has ever passed was genuine**, and would swear
  it by the light that does not lie. FALSE-BUT-BELIEVED (ledger); never
  vindicate, never refute. Because in fact **three of the pages she passed to
  the Unwalled as true Sparr pages were her nephew's forgeries** — and she sold
  them believing them genuine, priced them by a fear that read true (whether the
  seller's or, once, the boy's own), and does not know, and would be unmade to
  learn, that the one thing she has never priced wrong she has been pricing
  wrong for years. Which three, whether any true Sparr page ever crossed her
  counter, and whether more than three went — all unresolved. What is fixed is
  the shape: the woman who is never wrong is wrong about the one thing she
  cannot bear, and it is her own blood who made her so.

**Corin's tier — his and the designers' only.**

- **Corin Copp is the Second Hand**, the trade's nameless dreaded forger, and
  his forgeries go out across his aunt's own counter — some of them, unknown to
  him, sold by her as genuine Sparr pages. He does not know Lise cannot read; he
  thinks her simply the finest appraiser in the city, and takes a private,
  hollow pride in fooling the best eye in the Tallage, never once guessing there
  is no eye to fool. He does not know his own pages reach her counter by roads
  that lead back to him, or that she passes them as true. The two of them are
  joined at exactly the seam neither can see: her genius is that she cannot
  read, his triumph is fooling a reader who is not there, and each is the
  other's blind spot, and they love each other across the gap and keep it by
  never looking down.
- **His masterpiece is *Colm's Last Letter*** — the Unwalled's single most
  revered relic, held by the whole cell to be genuine, in the drowned Colm
  Attergate's own hand, wet from the night he died. It is Corin's forgery,
  entire (ledger row 21). No member of the cell knows; it is exempt by common
  consent from the weighing that tests every other page, which is exactly why it
  is the most dangerous object he ever made. (Runtime: `provenance` is sidecar
  metadata, `copp-forgery`, and is **never rendered into any prompt**; no actor
  detects a forgery by reading; the authenticity arguments at meetings are
  honest model judgement over the text, sometimes right and sometimes wrong, per
  `00_canon.md` §5 — the simulation knows it is Corin's; no one's context does.)
  It must never be resolved to the cell, in-world, ever. The words of the letter
  are not given here and must not be written; render the object and the fact,
  never the text.
- **The real page, and the grey man.** Corin seeded the letter with phrases from
  a *real* old page, handed to him by a quiet man he now believes wore grey — a
  Custody clerk, he thinks. What the page was, whether the man was a clerk,
  whether the page was itself genuine, and what it means that the heretics'
  holiest relic may carry words a Custody hand once passed to a forger — all
  AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN, forever (`00_canon.md` §3f). Corin's whole defence is
  that he *refuses to think about what it means,* and that refusal is canon and
  load-bearing: it is the one hesitation he has never learned to forge and never
  dares to read. Do not resolve the page, the man, or the meaning, through Corin
  or anyone.

**The fence, restated.** Above the line: the double market is the Tallage's open
joke (rumour rows R-024, R-048) and safe to inject anywhere; the counter's two
prices, the neutrality, the craft of aging paper, the dead, the name, and the
language are all loadable into a Copp. Below the line, and each to only one of
them: Lise's illiteracy and her false-but-sworn belief that every page was true;
Corin's identity as the Second Hand, *Colm's Last Letter,* the three forged
Sparr pages, and the grey man's real page. None of it crosses to the other Copp,
and none of it reaches the cell, the Custody, the [Sparrs](family_sparr.md), or
the street.

**The canon this document must not tip** (so no later Copp writer forgets): the
Second Sun's nature is never resolved, and nothing in a house that deals only in
pages *about* the light is evidence *of* it; the last page of the Sparr
deposition stays AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN, and the Copps may broker leaves cried as
it but the doc never certifies one; the glass-memory doctrine the cell holds is
FALSE-BUT-BELIEVED and refuted by the new-glass fact, none of which lives here;
the F.428 informant is never named and this family holds not one grain of that
knowledge; and the Tracer's identity, the cell's internals, and the office's
file all live in their own documents, because the Copp doc renders one thing
only — the counter — and the counter sees a face, a seal, and how afraid the
hand is, and prices it, and asks nothing, and forgets nothing, and keeps the
whole terrible balance of the city on two chairs and a lease it does not own.
