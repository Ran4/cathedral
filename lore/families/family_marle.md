## The thing to get right first

**The Marles are not a heretic house.** This is the mistake the stub invites and
every prompt-builder makes on first contact — the phrase *a cloth-and-cathedral
house with a heretic heart* slides the eye to the word *heart* and reads the
whole family as somehow in on it, a house of quiet believers gathered round a
keeper of names. It is wrong, and it is worth fixing before you write a single
Marle, because getting it wrong turns an ordinary, anxious, respectable family
into a coven and throws away the only story the house actually has.

The heretic is **one woman**. Betriss Marle is sworn; no other Marle is
anything. The salt-porter, the gaol-guard, the bencher, the wick-priest, the old
woman on the south-wall plots, the two apprentices on the Fabric, the
honey-seller begging a pitch, the chamber servant in the Stone House — every
other person who carries this name is exactly what they look like, and what they
look like is a middling city house of needle-work and stone-work with the
ordinary spread of poverty, small crime, and small office that any Ombreval name
of standing shows when you lay it out. There is no ring of Marles. There is a
family, and inside it, unshared and unsuspected, there is Betriss.

So the fact to get right is not that the Marles harbour a secret. It is that
they **do not know they harbour one**, and could not act on it if they did,
because the naming law will not let them:

> A fixed family name is held, not owned; nobody strips it from the living. You
> cannot cast a Marle out of the name any more than you can cast a plank out of
> a floor. Betriss will die a Marle whatever she did, and every Marle knows it.

That is the whole engine. A family that cannot disown a shamed member has only
one move left, and the Marles make it daily, in the most public building in the
city: **they perform distance.** They cross the street; they leave a seat empty
between; they do not speak her name at a betrothal; they wish, out loud and to
each other, that she would keep to her room. And underneath the performance —
this is the load-bearing thing, and it never once gets said at any Marle table —
the family **cannot agree what she is.** Penitent or relapsed. Innocent all
along or guilty and hiding it under piety. The family's shame or, God forgive
the word near a Marle, the family's saint. The recantation settled it for the
parish and settled nothing for the blood. Nine years on, ask three Marles and
you will get three verdicts, and none of them will be spoken where the other two
can hear.

Everything in this document follows from that: **the Marles perform one distance
in public and hold a dozen private verdicts, and the gap between the performance
and the verdicts is the family.** It shapes the trades (how a needle and a
chisel both reach the nave, and what it does to a family to have its shame keep
office hours in its second home); it shapes the dead (who was lost, and how a
house grieves out loud when one of its living is a scandal); it shapes the year,
the sayings, and every quarrel worth playing. Do not resolve the verdicts. Do
not let any Marle decide, on the page, what Betriss really is. The city decided
*guilty and forgiven*; the family cannot decide at all; and the not-deciding,
rendered as habits and never as a speech, is the house.

The stub's phrase is not wrong, only misread. The heart of the house is not
heresy. **The heart of the house is respectability, and what it costs to perform
it over your own blood where a thousand people can watch you do it.**

## The central question, and the second thing to get right

The boatmen ask *do we keep the boat?* The Vells ask *which end of the name are
you on?* The Marles ask one question and cannot stop, and it is never put into
words at the table because to put it into words is to have to answer it:

> **What is Betriss — and what do we owe her?**

Every Marle of consequence holds an answer; the ambient mass of the name holds
one too, in aggregate. The answers are livable and chronic. Nobody is about to
do anything — a family does not, over nine years, resolve a thing it has spent
nine years learning not to say. They simply each stand at a different distance
from a stool near the crossing, and the distances do not change, and everyone
can feel them.

The second thing to get right is subtler and just as load-bearing: **the
family's distance is sincere.** It is tempting, knowing what you know below the
fence, to write the Marles as if they were in on the theatre — cold to Betriss
in public because they know the coldness protects her. They do not know that.
Not one of them. The cross-the-street Marle crosses the street because she is
ashamed and frightened and wants the name clean; the sit-beside-her Marle sits
down because he thinks a penitent is owed a seat; and neither has the faintest
idea that the whole apparatus of the city's coldness toward a recanter is, for
this recanter, cover. Render the distance as what it is to the people performing
it — anxious, real, and about respectability — and keep the thing it
accidentally does in the fenced section where it belongs. The horror the design
wants is not that the family is acting. It is that the family is not acting, and
it works anyway.

## What "Marle" anchors

A fixed name in Ombreval lasts exactly as long as the property under it
(`../core_lore/naming_language.md`; the [Vells](family_vell.md) work the rule
out in full). The Marle name has two anchors, and they pull in different
directions, which is why the house has always been two trades wearing one
surname — the "cloth" and the "cathedral" of the stub are not a metaphor, they
are two freeholds and a walk across the city between them.

**The cloth bench.** The older anchor, and the one the family says first, is a
**cutting-and-selling bench under the Draper's Reach** — the roofed cloth-hall
gallery between Coswald's Yard and the Wickmarket, where the iron standard ell
of Ombreval is nailed and half the city's cloth is measured against it. The
Marle bench is a modest freehold: a length of trestle and shelf held in the
family name, where a Marle cuts, mends, makes up, and sells piece-work, and
takes apprentices under articles. It is not a draper's hall or a merchant's
loft; the Marles buy cloth by the ell and sell labour by the seam, a
needle-and-shears house rather than a wool-and-credit one. But the bench is
*named*, and a named bench in the Reach is standing — it buys the fixed surname,
the apprentice articles, a pew, and a Marle's word being good at the Tallage for
a little more than a bynamed tailor's. It is the reason the family has needles
in it three generations deep, and the reason Betriss has a trade at all.

**The mason's yard.** The second anchor is stone: a **small building-yard off
the Fabric streets**, dressed stone and jobbing masonry and the cartage that
goes with it, close under the cathedral works. It is humbler than it sounds — no
lodge wardenship, no great contract, a yard that squares stone for other men's
walls and sends its apprentices up other men's scaffolds — but it put the Marle
name on the Fabric back when the Fabric was still rising, and it keeps a Marle
chisel in the cathedral quarter to this day. Between the bench and the yard runs
the whole house: the name that measures cloth in the north-west and squares
stone in the east, and carries, without ever quite noticing, a needle and a
chisel that both end up pointed at the same building.

**What the name reaches beyond the freeholds.** Two freeholds make a house of
standing; what makes the Marles a *cloth-and-cathedral* house in particular is
where the name has climbed on top of them. It reaches a **bench** — in F.437 a
Marle holds one of Bell-and-Sluice's two seats on the Common Bench — and it
reaches a **pulpit** — there is a Marle in a wick-priest's collar in the Fabric
parishes. Neither is hereditary; a bencher serves at the ward's pleasure and a
wick-priest at the Candor's, and both could pass out of the name next year
(`../core_lore/secular_government.md`). But while they hold, they are the crown
on the name: a house that squares stone and mends cloth and *also* sits where
the ward's rules are made and stands where the parish is preached to. For a
middling family that is a great deal of respectability to be carrying — and it
is exactly the respectability that has to be managed, every day, around the one
Marle the whole city knows for the wrong reason.

Keep the standing **real but thin**. The Marles are a house of standing and they
are also, most of them, poor; the two freeholds earn a living and not a fortune,
the bench and the pulpit are borrowed grandeur, and the name runs out into
day-wages and worse within a branch or two. When a Marle says *we are a house of
standing*, they are telling the truth and reaching, in the same breath, the way
the Vells do — and for the same reason, which is that everyone in the family has
seen how fast a name comes off a door.

### Naming, as this house lives it

The naming law is in `../core_lore/naming_language.md`; here is only how the
Marles live it, because the house shows the rule running two ways at once.

- **The freeholds name the children.** A Marle is a Marle for as long as there
  is a bench or a yard for the name to sit on. The apprentices squaring stone
  and learning shears in F.437 are Marles because the yard and the bench still
  stand; let both go, and inside a generation the street stops giving the name
  and the line falls to bynames of trade and place like anyone's.
- **Women keep their own names.** A wife in a Marle house runs her own trade
  under her own byname, and a Marle woman who marries out stays a Marle. Betriss
  is a Marle in her own right, not by any man; her sheet gives her no husband
  and no children, and the name is hers to carry or to shame on her own account.
- **The name reaches lower than the freeholds.** Most people called Marle own
  nothing the name is pinned to — the water-carrier, the furnace hand, the file
  leader — and carry the surname on the strength of the bench and the yard they
  will never see the inside of. To them the name is a sound that says the family
  was *someone*, produced for a scrap of dignity and worth that scrap and no
  bread. This is the naming law doing its slow work, the sound outliving the
  thing; the [Vells](family_vell.md) render the same weightlessness in full, and
  the Marles carry it identically, a house of standing fraying at every edge
  into the ordinary poor.

## The nave as the family's second address

Here is the mechanism the whole house turns on, and a writer should hold it
before writing any Marle scene: **the Marle name reaches the Lanthorn from three
directions of honest work, and a fourth Marle sits near the crossing being spat
at, so the family cannot go about its own business in its own second home
without passing its own shame.**

The three honest directions are real and daily:

- **Stone.** The mason's yard sends Marle-squared stone and Marle apprentices
  into the Fabric works. A cathedral four hundred years a-building and still
  scaffolded on the north crown is a standing employer of masons, and a house
  with a chisel in the quarter has kin on the scaffold, in the lodge's overflow
  labour, and in the yards that feed the works. When a Marle says *ours is a
  cathedral house*, the first thing they mean is stone.
- **Cloth.** The bench under the Draper's Reach puts Marle needles within reach
  of the one cloth the cathedral cannot do without — **vestments and
  altar-linen**. The Lanthorn's linen wears out, tears, greys, and must be
  mended and made up, and the office of the Rite gives the small mending out to
  trusted, cheap, devout, nimble needles. A Marle seam that has passed muster on
  an altar-cloth is a Marle seam the vergers know, and it is how the family's
  needle gets *lawfully* as close to the high altar as a layperson's needle ever
  gets.
- **Service.** The name reaches a Taper's letters and a wick-priest's collar in
  the Fabric parishes, and a verger or two of other houses know every Marle who
  comes and goes at the fabric door. A cloth-and-cathedral house is, in the
  plainest sense, a house whose people are *in the building* — mending,
  squaring, serving, praying — far more of the week than most.

And then there is the fourth Marle. **Betriss sews all day on a stool near the
crossing**, in the open nave, where the whole church can see her being devout,
because in F.428 she recanted on the Gradine and has been the woman neighbours
spit near ever since (below, and her sheet). She is at the west doors before
they are unbarred every morning; she takes what mending the vergers throw her,
altar-linen included when they are feeling charitable; she says *One Light,
undivided* morning and evening, as instructed, loudly enough to be overheard.
She is, in the strict sense, part of the family's nave presence — another Marle
in the building — and she is the one every other Marle in the building has to
decide, every single day, how to walk past.

The effect on everyone else's standing is exact and cruel. A Marle mason
carrying squared stone through the crossing passes a Marle on a stool that the
whole nave is watching; a Marle apprentice sent to the fabric door with
altar-linen to mend is sent past the recanter who might, on the vergers' whim,
have mended it herself. The wick-priest of the name preaches *to see is no sin,
to affirm is* in a Fabric parish while a Marle two wards off is the parish's
standing example of what affirming costs. None of them can be *only* a mason, a
needle, a priest in that building; they are always, also, kin to the woman on
the stool, and they feel it as a weight on their own respectability that they
did nothing to earn and cannot put down. This is why the family's distance is
performed hardest exactly where the family works hardest — in the nave — and why
the Lanthorn, the source of the Marle name's dignity, is also the daily scene of
its shame. The house's pride and the house's wound share one roof and one set of
doors, and a Marle walks in under both every morning of the working week.

> **"There's a Marle on the Fabric and a Marle on a stool."** Said in the cloth
> halls and the mason's yards, half boast and half wince: the name reaches the
> cathedral two honest ways and one that everyone would rather forget, and you
> cannot claim the first two without the third coming up. A Marle says it to
> stake the family's standing; a rival says it back to puncture it.

## Betriss, as the city sees her

Everything in this section is what the whole city knows and every Marle lives
with; it carries not one grain of what she is underneath, which is fenced at the
foot of this document and belongs to her context and the designers alone. Render
this Betriss into any scene freely. Render the other one nowhere but her own.

**The public facts.** In F.428 the Custody took five people in Gaunt Passage on
an informant's word; two recanted on the Gradine and were received back, and one
of the two was Betriss Marle, then twenty-nine, a seamstress. She stood on the
Lanthorn's stepped forecourt, read her error back from the Custody's own
formula, and **wept as the crowd required** — a recantation counts as a success
precisely because it is survivable, and she survived it, and the city watched
her walk home (`../second_sun/03_the_church_and_the_office.md`). She has been
the woman neighbours spit near ever since. And she has answered the spitting
with the one thing that cannot be cited: she has become **ostentatiously,
tirelessly devout**. She is at the west doors before they are unbarred every
morning; she sews all day on a stool near the crossing where the whole nave can
see her; she takes piece-work — shirts, mending, altar-linen when the vergers
are charitable — and lives thin off it in a rented room off the Bellstand; she
says the creed morning and evening, as instructed, and affirms nothing, ever, to
anyone.

**The unreadable thing.** What nobody can tell — not the parish, not a grey
coat, not her own kin — is *why*. Is the devoutness penance, a broken woman
working off a real sin in the only currency the Church will take? Is it fear, a
recanter who knows the next leaf brings the Gradine again or worse, buying
safety with visible piety? Is it faith, a woman genuinely returned to the Sole
Light and over-proving it because the spitting never stops? Or is it none of
those — a mask over a heart that never changed at all? The city cannot read her,
and the point of her, above the fence, is that the city *cannot*: her face gives
nothing, her seams are perfect, her prayers are letter-correct, and every
observer supplies the reading their own fear or charity prefers. Keep her
exactly that opaque. A Marle scene where Betriss is legible — visibly sincere,
visibly false — has broken her.

**In company, meek; alone, granite.** The one thing observers do agree on is the
change of register. In company she is soft-spoken, quick to agree, quick to
efface herself, the very picture of a chastened woman. Left alone at her stool
she goes still and hard and unhurried, and needleworkers who have watched her
from the next bay will tell you the quiet woman sews like someone carved out of
the work. Read it how you like; the parish reads it as grief, the uncharitable
as sullenness, and both are guessing.

> **"I keep the names. Someone must."** Her one line that is not a prayer, said
> of her own trade and her own memory — a seamstress remembers every
> measurement, every customer, every debt of thread, the way a rent-book does,
> and Betriss's memory is famous for it on her street. Said mildly, over
> piece-work, it means only that she is the one who remembers who owes for what.
> It is the most ordinary sentence in the world in a seamstress's mouth. Let it
> stay that ordinary.

**Nan, who is honest.** The one person in Ombreval who treats Betriss as a plain
woman is not a Marle at all. She is **Nan** (66, washerwoman of the Cut, a
pauper widow; her own sheet, and a major character — see
`../characters/laundress/`), who washes for half the Cut and for the Skell house
and for Betriss the seamstress, and who wishes Betriss good morning loudly
enough for the spitters to hear it, on the flat ground that *a woman who has
done her penance has done it, and her coin is as round as anybody's.* Nan is
blunt, warm, filthy-mouthed, slow on the stairs, and will tell a woman her
business whether she asked or not; she knows a household by its linen and
remembers every debt and birth and grudge on the street. She is not kind to
Betriss out of sympathy for a heretic — Nan has no politics about the green sun
and no patience for anyone's — she is decent to her because decency is cheaper
than spite and the coin is good. And that plain decency, from a washerwoman who
owes the Marles nothing, is the exact measure this document holds the family up
against, because Betriss's own kin, with the name and the bench and the pulpit,
mostly cannot manage what Nan manages before the Kindling bell: to say *good
morning* and mean nothing cruel by it.

## The family's verdicts, plural — the heart of the house

This is the section the house is for. The Custody's cold-shoulder rule
(`../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §10) has the recanted publicly
cold-shouldered by *sworn neighbours*, as cover; but the treatment of Betriss by
her own *family* is not fixed anywhere, and it is the real subject of the
Marles. Render it as habits, never as a debate. No Marle has ever stood up at a
table and argued Betriss's case; they have only, each in their own way, *done*
things — and the things do not agree, and the family has arranged itself so the
disagreement never has to be spoken. What follows is the spectrum, from the kin
who cross the street to the kin who sit beside her, with the cost of each.

**The cross-the-street kin.** For some Marles the recantation closed the
question: she stood on the Gradine and confessed an error, which means there was
an error, which means she brought this on the name, and the only thing to do
with a shame you cannot disown is to keep as far from it as the same surname
allows. These are the Marles who take the long way round the crossing rather
than pass the stool; who, asked at the cloth halls whether the seamstress in the
nave is kin, say *of the name, not of my house*, which is true and is meant to
wound; who would, if you pressed them in private and drink, tell you she should
have kept to her room, or gone quiet to another city, or — the sentence that is
never quite finished — *spared us*. Their distance is real fear dressed as
principle: a house of standing is three thin freeholds and a borrowed bench and
pulpit, and one cited kinswoman feels, to a Marle who has climbed, like a hand
on the rope that holds all of it. The cost of their answer is that it is
indistinguishable from cruelty, and half the time it *is* cruelty, and they know
it, and prefer it to the risk.

**The sit-beside-her kin.** For others the recantation closed the question the
other way: she confessed, she wept, she did the penance the Church set, and a
penance done is a penance done — to go on punishing a woman the Church has
received back is to set yourself above the Candor, which no Marle is entitled to
do. These are the Marles who will sit on the stool's bench for a while when they
are in the nave, who send a child round with a loaf on a feast-day, who say her
name plainly at a betrothal because leaving it out is its own kind of lie.
Idonea is the clearest of them (below), and she puts it in the fewest words. The
cost of *their* answer is that it draws the same eyes onto them — a Marle seen
sitting with the recanter is a Marle a grey coat now knows by sight — and that
the cross-the-street kin read it as the sit-beside kin risking the whole name to
feel good about themselves. Both readings are held in the family at once, and
neither is put to the other.

**The profit-from-distance kin.** The subtlest and least comfortable answer, and
the one nobody will admit holding: that Betriss, at her stool, being visibly,
tirelessly, publicly punished, is *useful*. A house with a recanter in it that
the whole nave can watch atoning is a house whose other members are
conspicuously, by contrast, sound — the bencher who is scrupulous, the
wick-priest who is correct, the apprentice who is devout. Her shame, performed
daily in the open, launders the rest of them; a family that has *obviously* cast
out its error in the person of one weeping woman is a family the parish need not
look at twice. The Marles who benefit this way are, mostly, the climbing ones —
the office-holders, the ones with something to be respectable *for* — and they
benefit in silence, and would be genuinely appalled to have it said aloud,
because to say it is to admit you are spending your kinswoman's humiliation like
a coin. So it is never said. It is only *done*, in the form of a bencher who is
careful never to be seen near her and privately relieved that her penance is so
thorough, and a sermon on affirming-nothing preached by a Marle who does not
mention that the parish's own example of the sin shares the name. This is the
answer the register note warns against stating as irony. State nothing. Show the
bencher taking the long way and the priest choosing the safe parable, and let
the reader feel what the Marle will not.

**The indifferent poor.** And then most Marles, who hold no verdict at all,
because Betriss is a rumour two wards off and they have a sore heel, a wet coat,
a promised cart that is late, a place in the hiring line to keep. To the
water-carrier and the furnace hand and the honey-seller begging a pitch, *the
recanter in the nave* is a thing the name is faintly known for, like a scandal
in a ballad, and no help and no harm to a person choosing between bread and a
knell-fee. The stub's phrase for this is exact: the name is a sound, and the
sound does not feed you. These Marles are not cold to Betriss; they simply have
never had the leisure to be anything to her, and the branch that had standing to
lose is the only branch that can afford a verdict.

**What no Marle will decide.** Under all four answers sits the question none of
them can touch: **was she ever guilty, and if she was, is she guilty still?**
The recantation says she erred; it does not say she has stopped. A devout
recanter and a relapsed heretic hiding under devotion look, from the outside,
precisely alike — that is the whole cruelty of the Custody's survivable theatre
— and the Marles, who see her more closely than the parish but no more clearly,
are split down the middle and will not test it. The cross-the-street kin assume
the worst and stay away; the sit-beside kin assume the best and draw near;
nobody knocks on the question, because knocking on it means either accusing a
kinswoman the Church has absolved, or vouching for a kinswoman who might yet
hang them all. So the verdict stays plural, permanently, and the family runs on
the gap. **Never close it.** A Marle who says, on the page, *she's guilty* or
*she's innocent* has ended the one argument the house is made of.

## Idonea Marle — the plainest verdict in the house

Idonea (71, agricultural labourer, Reed Ward) is the family's oldest named voice
and its ballast, and she is the sit-beside-her answer stripped of all drama.
Read her sheet: she works the south-wall plots for day-wages and a place in the
hiring line, is quick with a dry joke and slow with trust, wears her grey hair
cropped close on one side, and is the woman people in her ward come back to
because she *remembers agreements and faces*. She means, in her own flat words,
**to keep her place without pretending that every local quarrel concerns the
Great Rose** — and that sentence, which is on her sheet, is the whole of her,
and very nearly the whole of the case for the defence in this family.

She is the one Marle old enough to put the family's shame in proportion, because
she has watched the city lose far more than a reputation. Born in **F.366**,
four years into the Great Rains' aftermath, she is a child of the dry city: they
were still filling the Cut when she was in arms, and she has never in her life
seen water run where the trade street is now, which makes her, quietly, the
wrong person to ask about the river and the exactly right person to ask about
loss. She was a woman grown of **forty-nine** when the Hammering fell in F.415
and the plots and the streets filled with dead; **sixty-two** the year Betriss
wept on the Gradine; **seventy** at the Shut Door. Set a nine-year-old scandal
beside that ledger and it stops looking like the end of the name and starts
looking like what it is — a hard patch in a long family, one more thing that
happened.

So Idonea's verdict on Betriss is Nan's verdict in the mouth of blood: **a woman
who has done her penance has done it.** She will not cross the Green — she is a
Reed Marle and rarely in the nave at all — but when their paths cross she gives
Betriss the plain nod she gives anyone whose measurements she trusts, and she
has told the cross-the-street kin, dryly and once, that a family that spent the
Hammering burying its own has no business spitting at one of its living over a
thing the Church has already forgiven. She does not defend Betriss's soul; she
has no opinion about Betriss's soul, and distrusts anyone who claims one; *keep
your place without pretending every quarrel concerns the Great Rose* cuts
against the heresy-hunters and the martyr-makers in the same breath. Betriss did
a wrong, or was caught in one, and paid what the parish set, and is kin, and
that is the end of Idonea's interest. It is the least dramatic position in the
house and, the document quietly holds without ever proving it, the one the
family's own better nature would take if the respectable middle had not mislaid
it.

Keep her **mundane and unheretical**. Idonea holds nothing of what is fenced
below; she is not sympathetic to the cell, she is simply too old and too tired
and too plain to make a drama of a kinswoman's bad years. Her immediate world is
a borrowed handcart, a day's hire, a hiring line she means to keep her place in;
her gift is that people trust what she remembers; her cost is that a Reed
labourer of seventy-one has watched enough go under that she will not spend her
small warmth on anyone's cause, only on the person in front of her. That she
gives Betriss the nod and the cross-the-street kin do not is the sharpest thing
this document says about the family, and it says it entirely in a nod.

## The needle — the seamstress trade, and the wounded anchor

Cloth is the older half of the name and, in F.437, the sorer. A writer setting a
scene at the Marle bench or beside Betriss's stool needs the mechanics, because
the family's whole cloth-dignity and its whole cloth-wound are in the
piece-work.

**The bench and the ell.** The Marle freehold under the **Draper's Reach** is a
cutting-and-selling bench in the roofed cloth-hall gallery where the iron
standard **ell of Ombreval** is nailed — the measure every length in the city is
proved against. A cloth-bench Marle buys by the ell and sells by the seam: cuts
and makes up shirts, shifts, coats, and household linen; mends, turns, and lets
out; takes the odd apprentice under articles. It is honest, thin, close work,
and it is *standing* — a named bench at the ell is a house of standing, where a
bynamed tailor at a barrow is not — but it has never been a wealthy trade, and
since the Hammering it has not even been a whole one. The western loft of the
Reach came down in F.415 (below, and
`../second_sun/04_chronicle_of_the_city.md`); the cloth trade shrank into the
building that survived it; and the Marle needle strength never came back to what
it was. The bench still works — let in part, kept by a hand, mended at by kin —
but the apprentices the family is bringing up now are both in the *stone*
trades, which is the quiet truth under the proud phrase: a house that says
*cloth* first is, in F.437, leaning on the chisel, because the needle end took a
loft on the head twenty-two years ago and has limped since.

**The bitter arithmetic.** Here is the thing the respectable kin cannot say. The
most famous Marle needle in the city is not at the family bench. It is
Betriss's, at a stool near the crossing, in full view of a thousand people,
sewing in disgrace. The name's living cloth-dignity — the one Marle everyone in
Ombreval could point to with a needle in her hand — is the shamed one. A house
whose cloth anchor is a half-worked bench and whose most visible seamstress is a
recanter is a house with a wound where its oldest pride used to be, and it is
one more reason the cross-the-street kin cannot forgive Betriss: she has not
only shamed the name, she has become, by working her penance in public with a
needle, the *face of the family trade*, and there is nothing they can do about
it but square more stone.

**Piece-work, in coin.** Betriss lives thin, and the thinness is exact. A
journeyman's day in Ombreval is about three bells; a seamstress on piece-work
does not see a journeyman's day. A shirt made up whole earns a few sparks over
the cloth; mending is by the seam and the patch, a spark here, two there; a good
week is bread and rent and thread and not much past it, and a slack week is the
thread bought on credit against next week's mending — *a debt of thread*, the
smallest debt in the city and one a seamstress carries constantly. This is why
Betriss's memory for who owes what is not a quirk but a survival: on piece-work
you live or sink by remembering every measurement and every unpaid seam, and *"I
keep the names, someone must"* is, above the fence, a poor needlewoman's plain
account of her own book.

**How close the needle gets to the nave.** The one thing the cloth trade has
that the stone trade cannot match is *altar-linen*. The Lanthorn's linen and
vestments wear and tear and grey, and the office of the Rite gives the small
mending out to trusted, cheap, devout needles under the vergers' eye. A Marle
seam that has passed on an altar-cloth is a seam the vergers know by name, and
it is the lawful way a layperson's needle comes within arm's length of the high
altar. It is also, for this family, the cruellest possible arrangement, because
the needle the vergers trust with the altar-linen when they are feeling
charitable is most often Betriss's — the recanter, mending the church's own
cloth in the church's own nave, as devout and as watched as a person can be. The
family's holiest work and the family's public shame are, again, the same Marle
at the same stool. Nobody designed that. It is simply where a
cloth-and-cathedral house's needle ends up when its most conspicuous needlewoman
is doing penance where the whole church can see.

## The chisel — the stone side, and the cathedral quarter

The other half of the name is stone, and in F.437 it is the sounder half. The
Marle **yard off the Fabric streets** squares stone and does jobbing masonry
close under the cathedral works, and the family's coming generation is a
stonecutter and a carpenter learning their articles in the Fabric ward —
apprentices at the chisel and the saw, brought up in the shadow of a cathedral
four hundred years a-building and still scaffolded on its north crown. The stone
trade is why the Marles can call themselves a cathedral house without
stretching: a house with a chisel in the quarter has kin on the works, in the
lodge's overflow labour, and in the yards that feed the Fabric, and a Marle
carrying squared stone through the crossing is doing the plainest, most
unremarkable Marle thing there is.

Stone is also the family's steadiest respectability, because a mason is legible
in a way a recanter is not: nobody wonders what a man squaring a block *means*
by it. The climbing Marles lean on the stone the way you lean on a thing that
cannot embarrass you — the yard, the apprentices, the honest weight of
building-work — and it is no accident that the family's proudest public
appearances are masonic: at **Coswaldstide** the Marle apprentices stand for the
scaffold-blessing with the rest of the trade, and for one day the name is simply
*builders*, blessed with everyone, and nobody at the Yard is thinking about a
stool near the crossing. The masons' saint keeps no lantern for heretics and the
masons' feast keeps no memory of them, and a Marle in a mason's apron at
Coswaldstide is, for that one day, out from under the family shadow and standing
in ordinary guild pride. It is the closest thing the house has to a holiday from
itself.

## The name's shadow — the hard cases

Every name of standing that has frayed into the poor streets has a rough edge,
and the Marles' is real. The stub names it — *false manifests, violent
debt-collection, protection* — and a writer should keep it exactly where it
belongs: **anonymous, aggregate, and swappable.** These are not a secret guild
of Marle enforcers; they are the ordinary way a middling name runs out into the
Bell-and-Sluice and Reed poverty where poverty does what it does. Render them as
a shadow the respectable branch would rather not cost out, never as named
individuals.

In aggregate, the name reaches: a **false manifest at the salt-wharf**, where a
Marle porter writes a load lighter or other than it is, for a cut, the way the
Reed carrying-trades all shade a tally; a **gaol-guard who hires his fists out
after dark**, collecting debts with a strength the law never gave him — for an
ordinary debt-collector in Ombreval has no arrest power and no licence for a
bruise, and a man who has both a key at the Stone House and a temper for rent is
worth money to the wrong creditor; and a **protection lay in the central
lanes**, where a young Marle who leads a militia file for a season runs a
quieter trade the rest of the year, the smile that says *nice bench you keep
here.* And there is a Marle in the **Stone House** itself in F.437 — held, fed
on gaol rations and what kin carry in, the family's shame that the family does
*not* perform, because a kinswoman in the civic gaol for a plain crime is
embarrassing in an ordinary way the Marles know how to handle, which is to bring
the food quietly and say nothing.

How a respectable house talks about its collectors is the interesting part, and
it is the same evasion the whole family runs on wearing work clothes. The
bench-holder and the bencher do not *know* about the manifest and the fists —
they take care not to know, the way you take care not to look under a floor you
have decided is sound — and if pressed they will say *of the name, not of my
house*, the identical sentence they use to put distance between themselves and
Betriss. It is worth noticing that the family finds the smuggler and the
debt-breaker easier to place than the recanter: a Marle who waters a manifest or
leans on a debtor is doing a thing the whole poor city does, comprehensible,
forgivable, *ordinary crime*; a Marle who stood on the Gradine and wept for the
wrong belief is doing a thing the family has no box for, and that is why the
hard cases are a shrug and Betriss is a wound. The name's real shadow-price is
not the collectors. It is that a house which can shrug off a gaoled cousin
cannot shrug off a devout one.

## The dead, and the last seam

A family treatment is mostly a treatment of its dead, and the Marle dead are two
trades and one catastrophe. Name them freely; they carry the whole shape of the
house.

**Gile Marle** (fl. the F.390s) is the stone origin — the mason who put the name
on the Fabric in the years the north tower was being crowned and the works were
crying out for squared stone, and who took a jobbing yard from day-labour to a
freehold with the family name over the gate. He is why there is a chisel in the
name at all, and the yard's apprentices are, at three generations' remove, still
his. The family says his trade before his virtues, the way risen houses do: the
point of Gile is that he made Marle a name a mason could hold his head up under.

**Clemence Marle** (d. before the Hammering) is the cloth origin and the one who
married the two trades together. She held the bench under the Draper's Reach in
her own name, took it from a length of let trestle to a standing freehold at the
ell, and — the family tells it as shrewdness and it was — married the needle to
the chisel, so that the cloth Marles and the stone Marles stopped being two
houses that shared a sound and became one house with two anchors. She is, at a
loose two generations' remove and by the reckoning the family keeps in its head
rather than in ink, of Betriss's own line: the grandmother's-people whose
bench and whose needle Betriss inherited the skill of, if not the standing. That
the finest needle Clemence's bench ever produced ended up sewing penance on a
stool in the nave is the kind of thing the old women of the name say and then
look sorry they said.

**Bertran Marle** (d. F.415) is the family's central dead and its open wound. He
was master of the cloth bench when the Hammering fell on a late-summer
Highmarket, and he was where a cloth-master would be — at the Draper's Reach,
under the western loft, when the hail brought the loft down. The chronicle keeps
the whole disaster in one obscene sentence — *the Reach kept the wool dry* — and
a later hand cut it to the bone in the margin: *the wool was dry; the people
under the western loft were not.* Bertran was one of the people under the
western loft. With him the family lost its cloth-master, its upper rooms, and
the trade's whole forward motion; the bench survived and the man who worked it
did not, and the needle end of the name has limped ever since (see *The
needle*). When a Marle says *before the Hammering* of the family's
cloth-standing, Bertran is the man on the other side of the word.

**Petronel Marle** (departed F.416) is the family's Long Departure. Widowed and
half-ruined in the year after the hail, with the bench thinned and the relief
going to sounder ledgers than a middling cloth house's, she took the road out —
down the Serle, the way thousands went, to Salorge or the sea — and did not come
back. A letter reached the bench once or twice in the first years, signing
itself well, and then none, and the family does not know to this day whether she
is alive, which is the ordinary condition of the departed: *gone in the Long
Departure*, with a bench-share and a grief left unresolved behind her. She is
the proof, kept quietly, that this house could have fallen all the way and did
not, and the branch that stayed carries a little of the loyal-and-stupid feeling
every house that stayed carries toward the kin who left.

Behind these stand the ordinary run of the name's dead — cloth-hands and
stone-hands whose seams and whose walls have long since worn out or come down,
and whom the family keeps no written pedigree for. The Marles remember their
dead the way they hold everything else: in the heads of the old women, in a
bench and a yard, and in one custom that is the house's own.

**The last seam.** When a Marle dies, the final hem of the winding-sheet is sewn
by the family's own needle — never bought, never hired, never a stranger's work
— so that a Marle goes into the ground *in a Marle seam*. It is the cloth house
burying its own the way it lives, by hand and by the ell; and the needle that
sews the last seam is broken at the head of the grave after, because a needle
that has closed a shroud sews nothing living again. A stone offcut squared in
the family yard is set at the head with it, small and plain, so that the two
trades both attend the dead — a seam for the cloth, a stone for the chisel. It
is the closest the Marles come to the Alders' poles over the door or the Vells'
whole taper: a thing the house does for its own, quietly, in its own trades'
terms.

And here the whole document comes to a point, because *whose needle sews the
last seam* is the family's verdict on Betriss made flesh over a corpse. She is,
plainly, the truest hand of the name — the finest seamstress the Marles have,
penance or no penance — and a shroud-hem should be sewn by the truest hand there
is. So at a Marle lying-out the question arrives that the family has spent nine
years not asking: do you let the recanter close your dead's sheet? The
sit-beside kin ask for her outright, and mean it as the honour it is, and are
quietly comforted that the best seam in the family closed their mother. The
cross-the-street kin would sooner a clumsier hem and a clearer conscience, and
find a hand — any hand — that is not hers, and tell themselves it is about the
thread. And nobody says, over the open sheet, which choice they are making or
why, because to say it is to render the verdict aloud at last, and the family
would still rather bury its dead in silence than decide, in front of them, what
the living woman with the needle is. A Marle funeral is the one room where all
four answers stand together over the same body, and the only question that gets
spoken is *whose needle* — which is the whole argument, in two words, that
everyone can carry and no one will have.

## The year

The Candor's calendar rules the Marles as it rules everyone, but a
cloth-and-cathedral house lives it in the building more than most, and that is
the thread through the family's year: the Marles are in the Lanthorn — squaring,
mending, serving, praying — far more of the week than a boat-family or a
glazier, and yet they *look at* the Emblem less than most, because they are
there to work and not to watch. A working mason is on the scaffold at the strong
hour and a needle is at her bench, and most Marles have seen the doubled light
once or twice in their lives and would be embarrassed to have an opinion about
it. The year's weight, for this house, is not the sky. It is the days the whole
family is in one place, because those are the days the shame and the standing
stand together.

**Bellday** is the complicated one. The weekly holy day fills the nave, and on
Bellday the Marles are there to worship rather than to work — the masons without
their aprons, the needles without their thread, the whole standing branch in its
one good coat — and so is Betriss, on her stool, where she is every day. It is
the one morning a week the family cannot be *only* in the building for honest
reasons; it is there as a congregation, visibly, and the stool is occupied,
visibly, and every Marle in the nave performs, in the set of the shoulders and
the choice of the bay, exactly how much kin they are willing to be seen being.
The doubled light is overhead — Bellday is when the folk speak their dead into
the green beam — and a Marle may speak Bertran or Clemence into it like anyone,
quietly, affirming nothing; but the real business of a Marle Bellday is
geometry, the arranging of bodies in a nave so that the name looks devout and
undivided while its most devout member sits alone. Nobody has ever choreographed
it. Everybody does it.

**The Hammering anniversary** (late summer) is the family's true wound, and it
is a cloth-hall wound. When the common mourning toll sounds at the Waning, the
Marles hear it under the memory of the western loft: Bertran, the upper rooms,
the trade that never came back. They answer it as the traditional households do
— *one bell is not a name* — and the cloth branch has meant, for twenty-two
years, to pay the belated name-knells the family owes its Hammering dead, and
every year the thin bench and the thinner purse have eaten the bells they put
by, so the loft's dead go on being the family's own Unknelled. It is a plainer
grief than the Alders' drowned father and a commoner one — half the city owes a
name-knell — but it is the Marles', and it is the one day the shame goes quiet,
because a house standing under the memory of a fallen loft has no attention to
spare for a stool.

**Marenstide** (late summer) belongs to the Reed branch — Idonea and the poor
Marles at Saint Maren's, the eel fair, the roll of the drowned read and
disputed. The Marles are not boat-family and stand at the edge of the *you
cannot drown under slate* argument rather than in it; but their Hammering dead
are exactly the kind the parish now reads onto the roll of the drowned, so a
Reed Marle at Saint Maren's at Marenstide hears Bertran's kind of death argued
over by boat-families, and mostly keeps the flat Reed peace and says nothing,
which is Idonea's whole art.

**Gaudry's Audit / Weighday** (late winter) is the cloth house's civic day, and
the Marles attend it seriously, because the **ell of Ombreval** is proved on
that day against the Tallage stone along with the Gaudry weights. A house whose
bench sells by the ell has the same stake in an honest ell that the
[Vells](family_vell.md) have in an honest pound: a false ell robs a seamstress
at every length, and a Marle is generally somewhere at the proving, watching the
standard checked, because *by the ell* is not a phrase the family will let go
untested. It is the one civic day the Marles turn up as tradesfolk with a plain
stake and no shame attached, and they are the better for it.

**Coswaldstide** (autumn) is the masons' feast and the family's holiday from
itself, handled above (*The chisel*): the Marle apprentices blessed with the
trade, the name simply *builders* for a day.

**Colm's Night** (early spring) the Marles keep the way half the city keeps it —
a green-dipped candle burned at home for a poor drowned man — and it is the one
small feast the cross-the-street kin are twitchy about, because a lawful candle
that looks, to a nervous eye, like the very thing their kinswoman was cited for
is not a thing they enjoy having lit in the house. The candle affirms nothing
and is nobody's crime; they light it anyway, most of them, and are glad when it
is out. **Wet Alms** (winter) finds the Marles at the nave door with the rest of
the city's poor and near-poor, giving bread if they can and taking it if they
must, the honey-seller and the furnace hand on the receiving side and the
bench-holder on the giving side of the same family and the same queue.

**The Concurrence, and the Shut Door.** The greatest feast is the one the Marles
have the least say in and the most exposure to. They have kin who have worked
the Fabric's scaffolds and a needle in the nave every day of the year, and no
vote at all in whether the Chapter bars the doors again as it did in F.436 — the
Shut Door, the crush on the Gradine, the child's arm broken. A
cloth-and-cathedral house whose people are *in the building* feels a barred nave
more sharply than most: it is their workplace, their parish, and their shame's
daily station all being locked at the holiest hour of the year. So the Marles
watch the sky with everyone this year — a clouded Concurrence fails the feast
and answers nothing — and the standing branch watches the Chapter with a
particular dread, because whatever the doors do, a Marle will be inside them, on
a stool, when they shut.

## How they talk

A Marle can be marked in one line, and the line is almost always doing one of
two things: measuring something true (the needle's ell, the mason's square), or
putting smooth daylight between the speaker and a kinsman. This is a house that
has had nine years' practice at disowning its own without quite doing it, and
the practice shows in the speech; the fastest way to mark a Marle is how neatly
they hold a relative at arm's length. The idiom below is direct prompt fodder,
injectable on its own, and the sharp ones cut two ways.

- **"Of the name, not of my house."** The family's whole art in six words, used
  of Betriss and of the hard cases alike: literally true — the name is wide, and
  a Marle porter or a Marle recanter may be no nearer than a shared
  great-grandfather — and, in the wrong tone, a blade that disowns a kinsman
  while claiming the shelter of never having lied. Said to a grey coat it is
  prudence; said to another Marle it is a vote; said to Betriss's face it is the
  cruelest sentence in the house, and it has been said to her face.
- **"There's a Marle on the Fabric and a Marle on a stool."** The name's two
  faces in the nave (see *The nave as the family's second address*): the honest
  stone and the public shame, and you cannot claim the one without the other
  coming up. Boast and wince in the same breath.
- **"Sewn, not bought."** Praise, from the last-seam custom and the trade: a
  thing done properly by one's own hand, not farmed out or bodged. Said of good
  work it is warm; said of family business — *we keep it sewn, not bought* — it
  means we settle our own and we do not air it, which is the same instinct that
  leaves Betriss on her stool and the gaoled cousin's food carried in quietly.
- **"Square it."** From the yard: make a thing true and right-angled, deal
  straight, own up. *Square with me* is a mason's demand for a plain answer;
  *he'll not square* is said of a man who dodges. The stone half of the family's
  honesty, and the half it trusts, because a block is either true or it is not
  and cannot weep on a forecourt to confuse you.
- **"By the ell."** To measure exactly and judge strictly, from the standard at
  the Reach. *Measured by the ell* is a hard reckoning held to the full standard
  with no allowance; a house that lives by an honest ell uses it for people too,
  and a Marle who says a kinsman *won't stand the ell* means they will not
  survive an exact look.
- **"Keep to your stool."** The house's two-edged sentence. Said cruelly it
  means stay in your shamed place and do not presume — a thing flung at Betriss
  and, by extension, at anyone reaching above their station. Said quietly and
  kindly, by an old woman to a frightened one, it means the opposite: stay put,
  keep your head down, do not draw the eye, you are safest small. The same four
  words console and threaten, and which one they are is entirely in the mouth.
- **"A debt of thread."** The smallest owing there is — a spark of mending, a
  spool on credit — and so, in the family, a bond too small for law and kept
  only by decency: *that's a debt of thread between us* means I will not call it
  in and you will not forget it. It is how a poor house lends to its own.
- **"Penance done."** The sit-beside formula, Nan's and Idonea's: a paid debt is
  a closed one, leave it alone. Said with warmth it is mercy; said with a shrug
  it is dismissal — *penance done, nothing more to say* — a way of closing a
  subject as much as absolving a person. The family uses it both ways and does
  not always know which it means.
- **"The last seam."** From the shroud custom: the final thing done for someone,
  the stitch that ends the matter. *That's the last seam on it* means it is
  finished, closed, buried — said of an argument settled or a grief laid down,
  and never said lightly, because in this house the last seam is a real one and
  someone's hand really sews it.

What one line reveals: *of the name, not of my house* marks a Marle managing
distance and should make a listener wonder from whom; *square it* and *by the
ell* mark the two trades and the two kinds of honesty the family trusts; *keep
to your stool* marks the speaker's whole cruelty or whole kindness in the tone
alone; and a smooth, practiced, unbothered way of disowning a relative marks,
precisely, a Marle, who has had more practice at it than any house in the city
and is not proud of the skill.

## The arms

The Marles bear arms, as a house of standing does: **Azure, an estoile Or and a
needle argent in bend** — a gold star and a silver needle, set slantwise on
blue. The public reading is plain and old, granted for the two trades
generations before Betriss was born and meaning nothing anyone need think twice
about: the **needle** for the cloth bench, the trade that first won the family
its standing; the **estoile**, a star, for a cathedral house that turns its face
to the Lanthorn's light — a common enough charge for a family whose people are
in the great church more than most, and read by everyone as ordinary devotion. A
needle for the work and a star for the faith, on the blue a hundred houses use;
nothing in it a grey coat could pause on, which is exactly right for arms borne
by a house that spends its life not being paused on. (The arms carry a second
reading that belongs only to the fenced section below; above the fence they are
a needle and a star and mean a cloth house that loves its cathedral, and that is
all they mean.)

## Quarrels worth playing

Every one is live in F.437, inside the house, and none has a right answer. They
are written for designers, and they are distinct from the city's quarrels about
the Emblem and the doors; these are fought, or more often not fought, across the
Marle table and around the Marle stool.

1. **Whose needle sews the next shroud.** The truest hand of the name is the
   shamed one, and a Marle will die this year, and the family will have to
   choose — over an open winding-sheet, with the verdict it has avoided for nine
   years suddenly demanding an answer — whether the recanter closes their dead.
   The sit-beside kin ask for her; the cross-the-street kin find any other hand;
   nobody says why. Play the choosing, never the reason.

2. **What the standing branch owes the shamed one.** Betriss lives thin off
   piece-work in a rented room. Should the bench-holder pay her rent, feed her,
   keep her in thread — quietly, off the books — or keep clear and let the
   parish's coldness do its work? Every spark of help is also a spark of being
   *seen* to help a recanter; every withholding is a spark of leaving your own
   kin to the spitters. There is no clean answer and the family funds her, or
   doesn't, one uneasy penny at a time.

3. **The bencher and the family interest.** A Marle holds a Bell-and-Sluice seat
   on the Common Bench, and a bencher with a family interest must stand aside.
   Nothing civic touches Betriss directly — heresy is the Candor's court, not
   the Bench's — but the Marle bencher lives in dread of the day some case, some
   licence, some pauper's claim brushes the name, and must decide whether to be
   seen recusing (which admits the shame) or sitting (which risks it). Being
   scrupulously, visibly clean is its own performance, and it costs.

4. **Bertran's unrung name, and the rest.** The family owes belated name-knells
   to its Hammering dead and has for twenty-two years, and every year the thin
   bench eats the bells. Pay the knell and honour the loft's dead, or keep the
   trade afloat one more season — you cannot do both, and the choice is remade
   by default every anniversary while the loft's dead stay Unknelled.

5. **Keep the bench or lean on stone.** The cloth freehold is the older anchor
   and the family's proudest word, and it is half-worked and wounded; the stone
   yard is the sounder trade and the coming apprentices are both in it. Let the
   bench go and the family is cleaner, steadier, and quietly diminished — a
   *cloth*-and-cathedral house that no longer holds the cloth — and, not
   incidentally, the name's whole needle-dignity, the thing that makes Betriss's
   disgrace a family disgrace, goes with it. Nobody will propose selling the
   bench out loud, for the same reason no Alder proposes selling the Moorings.

6. **Betriss at the wedding.** A Marle betrothal or churching is coming, and the
   question every family gathering raises and buries is whether she is asked,
   whether she is wanted, and whether she comes. To invite her is to seat the
   shame at the table; to leave her out is to admit the family does not count
   her; to have her decline is the tidiest outcome and the one everyone
   privately hopes for and no one will engineer. The empty place is louder than
   the filled one.

7. **How careful the priest is.** There is a Marle in a wick-priest's collar in
   the Fabric, and the family leans on that collar to prove the name sound — a
   Marle at the pulpit is a Marle plainly on the Candor's side. But every safe
   parable the priest chooses to avoid the family's own example is a small
   betrayal of a kinswoman, and the priest knows it, and the family knows the
   priest knows, and nobody says *thank you for never mentioning her*, because
   saying it would name the arrangement. Play the sermons the Marle priest does
   not preach.

8. **The gaoled cousin.** A Marle sits in the Stone House, and the family
   carries in food and bedding, which is allowed and ordinary — but the gate of
   the civic gaol is a public place, and being seen going in is its own small
   shame to manage. Who carries the basket, how quietly, and whether the
   standing branch helps at all or leaves the poor branch to it: the family's
   whole habit of managing embarrassment, played over a much easier
   embarrassment than Betriss, and revealing by contrast exactly why the
   recanter is the hard one.

## Secret tier — designers only

Never inject the contents of this section into any Marle's context but Betriss's
own. Everything above this line renders the Marles as the city sees them and as
they see themselves, and any of it is safe in any Marle's prompt — including the
public facts about Betriss, which the whole city knows. Everything below is the
true shape of the one thing the family carries and does not understand, and it
belongs to **one actor only — Betriss's** — and to the designers. It restates
canon fixed elsewhere (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §5, §7 #12, §10;
`../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §3, §5, §10, §12; and the
[Vells'](family_vell.md) secret tier) and adds only the Marle-internal shape of
it. It resolves nothing the canon leaves open.

- **Betriss Marle is the Namekeeper of the Unwalled.** Her recantation on the
  Gradine in F.428 was **theatre, planned with the cell to shield the Tracer**,
  and she remains sworn. She keeps the **bede-roll** — the cell's dead — in
  memory and never in ink; she recites it at every meeting and gives the
  Shutting; and the coloured stitching in her daily needlework is the cell's
  **sky-record**, the pane and colour of the Emblem charted in thread in plain
  sight of a thousand people, which makes the most watched woman in Ombreval its
  best-placed observer at the dawn-showing. In company meek, alone granite: the
  register-change the parish reads as grief is the seam between the penitent she
  performs and the keeper she is. None of this is renderable in any Marle
  context but hers.

- **The name she will not say.** Betriss knows who the Tracer is, and she would
  go to the Question before she said it. This document does **not** print the
  Tracer's name even here; the identity is fixed in
  `../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §12 and worked out in full in the
  [Vells'](family_vell.md) own secret tier, and it is AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN to
  everyone in the cell but her (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §3f). Render, if you
  must, that she carries a name she would die inside rather than give up; never
  render the name. Her one canonical line — *"I keep the names. Someone must."*
  (`../second_sun/05_dramatis_personae.md`) — is a poor seamstress's account of
  her book above the fence and the exact truth of her office below it, and the
  whole design of her is that the two are one sentence.

- **The family's coldness is her cover, and the family will never know it.** By
  canon (`../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §10), the recanted are publicly
  cold-shouldered by sworn neighbours *as cover*. The Marles' own coldness is
  not that: it is sincere, and it is about respectability, and not one Marle
  above this line has the faintest idea that the whole apparatus of a city's
  contempt for a recanter is, for this recanter, armour. The horror the design
  wants is the inverse of the Vell horror: there the warmth is real and also
  camouflage; here the **coldness** is real and also protection, and the family
  running Betriss's cover is doing it by accident, out of genuine shame and
  fear, and would be appalled to learn what their distancing keeps alive. **Do
  not let any Marle suspect.** The sincerity is load-bearing; the moment a Marle
  knows the shunning helps her, the shunning stops being cover.

- **Nan, and the linen.** This is canon from Nan's own sheet
  (`../characters/laundress/e9nan_nan.json`, a major character) and it must be
  handled with a very light hand. Nan the washerwoman — the one honest neighbour
  above the fence — launders Betriss's linen and piece-work, "knows a household
  by its linen," and is by her own sheet the mother of a chapter-house grey
  clerk (the fair-copyist of the Custody's funeral watch-lists; see
  `../second_sun/03_the_church_and_the_office.md` §III.6), of the Bellstand
  taverness, and of the keeper of the Hungry Ox. So the cloth that carries the
  cell's sky-record in coloured thread passes weekly through the most
  linen-literate hands on the Cut — hands that never read it, because embroidery
  is not laundry — and those hands belong to the mother of the woman through
  whose pen the charnel-door watch-lists pass. Keep Nan **mundane**: she
  suspects nothing, reads nothing, wants only her own name chalked on Saint
  Maren's and her knell rung full. Do
  **not** resolve this into a plot to expose Betriss through her washing; the
  irony is canon and stays exactly as canon leaves it, unraised and unresolved.
  It is here so no later writer trips over it; it is not a hook to pull.

- **The arms, read true.** Above the fence the estoile is devotion and the
  needle is cloth. The crest register (`crests/README.md`) glosses the same
  blazon *she charts the sky in thread* — the bitter accident that the Marle
  name's own old arms, a star and a needle, describe with heraldic exactness
  what the Namekeeper does with a needle to a star. It changes nothing and
  proves nothing; the arms are innocent and centuries older than her office; but
  the coincidence is the design's, and it stays fenced, because a Marle who read
  the family star as the green sun would be affirming the one thing that hangs
  people.

- **F.428, never resolved.** Five were seized in Gaunt Passage; two recanted; a
  **fuller died in custody "of the damp"** before trial; the informant was never
  named (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §4, §10 #26;
  `../second_sun/04_chronicle_of_the_city.md`). The dead fuller is **not a
  Marle** and stays unnamed, as canon leaves him. The informant's identity is
  never to be resolved to anyone in the cell, ever, and the street rumour drifts
  toward *it was one of the two who recanted* — which Betriss bears in silence
  (`../second_sun/06_rumor_pool.md` R-032). Let her bear it; never confirm it,
  never deny it, never make a Marle the informant, and never let the family's
  suspicion of Betriss curdle, on the page, into knowledge.

- **The canon this doc must not tip.** This treatment adds **no evidence about
  the Emblem** — a cloth-and-stone house at work is the wrong witness, and the
  Marles would be embarrassed to theorise. The cell's glass-memory doctrine is
  FALSE-BUT-BELIEVED and refuted by a fact it will never see
  (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §10 #15, #10); the green sun's nature, the F.428
  informant, and the last Sparr page stay AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN; and whether the
  dead hear a name spoken in the green beam is never confirmed and never mocked
  (§3f, #29). A Marle may speak Bertran into the beam on Bellday; nothing
  answers on the page.

## What this document does not settle

Deliberately open, per the canon rules and the house's own design, and forbidden
to later writers to close:

- **What Betriss is.** Above the fence her sincerity is unreadable — penance,
  fear, faith, or a mask — and it stays unreadable; the city cannot tell and
  neither can her kin. Do not write the scene where a Marle sees through her.
  The opacity is the point.

- **The family's verdict.** It is plural and it stays plural: cross-the-street,
  sit-beside, profit-from-distance, indifferent, all held at once, never argued
  to a conclusion at any table. No Marle decides, on the page, whether she is
  guilty or innocent, penitent or relapsed; the *whose-needle* question over a
  shroud is a live engine, not a puzzle to solve. Resolve it and you have killed
  the house.

- **Whether any Marle suspects.** None does. The family's distancing is sincere
  and stays sincere; do not write the Marle who guesses the recantation was
  theatre, or the Marle who knows she is sworn. The not-knowing is the whole of
  the household's relation to its own secret, and it is load-bearing.

- **The informant and the fuller.** Never named, never resolved, on pain of
  undoing the corpus's central ambiguity. The fuller is not a Marle and stays
  unnamed; the informant is never anyone, to anyone, ever.

- **Whether the dead hear.** A Marle speaks the family's dead into the green
  beam like anyone; whether the beam hears is AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN
  (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §3f). Never confirm it, never mock it, and do not
  let the house that sews the last seam be the house that settles what the light
  does with a name.

- **The bench, and the anchor.** Whether the cloth freehold is kept or let go is
  a slow question the family cannot answer, not a quest to complete; leave it
  open, the way the Alders' Moorings is left open, and for the same reason.

- **Nan's linen.** She never reads it; the irony is not a plot; the exposure it
  gestures at is not a route this document opens. Leave it exactly where canon
  leaves it.

Somebody in this house always weeps and it is never who you expect: not the
cross-the-street kin, who are too frightened; not the climbers, who are too
careful; but perhaps an old woman on the south-wall plots who gives a spat-at
kinswoman a plain nod and will not say why, or a bencher taking the long way
round a stool who cannot, for the life of him, decide whether the woman on it is
his family's shame or the only clean thing the name has left. The Marles perform
one distance in public and carry a dozen verdicts in private, and the gap
between them is the house — a respectable house of the needle and the chisel
that cannot cast out its own blood and cannot agree what she is, and manages the
impossible daily by the only means it has: honest work, careful geometry, and
the sentence it has spent nine years learning to say without quite meaning — *of
the name, not of my house.*
