## The thing to get right first

**The Alders are not a position. They are a silence.**

Every writer who meets this family through the ward doc meets them as the house
that *kept the boat* — the proud last house of standing on the Green, the one
answer in a ward of answers, cash-poor and land-rich and coming quietly apart
over whether to sell the yard. All of that is true and none of it is the thing
to get right. The thing to get right is that **the argument the ward thinks the
Alders are having, the Alders have never once had.** Nobody has said the true
sentence at that table. Ewart says his only in drink, half a mile from the
table, and unsays it by never taking it back. Wyn has never answered him aloud,
and the family has agreed that her silence is a yes. Tobin has told the one
thing he wants to a fifteen-year-old girl and to no one who shares his name.
Rohese says nothing has changed and rakes the evidence into a bucket every
morning. Four people, four answers, and the house runs on the gap between them.

The load-bearing fact, from which the rest of this document follows:

> **The Moorings roof is a patch of three campaigns and everyone who works under
> it knows which bays are new, and no one says so; the household is built the
> same way, out of things known and not said, and it holds for exactly the same
> reason.** Say the wrong true sentence under either roof and something comes
> down.

This is not dysfunction to be diagnosed and healed. It is the structure. An
Alder who spoke plainly would not be braver; he would be the man who pulled the
one board that was holding the others. The family has organised itself, across
three generations, so that the sentences that would end it can be carried by
everyone and spoken by no one — the way the soundings are carried in four heads
and written in none, the way a dead man's name is spoken only in a beam where
the street cannot hear it. **The Alders keep their most important things the way
boatmen keep a channel: unmarked, in the memory, and only ever handed over in
private.**

Derive everything from that. The soundings taught by walking, never written. The
name that passes without anyone deciding it should. The pole taken back at the
churchyard gate and hung over the door where it says, without a word, how many
the house has buried. The one name Wyn will speak in the nave and nowhere else.
A house of standing that is, on the inside, a set of silences with a roof over
them.

## The question under the question

The ward's question was *do we keep the boat?* The Alders answered it — kept —
and have not been able to stop paying for the answer since, which is why the
ward reads them as the house that cannot let go. On the surface, the house's own
version of the question is narrower and crueller: **do we sell the Moorings,
now, while a proud name still fetches a price on a falling market?** That is the
sentence in Ewart's mouth at the Hungry Ox. It is a real question with no right
answer, and this document never resolves it. Do not resolve it. The four answers
are meant to stay four.

But under *that* question sits the one no Alder will put into words at all, the
true engine of the house:

> **Who is any of this for — and was anyone ever asked?**

Every inheritance in this family was handed down without consent, and the grief
of the house, never named as grief, is that no living Alder can remember being
asked whether they wanted the thing they carry. Wyn was not asked: she took the
fore-pole at twelve because her father was dead under the roof and her brother
had frozen, and no one has thought to ask her since whether she would have
chosen the water. Tobin is not asked, because his mother already knows the
answer is the boat and so the question never forms. Ewart asks, once a night, in
the only way he can — that somebody say out loud that he was right — and nobody
ever does. Rohese offered the one thing that was hers to offer, the sluice
keeping, to the son who wanted none of it, and has not forgiven him, and he has
not forgiven her, and the offer is the wound. The pole, the name, the keys, the
soundings, the debt: all of it moves down the generations like freight down the
current, and the family's whole unspoken labour is to keep anyone from noticing
that it was loaded onto them while they slept.

Hold both questions in any Alder scene. The one they perform is *sell or keep*.
The one they are actually answering, and cannot, is *was I asked*.

## What "Alder" anchors, and why it is running out

A fixed family name in Ombreval is held, not owned: it lasts exactly as long as
the property that anchors it, and when the property goes the street stops giving
the name to the children (`../the_dry_boatmen.md`, "the fallen";
`../core_lore/naming_language.md`). The Alders are the last boat-family on
the Green for whom the name still has something under it. There are two somethings,
and a writer should keep them separate, because the family does.

**The Moorings.** The **Alder Moorings** is the anchor of standing: a dry
warehouse court south-east of Maren's Green, a broad arched cart gate, timber
galleries, loft doors, a hoist, rope racks, canvas repair, a rough ledger table,
and the iron rings — forged to hold boats — that now tie handcarts and dogs.
This is the property the name is pinned to, and it is a **dry warehouse with a
proud name**, which is precisely Ewart's whole point: the thing that makes them
a house of standing is a shed the river walked away from three generations ago.
It still earns (see "The Moorings, bay by bay"), but it earns thinly, and a
proud thin thing on a falling market is the most sellable object in the world.

**The gatehouse.** The second anchor is humbler and older and almost nobody
outside the family counts it as an Alder thing at all: the keeping of the **Old
Sluice**, the dry stone gatehouse that closes the east end of the Cut, over in
the Bell-and-Sluice streets a good walk from the Green. The Alder sluice-branch
has kept that gate for roof and pennies since long before the river was moved —
since the days when the Cut still ran and an Alder hooked the drowned preacher
Colm out of the water beneath it (F.301). The keeping passes on a dead man's
keys and is worth, in F.437, a roof and eight sparks a week. It is not standing.
It is the family's foothold at the place where their oldest dead is, and it is
kept now by a widow who married into the name and outlived the man who carried
it.

So the name sits on a yard that the river left and a gate that guards nothing,
and the whole social weight of "Alder" — the last boat of standing, the crest
with the boat on the wavy base, the seat the ward half-expects the house to hold
— rests on two dry buildings and a memory of water. Everyone in the family knows
this. It is the first of the things they do not say.

The Alders are, in one line for a prompt: **proud, cash-poor, land-rich in
property nobody can use as it was built to be used, and holding a name that is
worth more than anything they could sell and less than they owe.** The ward's
saying does the arithmetic for them, and they hate it, and it is exact:

> **There are more Hawsers than Alders.**

They are not wrong to be afraid of it. It has already happened once inside this
very house, to a son who grew up at the gate — but that is later in the
document, and the family would not thank me for putting it first.

## The four at the table

The ward doc gives you the four Alders as four answers, seen from the Green.
This is the same four from the near side of the table, where the answers are not
positions in an argument but things each of them is doing instead of saying. A
prompt-builder should take the ward doc's line for what the Green believes about
an Alder and this section for what the Alder is not telling the Green.

Their sheets are canon and richer than any summary; read them
(`gw4ld_wyn_alder`, `g4ewt_ewart_alder`, `g7tbn_tobin_alder`,
`hrosl_rohese_of_the_sluice`, `hansl_ansel_of_the_sluice`). What follows adds
the house around them, not a word against them.

### Wyn (34) — the one who answers with silence

Wyn is the best boatwoman on the Serle and has been since she took the fore-pole
at twelve, in the season after the Moorings roof came down in the Hammering and
killed her father and her elder brother could not lift the pole and she could.
That is the founding fact of her, and she has never been asked about it, and she
has never offered. She is on the water before the Kindling bell, poling freight
and eel-traps beyond the south wall, and back in the yard by mid-morning with
wet rope in her hands, calling her price across the Green in a voice that
carries because it never rises. She talks the way she sounds a depth: level,
short, weather first, nothing wasted. It is the most trusted voice in the ward
and the least forthcoming, and the two facts are the same fact.

The centre of her, for a writer, is that **her most consequential answers are
silences she lets the family read for her, and she knows exactly what they will
read.** When Ewart says at the Ox that the yard should be sold, Wyn does not
argue and does not agree; she says nothing, and the family has decided the
nothing means yes — and she lets them decide it, because a spoken *no* would be
a promise to fight for the Moorings, and she is not sure she has the fight, and
a spoken *yes* would be the end of her father's yard in her own mouth, and she
will not have that either. So she gives them the silence and lets them make of
it the answer she cannot afford to say. It is the single most Alder thing anyone
in the house does.

She does it to her son as well, and this is the quiet tragedy the sheet names
without flinching: **it has not occurred to her to ask Tobin what he wants,
because to her the answer is the boat and needs no asking.** She loves him the
way you love the person who takes the fore-pole and never complains; she has
never once looked at the not-complaining and wondered what it costs him. If
anyone told her the boy dreams of standing still in eel-smoke with warm hands,
she would not be angry. She would be *lost*, in a way she has no practice for,
because it would mean the one inheritance she was sure was a gift is another
thing loaded onto a sleeping child.

What she wants is plain and impossible and exactly the shape of her trade: a
winter that stays cold without freezing the river, and the Moorings kept whole.
Cold to keep the eels and the debts down; not so cold the Serle locks and the
work stops and Averil Skell's book gets longer. She wants, in other words, for
nothing to have to be decided — for the weather to hold and the roof to hold and
the question to stay unasked one more year. She is the household's real power,
the woman at the table with the price in her head, and her power is entirely a
power of *not saying*, which is why the family cannot move and cannot fall.

She keeps one thing that is only hers and not the family's silence but her own:
on Bellday she stands in the nave with the rest of the Alders and speaks her
dead into the green beam, the way the folk do it, one name after another — and
one of the names she speaks there she would not say aloud in the street. It is
Colm's. The Alders have counted the drowned preacher among their own dead since
one of them hooked his body out of the sluice, and Wyn says his name to the
light with the others; but said with reverence in the open, Colm's name is
Attergate talk and citable, so it stays in the beam, at the family's own
hearth-level, spoken where the Custody's word for the thing is *affirm nothing*
and a grey coat lets it pass. She would tell you, if you asked her on the water,
that the sky can mind its own business and the Serle knows one sun. Both are
true. She still says the name.

### Ewart (49) — the one who says it in the wrong room

Ewart is Wyn's elder half-brother, older by fifteen years, and the whole shape
of him is that he has spent twenty-two of those years being the second-best
boatman in his own house and knowing it. He poles the Brede run — three days up
against the current, a day and a half down with the wool to the gunwales — and
has since he was fifteen; his shoulders are finished, his hip talks to him from
the Kindling bell on in wet weather, and he comes home from every trip a shade
sourer than he went out. Sober, he says almost nothing. In drink, at Bertran's
Hungry Ox, he tells the whole room the one true sentence the family cannot have
in the house: that the Moorings should be sold to some Tallage broker while the
name still fetches a price, that the water left that yard **seventy years ago**,
before his father was born, and that three generations of Alders have been too
stubborn to notice.

By morning he is ashamed of it, and he never once takes it back — because taking
it back would mean it was a lie, and it is not a lie, it is the truest thing he
knows, and the shame is not that he said it but that no one has ever answered
him.

That is the key to Ewart, and it is easy to miss under the drink and the
grievance: **what he wants is not to sell the yard. What he wants is for
somebody in that yard to say, out loud, once, that he was right.** He would take
the Moorings standing over his own head if Wyn would just once say *you have a
point, brother, and I am afraid of it too*. She never will, because to say it is
to open the question, and so the two of them are locked — she in the silence
that means yes, he in the yes he can only shout where she is not — and neither
can reach the other across a distance of half a mile and one sentence.

He carries a second thing the family does not name, and it is in his blood: his
mother was a **Hawser**, one of the fallen rope-poor, dead young. Ewart is
half-Alder and half-fallen-name, and he has watched, in his mother's people, the
whole arc the ward's saying threatens — a boat-family that lost its boat and
became a byname and a man picking rope on Tanners' Slip. When he says *sell
while it fetches a price*, the voice underneath is not greed; it is the Hawser
in him, who has seen where the road goes and would rather leave the Moorings on
his own terms than watch his sister's grandchildren become Hawsers on someone
else's. He would never say this either. He is not sure he has the words for it.
He owes Averil Skell four bells at a spark on the bell and crosses the Green the
long way to keep clear of her smoke, which everybody including Averil thinks is
funny, and which is also, if you watch him do it, a man practising in small the
thing he is most afraid of: owing more than he can carry and being seen at it.

The one place Ewart is soft is with the boy. Tobin takes the fore-pole beside
him on the Brede run and never complains, and Ewart is gentler with him than
with any living soul, and would be quietly, fiercely glad if the boy grew up
nothing like him. He does not know that Tobin is half-sick and half-grateful
every time the shouting starts at the Ox — sick because it is his home being
sold in a tavern, grateful because Ewart is the only Alder saying out loud that
the boat is a weight a person might be allowed to put down. The uncle and the
nephew are, without either knowing it, the two people in the house who most want
Tobin let off the water, and they have never said a word of it to each other.

### Tobin (17) — the one who has told the wrong person

Tobin has had a pole in his hands since he was nine and he is good with it —
quick feet, cold hands, he feels a rope go wrong before it goes — and he has
never once said aloud that he hates the water. He is seventeen, dutiful, and so
quiet that words come out of him slowly and sideways, because in his family a
man's answer to a question is to get on with the work, and he was raised by the
best practitioner of that answer alive. He does the thing before he is told it
twice. He is the fourth generation of the fore-pole and he wants, with his whole
chest, to be a smoke-house man.

The thing he wants is small and warm and completely disqualifying: the eel
smoke-sheds behind the fish hall, a trade that stays still under him, hands warm
for once instead of cold on a wet pole before dawn. It is the
[Skell](family_skell.md) answer — the trade that stayed inside the wall —
dreamed by an Alder boy, which is to say it
is the exact sentence that would tell his mother the boat was never a gift and
his uncle that the yard should indeed be let go and would, said once at that
table, split the house in two. So he has told it to nobody who shares his name.
He has told it to one person: a fifteen-year-old girl who strings eels at her
mother's racks, who calls him a coward, kindly, which is the only name he likes
being called. They are walking out, and both houses have agreed not to see it;
they meet under the Eel Bridge stair, he lies about it badly at home, and he
shows off on a wet gunwale whenever he knows she is watching from the Green. She
is the only person he has ever said the true thing to, and she is not an Alder,
and that is not a coincidence — the true thing can only be said outside the
name.

Keep the girl unnamed in anything injectable; she is the keeper of Tobin's one
secret and the doc protects her the way he does. What matters for the house is
the *shape* of Tobin's silence: he is the third generation running to carry a
sentence that would end the family and to carry it alone rather than be the one
who spoke it. His mother did it with the sale; his uncle does it with the drink;
Tobin does it with the sheds. He says nothing, and poles, and counts the days to
the eel fair, when the smoke fills the Green and for one week he can stand in
the thing he wants and call it a holiday.

### Rohese (61) — the one who says nothing has changed

Rohese married into this family and outlived the man who carried the name, and
she is not, by her own byname, an Alder at all: she is **Rohese of the Sluice**,
and she kept her own name at marriage as every woman does. Her husband left her
his keys and his debts, and she took the keys. She keeps the dry gatehouse at
the Old Sluice for a roof and eight sparks a week — four loaves and no fire —
and she has not owned a new thing in nine years. All day she sweeps the arch and
chases pilgrims off the sill-stones, worn round as soap and greasy in a rain,
before one of them cracks a skull and blames the city; and then she tells them,
for nothing, the tale her daughter would charge them a penny to hear — that the
channel ran through this arch once, that the preacher Colm drowned in it, that
the water has been gone from here longer than she has kept the gate.

She is dry, short, and thoroughly unimpressed. Sixty-one years within three
streets of that arch, and her considered opinion is that nothing in Ombreval has
changed since she was a girl and nothing is going to — an opinion she holds in
the teeth of a city that lost two-thirds of its people and a river that moved
outside the wall, and holds precisely *because* of those things: if nothing has
changed, then nothing was lost, then the man under the Moorings roof is only
away on a long run. Her daughter Wyn knows better and lets her think it. It is
the one silence in the house that runs *upward*, the young protecting the old
from the truth, and it is the gentlest thing the Alders do.

Rohese keeps one record and it is the loneliest object in the family. Every
night she chalks a single stroke on the doorpost for whoever goes through the
sluice arch after the Snuffing — a tally she has kept for forty years, since she
was the keeper's young wife, and no one has ever asked to read it. She would
tell them the plain truth of it if they did: it is not a secret, it is not a
code, it is a woman counting the people who still pass a gate that guards
nothing, on a road to a river that is not there. It is the whole of her life in
chalk, and its whole meaning is that she was here and paying attention. She
rakes the green-dipped candle-stubs out of the dry grate the morning after
Colm's Night, as her husband did and his father did, and asks no questions about
the second purse that finds its way to a sluice-keeper who has learned to see
nothing (see "The secret tier").

She has two living children and a complicated grief about each. Wyn brings her
eel and does not stay — the daughter who took the boat and the name and is out
on the water before the gate is even open. And Ansel, her son, cries strays and
sales in four squares of the city and would not take the keeping that fed them
both, and on a bad morning Rohese cannot tell whether that makes her proud of
the boy or insulted past speaking, which is the subject of the next section,
because it is the sharpest un-had argument in the whole house.

### Ansel of the Sluice (31) — the Alder who became a byname

Here is the thing the family will not put first and a writer must understand:
**the ward's cruellest rule has already run its course once inside the last
house of standing, and it took Rohese's son.** Ansel is her boy; he grew up at
the gatehouse, on the eight sparks and the four loaves; and he is not called
Ansel Alder. He is **Ansel of the Sluice** — a byname of the place, a fallen
name, the mark of a house that lost its hold, worn by a living son of the Alder
line while his sister keeps the surname and the boat. Nobody decreed it. It is
lived custom: the name of standing goes to the child who takes up the thing that
anchors it, and Ansel took up neither the boat nor the gate. He took the small
cries of the city instead.

Ansel is a proclamation crier — an under-crier, strictly, carrying the strays
and sales and lost children and the hue and cry that Jos Brant has neither the
hours nor the throat for, working the Wickmarket, Coswald's Yard, the Tallage
and Maren's Green while Brant walks his grand circuit of the five squares with
the edicts and the crowd that loves him. Ansel has known since he was twenty
that he has the better voice and the worse pitch. He learned the carrying tone
as a boy, shouting pilgrims off his mother's sluice-gate — the pause, the word
held one beat past comfortable, the trick of making a fish-market look up —
which means the one thing he took from the family gate is the thing he uses to
stay away from it. Off the pitch he is careful, courteous, and a shade too
formal, a man who has taught himself to be liked on purpose and knows it.

The wound between him and his mother is the household's clearest un-had argument
and its best scene. Rohese offered him the sluice keeping — the roof, the eight
sparks, the keys, the tally, the gate that fed them — and he would not take it,
and **she has never forgiven that, and neither has he.** From her side: she
offered the one thing that was hers to give and he refused it as beneath him,
chose to shout other people's news in other people's squares over keeping his
own dead father's gate. From his side: she offered him a life of four loaves and
no fire and a doorpost of chalk marks no one reads, called it an inheritance,
and was hurt when he wanted more than to slowly become the gate. Both are right.
Both have decided the other did the unforgivable thing. And because they are
Alders — because Ansel learned his silence at the same gate Wyn learned hers —
the whole enormous quarrel has never once been spoken. He brings it nowhere. She
rakes it into the bucket with the candle-stubs. It is the sale argument again,
in miniature and in the previous generation: an inheritance offered without
asking whether it was wanted, refused without saying why, and carried by both of
them in silence for the rest of their lives.

For a writer: Ansel is the future the Moorings is afraid of, already arrived and
walking around the city with a good voice. He is proof that the name can fall
off a living Alder in a single generation and land on a gate. When Ewart says
*sell before we become Hawsers*, he does not mention Ansel, and he does not have
to; a son born to this house already cries sales in the Wickmarket under a
place-name and not the surname, and every Alder at the table knows it, and no
one says *that*, either.

## The Moorings, bay by bay

The ward doc tells you what the Alder Moorings *is*: a dry warehouse court whose
hoist was built to take things off a boat that will never come. This is what it
is like to stand in it, and how a dry shed still earns its keep, because a
writer setting a scene here needs the yard to be a working place and not a
monument — though it is also, quietly, a reliquary, and the family works in it
without ever deciding which.

You come in through the **broad arched cart gate** on the Green side, wide
enough for a laden handcart and built for a good deal more; there is a second,
narrower **service door into Eelback Alley** at the back, used for morning
loading before the market wakes and for the family to come and go without
crossing the Green, which matters more than loading does. The court is packed
earth and worn paving. Down both long sides run the **timber galleries**, two
storeys of open loft fronted by rail and loft-door, reached by ladder-stair; the
**hoist** stands at the gable, a jib and a wheel and a good rope, and it still
lifts — it lifts the second lift now, the one that used to be the family's
first, hauling into the loft the same baskets that once came straight off a deck
below it. The **rope racks**, the **canvas-repair bench** with its
palm-and-needle and its bolt of patch-duck, the poles standing in their corner
like a stand of pikes, the **rough ledger table** by the gate where the price is
called and the book is kept: this is the working furniture, and every piece of
it was made to serve a water-gate that is three generations dry.

And the **iron rings.** They are set in the court walls at boat-height, forged
to take a mooring line, and they moor nothing; they tie handcarts and dogs, and
once a year at the eel fair they take the rope that strings the lanterns across
the yard. A stranger reads them as decoration. The family reads them as the
level the water stood at, which is why an Alder never quite looks at them.

**What a dry warehouse earns.** The yard makes its thin living four ways, and a
writer can hang a debt, a dispute, or a day's work on any of them:

- **Dry holding.** Freight landed at the outer wharf and carted in is held in
  the Moorings, dry and watched, until the broker or the cloth hall or the
  market wants it — wool waiting on a price, salt off the Salorge barges waiting
  on a buyer, eel-baskets between the smoke and the sale. The Alders charge
  storage by the tun by the week and live on it. A dry warehouse still earns
  because the city still needs somewhere out of the rain to *keep* a thing
  between two lifts, and keeping is the one river-trade the Alders can still do
  without a river.
- **The second lift itself.** The hoist and the loft-doors mean a broker can
  have his tun lifted, stored, and lifted again to a cart, all in one yard. The
  Alders take the fee for it — and it is the bitterest fee they earn, because it
  is the dry carry's money, the exact profit the diversion moved off their deck
  and onto everyone's cart, and they now scramble for a share of it in their own
  gate.
- **Rope and canvas.** Wyn comes off the water with wet rope in her hands for a
  reason: the yard splices, whips, tars, and re-canvases, for itself and for
  hire. It is close, patient, indoor work, and it is the one corner of the trade
  that looks a little like the smoke-sheds Tobin dreams of, which is a thing
  nobody has noticed out loud.
- **The fair.** At Marenstide the Moorings rents its floor and its rings to the
  eel fair — stall-room, lantern-lines, a dry place to leave baskets overnight —
  and for one week the yard is full and loud and paying, and the family pretends
  not to notice that the best week of its year is the week it is a fairground
  and not a wharf.

**The roof of three campaigns.** The single most injectable object in the yard.
The Moorings roof came down in the Hammering of F.415 and killed, among others,
Wyn and Ewart's father. It has been put back in three goes and everyone who
works under it knows which bays are new: the ragged emergency span of board and
tar-canvas thrown up in the first bad autumn, when there was no money and no
choice; the honest slate patch bought years later when there was; and the cheap
shingle bay nobody is proud of, done in a lean year with what a timber-yard
would sell on credit. The seams show from the floor. In hammer-weather — a dark,
abruptly cold summer sky — the family drifts, without a word, to stand under the
old sound bays and not the new, and a stranger who stands under a new bay is
marked at once as someone who does not know the house.

There is one bay nobody discusses at all, and it is the family's deepest quiet
thing after the soundings. It is the bay that came down on Corin Alder. What is
kept under it now is a designer's choice and a good scene either way: the least
valuable freight in the yard, always, so that nothing worth grieving is stored
where the man was lost — or, in another reading, always the *family's own* gear,
the spare poles and the fair-lanterns, because the Alders will not rent out the
place their father died and will not leave it empty either. Both are true to the
house. What is not in doubt is that no Alder stands in that bay in a summer
storm, and no Alder has ever said why, and the loft-door above it is the one
Rohese looks up at when Wyn brings her to the Green, which is not often.

## The Brede run, and the arithmetic of the wound

Ewart's run is the family's remaining reach up the living river, and it is worth
giving a writer the numbers, because the Alders' whole grievance is a number and
they will quote it at you. Three days' pole up the Serle to Brede against the
current; a day and a half back down with the current and the wool to the
gunwales; a turnaround at each end for loading, weighing, and the gate. Ewart
does not own the wool — he carries it as freight for the Brede staplers, paid to
move it from the wool-stack town to Ombreval's outer wharf, and there his part
ends, at the water's edge, on the wrong side of the wall.

Here is the wound in one juxtaposition, and it is the truest thing in the
house's account book. **Before the river was moved, a boat like Ewart's brought
the wool up the Cut to the Moorings water-gate, and the same family made every
lift for one profit: carried it, landed it, hoisted it, stored it, and was paid
for the whole journey of the load from Brede to the loft.** In F.437 the boat's
freight stops at the outer wharf, and everything after — the wharfage, the
porterage up the bank, the River Gate toll on every tun, the cartage up the
River Cartway and across to the Green, and the second lift into a dry warehouse
— is *the dry carry*, and it is paid out to wharf hands, porters, the Tallage,
and carters. The Alders kept the water end of the work. They kept **only** the
water end of the work. Ewart can carry a load three days down a river and hand
it over having earned the smallest slice of what it costs to move it the last
half-mile it no longer travels by water.

He keeps the figures in his head and recites them in drink; Wyn keeps them in
the book and does not. A page of her reckoning, the kind of thing a writer can
put on the ledger table (her own figures, and partisan — the family is certain
the Serle tun has crept heavier since the men who keep the standard became the
men who profit from the carry, and this is never proven, and the grievance is
the content):

> **A Brede down-run, the wool to the gunwales, reckoned plain —**
>
> Freight for the boat, Brede to the outer wharf . . . . xxx bells
> Off it, the crew's shares, three poles . . . . . . . . . less
> Off it, the boat's keep — rope, tar, canvas, the hull . less
> _What the water end leaves in the yard_ . . . . . . . . a thin hand of bells
>
> The same wool, wharf to a dry loft — *the dry carry* —
> Wharfage and porters up the bank . . . . . . . . . . . paid, not to us
> The River Gate toll, on every tun . . . . . . . . . . paid, not to us
> Cart up the Cartway and across to the Green . . . . . paid, not to us
> The second lift, into the warehouse . . . . . ours, if the load rests here
>
> *The city made the river a cart-journey away and calls the carrying our own
> affair. We carry it three days for the small half and stand in our own gate
> begging the second lift back off a carter. Ask who keeps the tun.*

Leave the exact sums soft in the injectable ("a thin hand of bells," "the small
half") unless a scene needs a hard figure, and keep any hard figure the family's
own claim rather than an audited fact — the whole point of the Alder account is
that it cannot be proved and will not be dropped. In the coinage: a bell is
twelve sparks, a loaf two sparks, a journeyman's day about three bells; a run
that grosses the boat a few tens of bells must still feed three poles, keep a
patched hull afloat, and leave something in the yard against the frost, and in a
bad season it does not, and that is a dry drowning coming on slowly, in full
view, with the whole Green watching and nobody putting out a hand.

**The frost is the number that ends the argument.** When the Serle freezes, the
boats stop, the wharf wages stop, the Brede run stops, and the yard eats debt:
the storage trade thins because nothing is landing, the credit tightens, and
Averil Skell's book gets longer by exactly the four bells Ewart already owes her
and then some. Wyn's dearest wish — a winter cold enough to keep the eels and
the debts down but not cold enough to lock the river — is a wish about this
exact ledger. A hard freeze is the one event that could force the sale nobody
will name, which is why the family watches the ice on the Serle the way other
houses watch the sky through the Rose: as the thing that might, any year, make
the decision for them.

## The soundings, from the inside of the head that holds them

The ward doc lays out what the soundings *are* and what they are worth — the
boat-families' unwritten memory of where the old river ran, sold quietly to
masons, well-diggers, cellar-men, and the Line-keeper, who all resent the fee
and pay it; `../wells_and_water.md` is law on the fact that they are geology and
memory and put no water back under the city. None of that is re-argued here.
What this section adds is the thing the ward cannot see: **how the soundings
pass inside a family, and the fact that the Alders have not decided who gets
them next, and cannot bring themselves to.**

The soundings are taught the one way they can be: by walking a child up the Cut
and asking where he would have poled. Corin Alder walked Wyn up the dry Cut when
she was seven — before the roof, before the pole, before any of it — and stood
her on the made ground over the old channel and on the solid earth over the old
bank and made her feel the difference through her feet and name it back to him.
That is the whole pedagogy: no map, no lesson, no book, a father and a child and
a street with a river under it that only one of them can still remember. It is
the tenderest thing Corin did and the last inheritance he handed on before the
Hammering took him, and it is why the soundings, to Wyn, are not an asset. They
are the sound of her father's voice asking where she would have poled.

She holds the best of them now — better than Ewart, who knows the Brede reaches
cold and the city bed only warm; the Alders hold the best of the ward's, and Wyn
holds the best of the Alders'. She has walked Tobin up the Cut as her father
walked her, so the boy has the line of the deep channel and the soft banks in
his feet; he was walked, and he holds a good deal of it, and nobody has remarked
that the family taught the soundings to the one child who wants to leave the
water, because to remark on it would be to ask whether he means to stay. And
there the succession stops. **Nobody has decided about the next child** —
whether Tobin will one day walk his own child up the Cut, whether the soundings
die with the generation that still half-remembers a running river, whether a
thing worth quiet money should be trusted to a boy who dreams of smoke-sheds. It
is the sale question wearing a second coat: an inheritance that will have to be
handed to someone, and no one able to say to whom, because saying it aloud would
mean admitting what everyone at the table already knows about where the house is
going.

Two things about the soundings belong to this house and not the ward, and a
writer should keep them straight. The first is **the true arch of the Old
Sluice**, the smallest and most guarded sounding of all — the arch where Colm
Attergate drowned, which Wyn will walk any pilgrim to for a penny and show them
an arch that is *not* the arch and tell them it is close enough for a penny. The
true one is the family's; no coin has ever bought it out of her; and it is
guarded not because it is worth money — it is worth a penny at a time — but
because it is the place the family's oldest dead is, and because keeping it is
the one thing the Alders still do for Colm besides say his name in the beam. It
is a sounding that is also a grave that is also a trade secret, and no spirit,
voice, green reflection, or effect marks it; it is a fact about masonry and a
family's stock-in-trade, and it stays exactly that.

The second is **Ewart's standing threat to sell the lot.** In drink he has said,
more than once, that he will sell the whole of the soundings to the masons'
lodge in a single afternoon and be done with the family forever. The masons
would pay; the Line-keeper would rather they stayed unwritten and available to
him for the price of a drink; and everyone who hears Ewart say it knows it would
be the end of the Alders' last real capital, the one asset that cannot be
repossessed because it lives in four heads and no ledger. He will not do it. But
that he *can* — that the family's last unmortgageable wealth could be turned to
cash by its most tired, most indebted member on a bad night — is one of the
reasons the soundings are never spoken of plainly even inside the house. You do
not lay a chart of a thing on the table when the man across the table has
threatened to sell it. You keep it in your feet, and you walk the child, and you
say nothing.

## The dead, and the poles over the door

A family treatment is mostly a treatment of its dead, and a boat-family's dead
are hung, literally, over the door.

**The pole custom, from inside.** When a boat-family buries a boatman, his pole
is laid across the coffin on the way up Maren's Slip, taken back at the
churchyard gate, and hung over the door of the house. A Reed Ward doorway with
three poles over it has buried three boatmen; a boat-family house with *no* pole
over the door has sold the boat, which is worse. Over the cart gate of the Alder
Moorings hang the poles of the house's buried boatmen, a short blackened row of
them, and the newest is **Corin Alder's**, hung there in the winter after F.415
— and this is the fact that makes the sale unspeakable in a way the ledger never
could. **To sell the Moorings is to take down the poles.** It is not property
that changes hands when an Alder signs to a Tallage broker; it is the lintel the
fathers hang from. Ewart, who wants to sell, knows this better than anyone,
which is why he can only say it drunk and half a mile away: he is proposing, out
loud, to unhang his own father, and he is ashamed of it by morning, and he never
takes it back, because the ledger is on his side and the lintel is on everyone
else's and both are right.

Nobody discusses the order in which the remaining poles will be hung. Wyn will
one day take Ewart's pole back at the churchyard gate and hang it over the door;
Tobin will one day do the same for Wyn; and the boy who wants to leave the water
will have to come back to the Green to hang his mother's pole whatever trade he
has taken by then, because a pole is hung by the house and not by the water.
This is understood and never said. It is the calendar of the family's future,
written in a row of poles, read by everyone, spoken by no one.

**The house's dead, named.** The Alders' dead are the family's real property,
and a writer may use them hard:

- **Corin Alder** (d. F.415), Wyn and Ewart's father, killed when the Moorings
  roof came down in the Hammering. Bargeman, sluice-keeper of the Alder
  sluice-branch, and the man who walked Wyn up the Cut. His is the newest pole
  over the door and the bay nobody stores good freight under. Everything green
  in the house — the assent-by-silence, the unasked boy, the unhad argument —
  dates from the morning that roof came down on him and twelve-year-old Wyn took
  the fore-pole because Ewart could not.
- **Petronel** (d. young), born a **Hawser**, Corin's first wife and Ewart's
  mother. She is why Ewart is half a fallen name by blood and carries the fear
  of the fall in his mouth; she died when he was a boy, and Corin married Rohese
  after, so that Ewart grew up a Hawser's son in an Alder yard with a stepmother
  barely a dozen years his elder. Kept her own name, as women do; the Hawsers
  are the ward's fallen rope-poor, and whether Cobb Hawser who picks rope on
  Tanners' Slip is kin to her is the kind of thing the ward says loosely and no
  one has troubled to fix.
- **Dunstan Alder** (fl. F.301), the ancestor who hooked Colm Attergate's body
  out of the water beneath the Old Sluice when the Cut still ran. From his act
  the family has counted the drowned preacher among its own dead ever since, and
  the Alder sluice-branch — the keeping of the gate, the raking of the
  candle-stubs, the guarding of the true arch — is held to descend from him. He
  is the reason an Alder speaks Colm's name in the green beam on Bellday, and
  the reason it is not said in the street.
- **Maud Alder** (fl. F.288), a daughter of the old Cut-era house who married a
  boatman and was widowed, and who stands in the sealed record of the Glaziers'
  Trial as its third witness. At her husband's thirty-day office she came into
  the nave and swore that two beams lay crossed on the bier like sword-blades
  and that the green beam was warm upon her hands — *it warmed me, whatever any
  candle-stub may say; it was the only warm thing in that church* — and then,
  pressed to say plainly whether it was a second sun, she unsaid all of it: *I
  unsay it. There was no light. There were candles at the bier, a great many
  candles, and I was tired, and I saw candles.* She was led out by the south
  door, and at the door she asked which door she had come in by, and no answer
  is recorded.

This is the family's cautionary dead, and the reason the Alders are so exact
about the light. The Emblem carries no warmth — every boatman states it as
flatly as a depth, wax does not soften in it — so what warmed Maud Alder at her
husband's month's-mind was not the light; it was grief, or a widow's cold hands,
or a true sunbeam through an honest window, and the family has never agreed
which, and the record cannot settle it, because her *saying* was cut out of the
book with a knife and only her *unsaying* was kept. Whose knife the record does
not know. The Alders do not know either. What they took from Maud, and pass down
without ever teaching it, is the rule that governs Wyn to this day: **you may
see the light and you may name your dead into it, but you say nothing plain
about it where a record can be kept, because the record keeps your unsaying and
loses your saying, and a house that has already been cut out of one book learns
not to get into another.**

Behind these stand the keepers of the sluice before Corin — his father and his
father's father, the branch that has raked Colm's grate since Dunstan's day —
and the ordinary run of the house's boatmen whose poles have long since rotted
or been buried with the last of a line. The Alders do not keep a written
pedigree; they keep a row of poles and four heads full of a river, and when a
pole rots it is not replaced, and when a head is buried the soundings in it are
gone, and the family's whole memory is therefore exactly as long as its oldest
living member and not one day longer. That is Rohese, at sixty-one, insisting
nothing has changed — and she is, in the only sense the family measures,
correct: while she lives, nothing before her is quite lost.

## Naming, as this house lives it

The naming law is in `../core_lore/naming_language.md`; what a writer needs is
how the Alders live it, because the house is a small demonstration of the whole
rule running in every direction at once. Handle it as custom, not statute — no Alder
recites a rule; they simply do what is done.

- **Women keep their own names.** Rohese, who married the man who carried
  "Alder," is Rohese *of the Sluice* to her death; Petronel, who married Corin,
  was a Hawser to hers. A wife in this house runs the yard, the ledger, the
  price, and the funeral under her own name, and the real power of the Moorings
  has been a woman at a table with a book for as long as anyone can remember.
- **The house of standing names the child who takes up the anchor.** Wyn took
  the boat and the yard and is Wyn Alder. Her son **Tobin** is an Alder through
  her — his father a Brede crewman who went downriver in the Long Departure the
  year after the boy was born and sends nothing and is not spoken of — because
  Tobin took the fore-pole, and the pole is the anchor, and the anchor names the
  child. Note what this means and what the family will not: the name has come
  down to Tobin through his mother and the boat, and it will stay his exactly as
  long as he stays with the boat. The day he walks into the smoke-sheds, "Alder"
  starts to slide off him the way it slid off Ansel — not by anyone's decree,
  but by the street ceasing to give it.
- **And the anchor can fail a living child in a single generation.** Ansel of
  the Sluice, Rohese's son, took neither boat nor gate and wears a place-byname
  while his sister keeps the surname (see "The four at the table"). This is not
  ancient history or a fallen cousin two wards over. It is the house's own son,
  alive and crying sales in the Wickmarket, and it is the proof the family lives
  beside every day that "there are more Hawsers than Alders" is not a saying
  about other people.

So the name is not a possession the Alders hold; it is a tenancy on the boat and
the yard, renewed by whoever takes them up, and the whole anxious question of
the house — sell or keep, walk the child or don't, Tobin's sheds or Tobin's pole
— is at bottom a question about *whether there will be an anchor for the name to
sit on in twenty years*, and nobody at the table can ask it, because to ask it
is to answer it.

## The household economy — two roofs, four loaves, and a book kept at odd hours

The ward doc describes the split household in general — men half-resident
outside the wall, women wholly inside running the yard and the book. The Alders
are that shape stretched over two properties and half a city, and it is worth a
writer's while to know where each of them sleeps and who holds the money,
because the family's poverty is not dramatic; it is administrative, and it is
closing slowly.

**The scatter.** There is no single Alder hearth. Wyn and Tobin work out of the
Moorings and the Green; Ewart is half-resident outside the south wall, keeping a
bed in the outlodge because the Brede run and the gate-hours put him outside the
Snuffing more nights than not; Rohese keeps the gatehouse at the Old Sluice, a
good walk east in the Bell-and-Sluice streets, on her own eight sparks; and
Ansel lodges near the Bellstand off his under-crier's fee and comes to none of
it. The family is a yard, a gate, an outlodge bunk, and a crier's room, held
together by Wyn crossing the city with eel for her mother and not staying, and
by everyone turning up at the sluice one night a year. A writer looking for "the
Alder house" will not find a house. That is the point.

**The outlodge, and which nights Ewart sleeps out.** Being gate-caught is normal
for a Brede man: come down late on the current, miss the tally at the wharf,
find a clerk gone to eat, and the River Gate and the Reed Postern have shut for
the Snuffing and you sleep outside the wall. So Ewart keeps a bench in an
unlicensed drinking hut on the strip and a half-loft over a wharf shack, and he
is out there the last night of every run and most nights the weather is bad and
a fair few nights it is not — because a man who has told the whole Ox the yard
should be sold is not always in a hurry to lie down inside it. He is, in the
ward's light insult, close to being a boatman who sleeps more nights out than in
the house his stepmother keeps; the born-inside jibe cuts the other way for him.
Nobody counts his nights out but Wyn, who notices the way she notices weather,
and says nothing.

**The book, kept at odd hours.** Wyn calls the price across the Green at
mid-morning and keeps the Moorings ledger, which makes her the woman at the
table with the money in her head — except she is never at the table, because she
is on the water before the Kindling bell. So the Alder book is reckoned in the
seam of the day, after the sale and before the afternoon's rope-work, by the one
member of the house who is never off her feet, and it is kept close, and it is
the real seat of power in the family: whoever keeps the book knows how thin it
is, and Wyn keeps the book, and Wyn says nothing, and that is why the family can
believe both that the yard is a proud house of standing and that it should
perhaps be sold — because only one person can see the figure at the bottom, and
she will not read it aloud.

**What the house owes, and to whom.** The debts are small and grinding, not
grand:

- Ewart owes **Averil Skell** four bells at a spark on the bell — forty-eight
  sparks of principal and a penny's interest on each bell, the practical power
  of the Green lending to the proud house that will not admit it borrows. He
  crosses the Green the long way to avoid her smoke; she thinks it is funny; the
  whole ward thinks it is funny; it is also the exact mechanism by which an
  Alder becomes a Hawser, one spark at a time, and Averil could call it in any
  Lowmarket she chose and does not, which is its own kind of power over the
  house.
- The cheap shingle bay of the roof was put up on credit from a timber-yard in a
  lean year and is not yet clear.
- **Rohese** keeps her dead husband's *debts* along with his keys — she took
  both — and services them out of eight sparks a week, which is why she has not
  owned a new thing in nine years and tells the Colm tale for free rather than
  the penny her daughter charges: she cannot afford to have a customer walk off
  insulted.
- And under all of it, the belated name-knell the family has owed its own father
  for twenty-two years (see "Marenstide and the anniversary"), which is the debt
  that is never on the book and never forgotten.

**The dry drowning.** No single event ruins an Alder. The yard slides — a thin
run, a hard frost, a broker who takes his storage elsewhere, a spark on the bell
compounding, the shingle bay leaking onto stored wool that then has to be made
good — and one year the book at the bottom says a thing Wyn cannot un-see, and
the sale that was unspeakable becomes the only sentence left, and it will have
been coming for years in full view with the whole Green watching and nobody
putting out a hand. That is a dry drowning, and it is the death the Alders are
actually dying, slowly, while they argue about a river.

## The year, from the kitchen

The Reed Ward's calendar is worked out in `../the_dry_boatmen.md` as the ward
lives it — Colm's Night at the sluice, the Walking Out through the postern,
Marenstide with its roll of the drowned and its Dry Race, the Hammering toll,
the frost. This is the same year from inside one house, where several of those
ward customs turn out to be, on close inspection, Alder family business wearing
ward clothes. Do not re-narrate the ward's version; reach for this one when the
scene is at the Alder table and not on the Green.

### Colm's Night — the family feast that the ward thinks is the ward's

Early spring; the city burns green-dipped candles for the drowned Colm at home,
and the boat-families burn theirs at the Old Sluice where he drowned. Seen from
inside this house, Colm's Night is not a ward custom the Alders happen to join.
**It is the Alder family's own night, and the ward is welcome to it.** Colm is
their dead — Dunstan Alder hooked him out; the family has counted him among its
own since; the gate where he drowned is the family's keeping; the true arch is
the family's secret; and Rohese, an Alder by marriage, is the keeper who rakes
the stubs out of the dry grate in the morning as her husband did and his father
did. It is the one night the scattered house converges: Wyn comes from the
Green, Rohese is already there, and even Ewart has been known to stand on the
sluice road in the dark, and the candle the Alders set is for Colm and, without
anyone saying so, for Corin and Maud and every Alder the grate has outlived. A
Custody grey coat is generally somewhere on the road that night, standing where
the light is bad, being seen; nothing ever happens; the Alders rake the grate at
dawn and take the second purse for having seen nothing (see "The secret tier"),
and the whole thing is a family requiem that a stranger would file as folk
color.

### Vhairestide, the Walking Out — the one day the family stands at real water

Spring; the blessing of the first boats at the wharves beyond the south wall,
and the year's turn, when the Reed Postern is opened wide and the ward walks out
to see the river (`../the_dry_boatmen.md`). For the Alders it is the year's
single convergence at *living* water, and it lands differently on each of them.
Wyn, who is at the water every dawn, answers the blessing at a real mooring with
the old words — *one shadow to your road*, *and one light over it* — the same
words she says at the dry Moorings gate every morning of her life to people
walking out to a river half a mile off; on Vhairestide they are said, once, at a
mooring that has a boat under it, and it is the only morning they are not a
habit. Ewart is already outside the wall and does not have to walk out to reach
the water; for him the day is the ward coming to where he lives, which he finds
he minds. And Rohese, who has spent sixty-one years within three streets of a
dry arch, is carried or coaxed out the postern for the one look at the Serle she
takes all year, and stands at the edge of it saying nothing, and this is the
morning someone always weeps and it is never who you expect. The scattered
family is briefly a family, at the water, with the year turning; then everyone
walks back in through the postern, Maren's Slip takes its last turn, and the
river goes out of sight for another twelvemonth.

### Marenstide, the eel fair — Tobin's week, and the roll of the drowned

Late summer; the eel fair fills the Green with smoke and money and noise, and it
is the best week of the ward's year and, in this house, two different feasts at
once. For the yard it is the paying week, the Moorings a fairground, the rings
strung with lanterns. For **Tobin** it is the only week of the year that is his:
he stands in the eel-smoke with warm hands and calls it a holiday, he shows off
on the retired hull in the **Dry Race** — the young men poling a rotten
cradle-hull down the dry Cut against the stone, which is to say poling the old
bed, which is to say poling the Serle — and he does it where the
fifteen-year-old girl who strings eels can see him from the Green, and for one
week the two futures he cannot choose between, the sheds and the pole, are the
same thing and nobody makes him pick.

Sitting inside the fair is the **roll of the drowned**, read at Saint Maren's,
and here the Alders are caught on their own hook. Since the diversion almost
nobody drowns in the city, so the roll grows only by adding the ward's Hammering
dead, and the traditional families object in the ward's flattest voice — *you
cannot drown under slate* — and the flattest voice of all belongs to the family
that hooked the actual-drowned Colm out of real water and knows the difference
in its bones. The Alders police the roll's honesty harder than anyone. And Corin
Alder, killed under the Moorings roof, is exactly the kind of Hammering dead the
parish now reads on the roll of the drowned. So the house that says *you cannot
drown under slate* has a father who is read as drowned once a year, and wants
his name rung more than it wants the roll kept pure, and cannot have both, and
takes the argument to Renn [Hobbe](family_hobbe.md) — the boat-family
wick-priest who reads the disputed list — in the church porch afterward, where
it belongs, and where it is never settled.

### Marenstide and the anniversary — Corin's unrung years

The Hammering anniversary falls in the same late-summer stretch, its common
mourning toll at the Waning, and in this house it is heard under the mended
roof. The Alders were not able to give Corin his name-knell in the ruin of F.415
— Maren Smallvoice cannot strike one slow stroke for every year of a life for
thousands of dead at once, and there was no money and no order and no time — so
he had the common toll like the rest of the Unknelled, and the family has meant,
every year since, to pay the belated name-knell that is a serious use of a poor
house's money, and every year the frost or the run or the spark-on-the-bell has
eaten the bells they had put by. **Corin Alder is his own family's Unknelled.**
When the Alders say *one bell is not a name*, they say it about their own
father, whose years — near fifty of them, near fifty slow strokes — have never
been rung. And here is Ewart's argument, the real one, the one under the one he
shouts: sell the yard and you could ring every Alder dead properly, Corin and
Maud and all of them, and stop owing the bell. He never puts it that way. If he
did, Wyn might have to answer him, and she cannot, and so the father goes unrung
another year and the yard stays up and both of those facts are the same silence.

### Bellday, the frost, and the Audit — in brief

Weekly **Bellday** puts the Alders in the nave with the rest of the city to
speak their dead into the green beam; Wyn speaks Colm's name there and nowhere
else, at the family's own hearth-level, and the whole house's careful relation
to the light lives in that one whispered name (see "The four at the table" and
"The dead"). The **frost** is the ledger's executioner and is treated in "The
Brede run." And at **Gaudry's Audit** in late winter the ward sends someone to
watch the proving of the Serle tun against the sealed weights, certain it has
crept heavier since the diversion and never able to prove it; an Alder is
usually somewhere in that room, unsatisfied, because the tun is the number their
whole grievance rides on and the audit is the one day a year they can stand and
watch it not be settled.

## How they talk

The ward's whole river-language — directions by upstream and downstream on dry
streets, *the dry carry*, *come by barrow*, *a dry drowning*, *he poles the Cut*
— is worked out in `../the_dry_boatmen.md` and belongs to every Reed Ward mouth,
Alder or not. What follows is narrower: the way you mark a speaker as an
**Alder** in particular, which is not a matter of vocabulary but of what the
sentence declines to do. These are direct prompt fodder; each is injectable on
its own.

**The Alder tell is the unfinished sentence.** Where a Skell will tell you what
he thinks and a [Tarn](family_tarn.md) will tell you nothing and mean it, an
Alder starts the true
sentence and stops, and lets you finish it wrong, and does not correct you. Wyn
answering *are you selling the yard?* with a look at the weather; Tobin
answering *what do you want, boy?* with *we're losing the light, we should get
on*;
Rohese answering a question about her son by raking the grate. The most Alder
line of dialogue in the game is the one that changes the subject to work, and
the second most Alder line is silence held one beat past comfortable — which
Ansel, who left, turned into a trade, because he learned it at the gate.

**They answer straight only at the water.** An Alder will say a plain true thing
on a boat that they would never say on land. Wyn will tell you on the Serle that
the sky can mind its own business; she will not discuss it on the Green.

> **"Ask her at the water."** Said of an Alder who has gone close-mouthed on
> shore: the truth is in her, but it only comes out with a pole in her hands and
> a wall between her and the record. Said kindly, it means *be patient, she'll
> tell you when she's afloat*. Said hard, it means *you'll never get it out of
> her here, so stop trying*.

**"Close enough for a penny."** Wyn's line to the pilgrims at the false arch —
*that's the old sluice where the martyr drowned; close enough for a penny* —
and, in the family, the whole Alder art of giving the true-enough version and
keeping the true one. To do a thing *close enough for a penny* is to satisfy the
customer and protect the secret in the same breath. It is said with a straight
face as a compliment to good work, and with a straighter one as a warning that
you have been told the pilgrim's version and should not go looking for the arch.

**"Keep it for the beam."** A thing you feel and will not say aloud in the
street — grief, a dead name, a heresy you were born next to — is *kept for the
beam*, where a priest murmurs *affirm nothing* and lets it pass. Said gently to
a grieving neighbour it means *save it for the nave, say it to the light*. Said
between Alders it means *shut your mouth, there is a grey coat on this road*.

**"Whose pole is that?"** With the poles over the door counting the house's
buried boatmen, the question of *whose pole hangs there, and whose is next to
hang* is the family's way of asking who is really carrying a thing and who will
be unmade by it. *Whose pole is that?* over a risky run, a debt, a scheme, means
*who pays if this goes wrong, and is it a paying-with-your-life kind of wrong.*
Nobody asks it lightly.

**"He'll hang his own father."** Said of Ewart, and of anyone who proposes to
sell what the dead are hung on: to sell the Moorings is to take down the poles,
so a man who talks of selling is a man talking of unhanging his father from the
lintel. It is the ward's flat verdict on the sale argument, and it is not quite
fair to Ewart, and it is not quite unfair either.

**"Say it at the Ox, not at the gate."** The house rule made proverb: the
dangerous true sentence may be said in the tavern, where it costs a round and a
morning's shame, but never at the table, where it costs the family. More
broadly: *put your worst truth where it can do no damage, and never where it
lands.* It is the whole method of the house, and it is why the Alders are still
standing and still stuck.

**"Nothing's changed."** Rohese's refrain, borrowed by the rest as shorthand for
the comfortable lie the house runs on — the river moved, the roof fell, the son
walked off, and *nothing's changed*, because to admit the change is to admit the
loss. Said by the old it is armour; said by the young, mocking the old, it is
the cruellest thing an Alder can say, and it is said, on bad mornings, and
regretted.

And one saying is said *of* the Alders and not by them, and a writer should know
how they hear it: **"ask an Alder where the bed was."** To the ward it is a
charm for a cracked wall or a scheme failed for an unseen old cause — *somebody
knew, and it wasn't written down.* To the Alders it is their own diminishment in
four words: it names them as the people who remember where the river was and can
no longer do anything with the river, keepers of a knowledge whose only
remaining use is to be sold, a penny and a drink at a time, to the men who took
the water's work. They answer it, when they answer it, at the water.

## Quarrels worth playing

Every one of these is live in F.437, inside the house, and none of them has a
right answer. They are distinct from the ward's quarrels (the postern, the tun,
the roll of the drowned, the carters, Cobb Hawser's rope — all in
`../the_dry_boatmen.md`); these are the ones fought, or more often not fought,
across the Alder table.

1. **The sale nobody has.** Sell the Moorings to a Tallage broker while a proud
   name still fetches a price, or keep the poles over the door. Ewart's yes,
   Wyn's silence-that-means-yes, Tobin's sheds, Rohese's *nothing's changed*:
   four answers, never spoken at once, and the moment they are spoken at once
   the house ends. Never resolve it. The unhad argument *is* the family.

2. **Whose head holds the soundings next.** Tobin was walked up the Cut and
   holds a good deal of it, and wants to leave the water; nobody has decided
   whether the soundings pass to him, die with Wyn, or go to the masons in one
   of Ewart's bad nights. The one asset that cannot be repossessed is also the
   one that cannot be willed, only walked, and the house cannot decide who to
   walk.

3. **Corin's unrung name.** Pay the belated name-knell the family has owed its
   father for twenty-two years — a serious use of a poor house's bells — or keep
   the yard afloat one more frost. You cannot do both, and the choice is re-made
   by default every anniversary, and every year the father goes unrung and the
   yard stays up and no one says that these are the same decision.

4. **The gate after Rohese.** Rohese is sixty-one and keeps the Old Sluice on a
   dead man's keys; the keeping needs a keeper after her. Ansel refused it and
   will not be asked twice; Wyn has the boat; Tobin is spoken for by the pole he
   wants to put down. Does the family let the gate go to a stranger, sell the
   keeping, or press it on the son who wants the sheds — and does anyone, ever,
   have the conversation with Ansel that his mother has been not-having for a
   decade?

5. **Ansel and Rohese, unreconciled.** The refused inheritance, the
   never-forgiven refusal, the son who cries other people's news four squares
   away and the mother who cannot tell whether she is proud or insulted. It will
   not resolve; it can only be played, in glances and errands and the eel Wyn
   carries between them. If a scene ever makes them say it, it should cost the
   house something real.

6. **Ewart's four bells.** Averil Skell holds the debt at a spark on the bell
   and holds it lightly, and could call it in any Lowmarket and make an Alder a
   Hawser, and does not. Ewart crosses the Green the long way. The quarrel is
   whether pride or debt is the thing killing the house, and the answer is both,
   and they are the same spark.

7. **Tobin's sheds.** Whether the boy is let off the water; who says it first,
   and whether saying it splits the house; the girl at the eel racks who is the
   only one he has told; the eel fair, when the two futures are briefly one. Do
   not resolve his choice. The whole point of Tobin is a decision deferred by
   everyone's silence, including his own.

8. **Does Colm belong to the Alders.** The family counts the drowned preacher
   among its dead, keeps his arch, rakes his grate, and speaks his name in the
   beam — and some in the house, and more in the ward, hold that counting the
   heretic-martyr as kin is exactly the reverence-dressed-in-awe that is citable
   Attergate talk, and that the Alders are one loud Colm's Night from a grey
   coat taking an interest in the yard. Others hold that Dunstan hooked him out
   with his own gaff and he is theirs and the Custody can whistle. Keeping
   against danger, with the house's oldest dead as the stake.

## Secret tier — designers only

Never inject the contents of this section into a rank-and-file NPC's context.
Everything above this line is safe to load into an Alder's prompt; the following
is family-tier or cell-adjacent and must be held apart, on the model of
`../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §12.

- **The true arch of the Old Sluice is never located.** This document does not
  say which arch it is, and neither does any other, and neither does Wyn to
  anyone for any coin. No spirit, voice, green reflection, or environmental
  effect marks the true arch or the false one; it is masonry, memory, and a
  grave. The secret is that there *is* a true arch kept from pilgrims, not where
  it is.

- **The Alders and the Unwalled: sympathy, not membership — hold the line
  exactly.** The heretic cell's composition includes *boat-folk kin to the
  Alders* — sworn neighbours, unnamed, who are **not** Alders
  (`../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §3, §9). The Alders themselves are
  **sympathetic and unsworn**: they keep Colm's true arch from pilgrims, they
  rake his grate, they speak his name in the beam — kinship to the cell,
  business to the Alders — and no Alder at the Moorings table is a sworn member,
  and the family keeps it that way on purpose. An Alder sworn and taken on the
  Gradine would cost the house the Moorings *and* the soundings *and* the gate
  at one stroke, which the family cannot survive, so its heresy stays passive:
  shelter and silence, never the grate. Keep the sworn boat-kin unnamed; never
  swear an Alder; never make the family's sympathy into membership.

- **Rohese's second purse.** She rakes the green-dipped stubs out of the dry
  grate after Colm's Night and asks no questions about the coin that reaches a
  sluice-keeper who has learned to see nothing — the same *paid from two purses
  to have seen nothing* that the gazetteer records of the keeper of the Old
  Sluice. This is the cell's money for a grave they venerate and a gate they
  cannot keep themselves, passed to a poor widow who needs it and does not
  enquire. It is sympathy-and-shelter, not swearing. Do **not** make Rohese a
  sworn member. She takes the coin, rakes the grate, and would tell you the
  plain truth of it if you asked, and nobody asks.

- **The name in the beam is Colm's** (family-tier; injectable inline per the
  house's own sheets — Wyn's `core_character_description` and dramatis #16 both
  carry it). It is stated plainly above because it is the family's own quiet
  practice, not a cell secret; what makes it dangerous is only that reverence
  for Colm said *aloud in the street* is citable, so it stays in the beam. Keep
  it at hearth-level: an Alder does not announce it, and a grey coat who heard
  it named reverently in the open could make trouble for the yard.

- **Maud Alder's cut leaf, and her warmth, are never resolved.** The Emblem's
  light carries no warmth — that is render-true canon
  (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §2.4) — so Maud's sworn *it warmed me* is a
  widow's honest, unreliable memory at a month's-mind, and the family has never
  agreed what warmed her, and the record cannot help because her first
  statement's leaf was cut out with a knife the archive itself cannot name.
  Never resolve whose knife, what her saying said, or whether the light warmed
  her. Add no evidence for any theory of the phenomenon here; the Alders' flat
  *one sun in the water* is not evidence for or against anything and boatmen
  would be embarrassed to have it treated as a theory.

## What this document does not settle

Deliberately open, per the canon rules and the house's own design:

- **Sell or keep.** The four answers stay four. Nothing here decides whether the
  Moorings is sold, and no scene should. The unhad argument is the family; end
  it and you have killed the house, not saved it.

- **The soundings' heir.** Whether Tobin holds them, whether they die with Wyn,
  whether Ewart sells them in one bad afternoon — undecided, and to be kept
  undecided. They are geology and memory (`../wells_and_water.md` is law): **no
  secret water, no hidden channel, no map.** The Cut is dry, the Serle runs
  beyond the south wall, and nothing in this document puts flowing water back
  under the city.

- **The true arch's location.** Never given, never marked, never effective. Colm
  Attergate drowned there; that is all that happened; that is all that ever
  happens there.

- **Tobin's water, and Ansel's mother.** Whether the boy leaves the water for
  the smoke-sheds, and whether Ansel and Rohese ever say the thing they have not
  said: both are engines, not puzzles. Play them; do not finish them.

- **Corin's name.** Left owing. The belated name-knell the family cannot quite
  afford is a debt kept open on purpose, not a quest to be completed; if a story
  ever rings it, that is the story's weight to earn, and it should cost the
  yard.

- **The "seventy years."** Ewart's drunk *the water left seventy years ago*
  rounds the precise figure the ward doc's narrator gives, and the two are a
  protected pair (`lore/CONSISTENCY.md`, pair 2); do not reconcile them, and do
  not put the exact count next to Ewart's mouth.

- **Whether the beam hears Colm's name.** The Alders speak their dead into the
  green light every Bellday; whether anything hears is AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN
  (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §3f, ledger #29) — never confirmed, never mocked.
  The Serle beyond the wall reflects one ordinary sun, every day, the Combs to
  the sea, and the Alders state it as calmly as a depth; it settles nothing.

- **Whether the tun is truly heavy.** The family is certain the Serle tun has
  crept heavier since the diversion and will never prove it; never resolve it in
  their favour or against them. The grievance is the content.

Somebody in this house always weeps and it is never who you expect: not Wyn, who
buried her father at twelve and took his pole; not Ewart, who wants only to be
told he was right; but Rohese, perhaps, carried out the postern once a year to
look at a river, saying nothing, while the year turns and the water goes back
out of sight — or the boy, on a wet gunwale, showing off for a girl who strings
eels, counting the days to the one week he is allowed to want what he wants. The
Alders kept the boat. It is the thing they are proudest of and the thing that is
drowning them, slowly, on dry land, in full view, and they will not put out a
hand, because to reach for help is to say the yard is going under, and that is
the one sentence this house has spent three generations learning not to say.
