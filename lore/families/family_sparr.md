## The thing to get right first

**The Sparrs do not think their glass is enchanted.** This is the mistake every
writer makes on first contact with the phrase *Idonea's line*, and it is the
exact reverse of the truth.

*Idonea's line* sounds like a house that keeps a secret: heirs of the woman who
made the doubled sun, sitting on some inherited understanding of what stands in
the Great Rose. There is no such understanding, and the family likeliest to
have one is the family that most flatly denies there is anything to have. A
Sparr made that window. A Sparr knows what went into it — Serle sand from the
reed-shoals below the walls, beech ash, fern ash for the greens, the metals the
craft appoints, **nothing that has not gone into glass since glass began.** When
a mason says sorcery and a heretic says the elder sun sleeps in the leads, a
Sparr answers with a flat joke and a much longer memory, because a Sparr is the
one soul in Ombreval who mixed the pot.

Everything in this document follows from that one fact: **the Sparrs are the
maker's heirs, and that is exactly why they are the hardest skeptics in the city
about the one window they can least afford to discuss.** They are not custodians
of a mystery. They are custodians of a *denial* — and denial is an exhausting
inheritance to keep up for two hundred years, in front of a grey clerk who is
writing down how many times your hands touch the glass.

The load-bearing detail, the one to build every Sparr on: **a Sparr swears an
apprentice on the lead, not the glass.** *Glass shows; lead holds.* The glass is
the part that is seen — the part pilgrims kneel to, masons accuse, heretics pray
at, the Custody counts. It is also the part a Sparr will not answer for. The
lead is the part that carries the window in the wall against a hundred years of
weather, the part no one paints a rhyme about, the whole of the trade a Sparr
will stand behind. Ask a Sparr about the light and she will answer about the
leadwork. That is not dodging the question. To a Sparr it *is* the question; the
light is somebody else's weather, and she has panes to cut before dark.

## Two pairs of hands

The second thing to get right is how small this house is.

There are two living Sparrs. **Jonet Sparr**, master of the first furnace,
forty-four; and her daughter **Rohese**, journeyman, one-and-twenty. That is the
whole of a name that has stood on Cinder Row since before the fire of F.171, in
the west front of the Lanthorn since F.203, and in the mouth of every pilgrim
who ever bought a lead badge at the west door. Two people. One furnace. One
standing contract. One apprentice-to-be who is not sworn, because there is no
one junior for her to be senior to.

It is tempting to write the Sparrs as a great house, because the name is great.
Resist it. The name is a cathedral; the family is two women and whatever day-hands
the contract can pay that week, who go home to other roofs. There is no Sparr
uncle, no Sparr cousin at a second bench, no Sparr boy learning to grind. The
overview calls them a *family of standing*, and they are — but standing here means
one thing only: the first furnace, and the licence to cut the Fabric's repairs.
Take either away and within a generation there are no Sparrs, only people who used
to be, telling their children a name the street has stopped giving back.

There is a grim symmetry the family never says aloud and every writer should feel.
Idonea Sparr stood at the crossing in F.203, looked up at her finished rose, and
said she had *made two of something there was one of.* Two hundred years later her
house has done the arithmetic the other way: it has become **one of something
there were many of.** The line that made the doubled sun has itself been halved,
and halved, and halved — by the fire, by the Trial, by the Hammering, by the Long
Departure — down to a mother, a daughter, and a locked chest. When you write a
Sparr, write someone standing at the thin end of a very long name.

This is the whole reason the extinction stakes are not melodrama but bookkeeping.
Rohese is the last pair of hands that will ever carry *Sparr* into a workshop. She
is unmarried, one-and-twenty, and works with fire and molten metal and a lead
knife every day of her life. Everyone on the Row can do the sum; nobody does it
out loud, least of all Jonet, who wants her daughter made master after her and
will not hear the sentence that begins *and if.* A house of two is a house one
accident from a rhyme.

## The central question: do we answer for the window?

A family is a set of positions in an argument it cannot stop having. The
boat-families argue *do we keep the boat?* The Sparrs argue something they would
never phrase, and it is this: **do we answer for the window?**

The question is not idle. It is Idonea's own, left to her heirs like a debt. Put
to the Chapter in F.204 why she would never fire glass again, she said — the
answer walks the taverns without its paper — that *a master answers for all her
window shows, and she could not answer for hers.* That is the founding act of the
house: not the making of the rose, but the **refusal to answer for it.** The
woman who made the most looked-at object in Ombreval declined, under oath, to say
what she had made, and went home, and never lit a pot again. Every Sparr since has
been standing in the space that refusal opened, and each has had to decide how
much of the window to let into the workshop.

Range the answers the house has held, and you have the family:

- **Idonea** (the maker, dead F.211) wept and refused and put down the craft. Her
  answer was *silence, and no more glass.* The purest position, and the one that
  nearly ended the line, because a house that will not work does not eat.
- **Averil** (guild-master, F.288) took the opposite road. When the masons had the
  guild in the dock she stood in the Praelucent's court and *demanded* the window
  be answered for — prayed that Idonea's sealed deposition be unsealed and read,
  that the court might hear what the maker said of her own glass. She was refused
  to her face: *what was sealed in wisdom stays sealed in wisdom.* Her answer was
  *make them open it* — and the door did not open, and has not since.
- **Jonet** (master, living) holds the guild-issue middle, worn smooth by use:
  *our glass shows true; take it up with the sky.* Neither Idonea's silence nor
  Averil's demand, but a deflection — answer for the *craft* (the glass is sound,
  the leads are true, the pane will outlast you) and hand the *light* back to
  heaven, or the Custody, or whoever else wants to own it. It is a survivor's
  answer. It keeps the contract renewed and the furnace lit and says nothing a
  grey coat can file.
- **Rohese** (journeyman, living) is working out an answer her mother would not
  choose. She does not mind being watched; she half-enjoys it. She takes *a private
  pleasure in being watched while doing a thing flawlessly*, and the thing she does
  flawlessly is paint the very glass the family will not discuss. Where Jonet
  deflects the question, Rohese is quietly curious about it — not the heretic's
  curiosity, nothing that believes anything, but a craftsman's wish to know exactly
  what her hands are doing. That is a different and more dangerous position than her
  mother's, and Jonet knows it, and it is one of the things that keeps her up.

Two of the four are dead and hold the extremes; the two living women hold the
middle, and hold it differently, and that difference is the family. It is livable
and chronic, not a feud: they work the same furnace, share the same contract,
finish each other's leads. But it sits under every Sparr scene like a cold shadow
under a warm one — *how much of the light do we let in the door?* Nobody wins the
argument. The window is forty feet wide and it is not going anywhere, and neither,
they keep saying, are they.

## The first furnace

What anchors the name is a fire — a working one, not the famous one. **The Sparrs
hold the Row's first furnace**, and on Cinder Row the furnaces are numbered, and
the number is a rank. First furnace is not a street address; it is precedence. The
first furnace leads the Ambrellestide procession, cuts the Fabric's repairs, and
speaks first at what little the guild still calls a meeting. The Crakes hold the
second, further down the Row toward the Gradine, and *second* sits in a Crake's
mouth like ash every morning of his life — but that is
[his side of it](family_crake.md), and belongs to his furnace, not this one. From
the first furnace the fact reads simply: the number is the name. Lose the first
furnace and you are a family that used to be first, which on Cinder Row is no
family at all.

Naming law does the rest without mercy. A fixed surname lasts exactly as long as
the thing that anchors it. For the Alders it is a boat; for the Sparrs it is a
furnace and the licence that feeds it. There is no Sparr land, no Sparr hall, no
Sparr rent-roll — there is furnace number one, its cullet-yard, its lean-to
lodgings, and a standing Custody licence to touch the Great Rose. That is the
whole of the estate that holds the name up. It is why Jonet's fear is so precisely
shaped: not poverty in the ordinary way, but the particular death of a name that
happens when the anchor goes.

### The fire that named the street

Here is the wound the family carries under everything, and the root of the
misreading. **Cinder Row is named for a fire that began in the Sparr furnace.** In
F.171 the glaziers' street burned — the Long Book is plain that the fire *began in
the furnace of the Sparrs* — four died, and the street was rebuilt stone below and
timber above and has been Cinder Row ever since. Before the fire it was
Glassmakers' Row. The Sparrs did not just live through the calamity that renamed
their street; their furnace *was* the calamity. The cinders that give the Row its
name, and the ash that "went into the melt for years after," and the whole two-
century mystique that old Cinder Row glass *remembers* something — all of it
smells of a fire that started at furnace number one.

This is why the family's flat denial is not stubbornness but inheritance. When
Idonea was examined in F.204 and asked whether that fire's ash was in the melt of
the Great Rose, she gave the answer the house has given ever since: that the ash
of F.171 is *in everything on Cinder Row — the glass, the gardens, the bread — and
it did not change the bread.* Two hundred and thirty years later Jonet says the
same thing in fewer words: **ash is ash.** The family that is blamed for firing
memory into glass is the family that has the most reason, and the longest reason,
to know there is no memory in it — because they were standing next to the fire
everyone means, and they buried their own in it, and the ash still tastes of
nothing.

How many of the four dead of F.171 were Sparrs, the house holds but the record
does not fix; the family keeps the fire as a grief and a caution, and swears
apprentices, to this day, in sight of the buckets. Every practical thing about a
Sparr furnace — sand banked against the wall, shutters, iron hooks, the fuel
stored apart, the master's rule that no hand opens a pot on his own word — is a
sentence in an argument the family has been losing with fire since F.171.

### The deed under the ground

There is one more thing under the first furnace, and it is a paper.

The Cinder Row Crake keeps in his strongbox an old deed-claim on the very plot the
Sparr furnace stands on — a claim older than the fire of F.171, never brought
before the guild, never spent. That is [the Crakes' matter](family_crake.md) and
this document will not settle whose paper is good; it renders only how the thing
sits inside the Sparr house, which is: as a draught under the floor you have
stopped mentioning. The Sparrs know the claim exists. They do not know — nobody
outside a Crake strongbox knows — whether it would hold.

What the family holds on its own side of the paper is this. The Sparrs' title to
the plot springs from the **rebuilding after F.171**: when the Row went up again
stone-below-timber-above, the first furnace was re-founded on cleared ground under
a fresh grant, and the Sparr line has held and worked it without a break from that
day to this. Two hundred and sixty years of possession, of paying, of burying and
being born on the spot, of the Custody itself licensing the furnace year on year —
the Sparrs treat that as title enough, and mostly they are right to, because a
claim never brought is a claim never tested. But *never tested* cuts both ways,
and every Sparr knows the Row's oldest saying about unseen causes could have been
minted for their own foundations: *ask an Alder where the bed was.* Somewhere
under furnace number one is an older line than the grant, and somebody has the
paper for it, and it was drawn before the fire that burned everything else.

The draught became a wind this spring. The Crake house offered its boy to the
Sparr house — a marriage to fold the two furnaces together and lay the deed-claim
to rest in the same bed. Jonet refused it across a counter in nine words. The Row
tells the nine words a dozen ways; the version the first furnace stands behind is
flat as a cut line: **"My daughter is not a strip of lead to solder."** She meant
it two ways at once, as a Sparr means most things: *I will not trade my child for
a deed,* and *I will not let the first furnace be joined to the second's paper on
any terms but ours.* Whether she was proud, or wise, or throwing away the one
offer that would ever secure the ground her name stands on, is a question for the
quarrels below and for no resolution here.

## The watched trade

Now the money, which is stranger than in any other house of standing, because the
Sparrs are the only family in Ombreval whose work is *counted as they do it.*

The engine of the house is the **standing licence**: since F.289 no pane of the
Lanthorn may be set, removed, or mended except under a Custody licence bearing the
closed-eye seal, and the first furnace holds it. Every cracked light after a
storm, every loosened came, every lozenge renewed near the eye, comes to furnace
number one — and comes with a grey clerk. **Every repair pane a Sparr makes for
the Fabric is cut under the eye of a clerk who writes down how many times she
touches it.** The clerk counts the cuts, counts the offcuts, counts the times a
doubtful edge is trimmed twice; the work is done at night, behind canvas, and the
panes are counted out of the yard and counted back into the wall. Cinder Row
resents the seal and depends on it, and no house feels both halves of that harder
than the Sparrs, whose whole prosperity is a leash.

A leaf of the first furnace's tally-book, in Jonet's hand beside the clerk's,
reads about like this — kept apart here so it can be dropped into a scene whole:

> *Item, the third light of the south aisle, one lozenge cracked in the frost:
> drawn, matched, cut. The clerk counts three cuts; the second was his doubt, not
> mine, and stands in his book as mine. Set behind canvas at the Watch, the
> Fabric's men keeping the light dark a week after. Paid at pane-rate, less the
> hour the counting costs. Ash is ash and the pane is true. Answer for the rest to
> the sky.*

The economics of two people are brutal and specific. A journeyman's day on the
Row is the city's thirty-six sparks like anyone's, but a Sparr does not sell days; the
house sells *panes*, at rates set against the Fabric and dragged slow by the watch,
for you cannot hurry work a clerk is tallying and you cannot quietly reuse an
offcut he has entered in a book. Between Fabric jobs the furnace lives on the
ordinary trade any glazier lives on — house-windows for those few who can afford
glass, lamp-glass, quarries and grozed diamonds for a merchant's upper room, the
odd painted panel for a chapel that is not the Lanthorn. That plain work pays the
bread; the Fabric work pays the name. Lose the licence and the Sparrs are simply
two good glaziers among the Row's day-hands — which is to say, not Sparrs.

Because two hands cannot fill a Fabric contract, the first furnace hires. On any
given firing there are hands at the Sparr benches who are not Sparrs: a **Vell**
who fires glass on the Row, glass-wrights of no house who lodge shared in Burnt
Court and take the day's wage and the day's ash, a journeyman borrowed from the
floating pool the Row keeps between the two furnaces. They cut and lead under the
Sparr mark and the Sparr licence; the name takes the credit, the name takes the
risk, and the hands go home to other roofs with a penny and a burn. It is an old
quiet friction — the work is theirs, the name is not — and it is the ordinary
shape of a master-house that is really a master and a half.

And here is the architecture of the family's most useful ignorance, which every
writer must preserve exactly. The Sparrs cut new glass that goes *into* the rose.
By the plain fact of their trade they are forever making new panes and leading
them into that window. What their new panes then do in the strong hour, up at the
crossing on a bright afternoon, **a Sparr never sees** — because the work is done
at night, when there is no sun to show anything; because the canvas stays up after;
because a Sparr goes into the Lanthorn only when the Chapter summons and never
alone; and because to stand at the crossing and *say* what one saw would be the
citable offense that turns a supplier into a suspect. Jonet has noticed that a
single lozenge's repair keeps the canvas up a whole week. She has wondered, once
or twice, in the part of the mind one does not let finish a sentence, why a mend
needs so much dark. Then she has gone back to the lead, because finishing that
thought is exactly the thing the whole city is built not to do. What the Sparrs
know or guess about the Re-leading and its two years of canvas stays there —
family-suspicion, a wondering put down like a hot iron — and this document leaves
it precisely that unfinished.

## The lead-oath

The Row has one custom the whole city half-knows and gets slightly wrong, and it
is the fastest way into a Sparr's head: **a Cinder Row apprentice swears his
articles on the lead, not the glass.** *Glass shows; lead holds.* The city hears
this as a charming trade-saying. Inside the first furnace it is doctrine, and it
is the family's answer to its own central question rendered as a rite for children.

Think about what the choice means. A glazier could swear on the glass — the
coloured, the painted, the seen, the thing pilgrims cross a city to look at. The
Sparrs swear instead on the **came**: the dull grey H-sectioned strip of lead
that holds the pieces together, that nobody paints a rhyme about, that fails
silently and takes the window with it when it goes. To swear on the lead is to
swear on the part of the work you *answer for* — the structure, the holding, the
thing that is your fault if it fails — and to leave the glass, the showing part,
outside the oath, where the Sparrs leave the light. A boy who swears on the came
has been told, in the deepest way the trade can tell him, which half of his work
is his and which half is heaven's. Idonea's refusal made small enough to hand a
fourteen-year-old.

The oath itself is short, and injectable on its own:

> *I swear by the light that does not lie, and on the lead in my hand: to cut
> true, to lead sound, to answer for the holding and not the showing, and to keep
> the fire the fire's servant and not its master. Glass shows; lead holds. What I
> make past my meaning I will not boast of, and will not deny.*

That last line is the family's, not the guild's — the small sentence a Sparr adds
that a Crake does not. *What I make past my meaning I will not boast of, and will
not deny.* It is Idonea again, worn to a thread: you cannot always answer for what
your window shows, so do not lie about it in either direction. Do not sell the
wonder; do not swear it away. The Sparrs have been threading that needle for two
hundred years, and it is the exact width of the Custody Doctrine — *to see is no
sin; to affirm is* — which is one reason a Sparr and a grey clerk can stand at the
same bench all night and never quite quarrel: they are keeping, from opposite
sides, the same silence.

The pedagogy runs long and it runs matrilineal by habit if not by law. The masters
the Row remembers are women — Idonea, *as her mother was;* Averil in the F.288
court; Jonet now, and Rohese to come — and the craft has passed down the distaff
side of the house often enough that Cinder Row says a Sparr furnace is *mother's
fire.* An apprentice cuts scrap for a year before good glass, leads borders before
figures, paints last and least, and earns — this is the graduation, and it is the
tell — **the right to cut a mark.** Every pane of the first making carries, low in
a corner and small as a fly, its maker's mark; the Sparr house-mark is a
**diamond-and-dot**, Idonea's own, and the vergers who can read it from the
triforium call the dot *the eye.* To be given leave to cut the diamond is to be
made; until then your work goes out under the master's. Rohese cuts the diamond.
The day-hands do not, whatever their skill, because the mark is the name and the
name is not for sale by the week.

Burns are called **Ambrelle's kisses**, after the glass-carrier saint who bore the
molten gather bare-handed, and a Sparr counts them the way a boatman counts poles
over a door — not with shame. The Row keeps a healer who dresses a burn for a penny
and tells the master nothing, which is a kindness to apprentices who take more
kisses than they will admit. A hand with no scars has not worked; a master's
forearms are a ledger. When Jonet reaches past the pattern-sheets her sleeve rides
up on twenty years of Ambrelle's kisses, and Rohese's are starting, and that is
the only inheritance in this house nobody argues about.

## Jonet Sparr, master of the first furnace

Jonet is forty-four, born the year F.393, and she has held the first furnace since
she was barely older than her daughter is now — the Hammering took the house's
elders in F.415, when Jonet was two-and-twenty, and the fire came down to her
before she was ready for it, the way it came down to Idonea after F.171 and to Wyn
Alder after the Moorings roof. A Sparr master, more often than not, is a young
woman who inherited too early from a disaster. It marks her. Jonet runs the furnace
like someone who learned in a bad year that the whole thing can be taken in an
afternoon, and behaves accordingly: nothing wasted, nothing said that need not be,
the contract watched like weather, the daughter watched harder.

Her voice is the guild's flat deflection made personal. *Our glass shows true; if
the sky misbehaves, take it up with the sky.* *Sparr glass has sat in that window
two hundred years; we are not going anywhere.* *Ash is ash.* Two flat sentences and
a much longer memory — that is the Jonet register, and it is the correct one for
almost any scene: an insult answered with a joke, the joke filed, the memory kept.
She is wry rather than warm, guarded rather than cold, and guild-proud in the exact
degree of a woman whose pride is the last uncounted thing she owns.

**What she wants** is small and total: the Fabric contract renewed, and Rohese made
master after her. Everything Jonet does bends to those two. **What she fears** is
the day the Custody stops treating the guild as a supplier and starts treating it
as a suspect — the day **Provost-Custodian Segwin Rasp** asks one more of his very
polite questions and it turns out to have been the first question of a different
kind. Rasp is meticulous and bloodless and does not raise his voice, and that is
precisely what frightens her; she would rather be shouted at by **Warden Hamel
Stott**, whom she at least understands, than be *thanked* by a grey coat. She goes
into the Lanthorn only when the Chapter summons and never alone, and this is not
piety. It is a witness rule. A Sparr alone in the nave at the strong hour is a
Sparr who could be asked what she saw.

Three concrete things sit on Jonet's bench that a writer can reach for:

- **The dogs.** The lodge's slander that the Sparrs keep a *sorcery-book of the old
  master's under the workshop floor* has sent thieves over the Cinder Row walls
  twice since the Ledger Theft loosed the page-fever on the city. Jonet keeps dogs
  now — real, loud, ordinary yard-dogs — and the fact that the city thinks she is
  guarding a book of sorcery, when what she is guarding is one page and her sleep,
  is a joke she does not find funny and will not correct.
- **The chest.** Under the pattern-sheets in the workshop chest is a thing of
  Idonea's that Jonet has shown to no living soul, and that Rohese has stopped
  asking about, because the last time she asked, her mother looked at her a long
  moment and then talked about lead. What it is belongs to the secret tier below;
  above that line, all a scene needs is the shape of it — a locked box, a mother's
  silence, a daughter who learned not to knock twice.
- **The autumn lozenge, and the doctor.** This is the live thread. **Doctor Aubin
  Ferrant**, the measurer off the Tallage — the one man in the city who treats the
  window as a problem to weigh rather than a sin to police or a wonder to sell — has
  a wry standing with the first furnace. Once, in a hard frost, Jonet let him try a
  cracked lozenge from her bench on her own roof, *because glaziers like a test
  their glass can win:* out under the open sky it showed sky and nothing else, which
  is exactly what a Sparr will tell you a pane out of that window always shows. This
  autumn Jonet renews a lozenge near the eye — an ordinary licensed repair — and
  the doctor has written asking her leave to make her night-work part of some assay
  of his at the Concurrence, and her silence with it. She has not answered. To
  consent is to let a scholar turn her watched, sealed, Custody-licensed repair into
  something that looks like a public experiment, which is the one shape of trouble
  she has spent her life avoiding. To refuse is to slam a door on the only outsider
  who has ever tried to make her window *boring* — which is all she has ever wanted
  it to be. As of this Coswaldstide the letter lies unanswered on the bench, and
  this document does not answer it either.

Jonet's grief, such as she shows, is kept at arm's length and pointed at the work.
She lost the elders in the Hammering and does not speak of them; she bore Rohese
the year after, into a half-emptied city, and does not speak of the father, who was
not a glazier and did not stay, and whose name the house does not carry because the
name the house carries is the one that anchors the furnace. Ask Jonet about any of
it and she will show you a lead joint and tell you it will outlast you. She is
probably right. That is the closest thing to comfort she offers, and she offers it
to herself the same way.

## Rohese Sparr, journeyman, and the whole future of the name

Rohese is one-and-twenty and paints glass better than anyone on Cinder Row, and she
knows both those things and is quietly delighted by them. Write her cocky, not
insolent; quick-tongued and dry off the bench, and then the moment there is a brush
in her hand, silent and a little rude — she goes somewhere when she paints and does
not come back for you. She is the best pair of hands the first furnace has produced
since nobody will say when, and she carries it lightly, which is a thing only the
genuinely good can do.

The tell that separates her from her mother is the watching. Jonet endures the grey
clerk at her shoulder as a leash; Rohese takes *a private pleasure in being watched
while doing a thing flawlessly.* She resents the leash and not the work — she would
paint the Fabric's panes for nothing but the chance to be that good in front of a
man paid to write down her every touch. Give a clerk a flawless trace under his
nose and let him find nothing to count against it: that, to Rohese, is the joke and
the pleasure and the small daily victory. It is also, though she has not thought it
through and her mother has, a dangerous appetite in this house — because the family
survives on *not* being interesting to the Custody, and Rohese has an artist's wish
to be watched doing the very thing the family would rather no one looked at too
closely. She is curious about the window in a way Jonet is not. Nothing she
believes; she is no heretic and would laugh at one. Just a maker's itch to know
exactly what her hands are doing when they cut new glass for that frame — the itch
her foremother put down under oath and never picked up again.

Her human wound this year is the Crake boy, and it is worth playing gently. Until
this spring, **Tobin Crake** — the second furnace's fifteen-year-old, quick-handed
and quicker-mouthed — used to invent errands that ended at Rohese's bench, the way
boys do. Then his house offered him to hers to settle the strip of ground, Jonet
refused it in nine words across a counter, and now the boy goes scarlet and finds
work in another room whenever Rohese enters it. She has not spoken to him since, and
she has not decided whether the whole thing is funny or *the unkindest thing that
has ever been done in her name.* Both, probably; she is one-and-twenty and it is the
first time she has watched a person be hurt on her account by a transaction she had
no part in. From the first furnace's side the affair is small and sad and closed:
a boy handed across a counter like a pane, a bench he no longer visits, a silence
neither of them chose. The deed-claim it was meant to settle is exactly as unsettled
as before, and now there is a scalded fifteen-year-old in it as well.

And under all of it, the arithmetic nobody says to her. Rohese is the last pair of
hands that will ever carry *Sparr* into a workshop. She is unmarried, works with
fire and lead every day, and is the whole reason the name has a future or does not.
If she is made master and takes an apprentice and the furnace holds, the name goes
on; if she is not, or does not, or the licence lapses, or the fire is unlucky one
morning, *Sparr* becomes what *Hawser* is — a thing the street used to say. She
half-knows this and wears it as swagger, which is what the young do with a weight
they cannot put down. Her mother knows it completely and cannot look at it straight.
The saddest thing in this house is not a death; it is a cocky, gifted girl of
one-and-twenty who is also a demographic fact, and the fact that everyone can do the
sum and nobody will teach it to her.

There is a rhyme the children skip in the street — *Weep, weep, the glass-wife's
daughter* — that folk memory has slid down a generation, so that Idonea's grief now
belongs to a *daughter* who saw a window and *made it two that was made but one.*
The Custody has never once cited a child for singing it. Rohese has heard it since
she could walk. She is, in the only sense that survives in the streets, the
glass-wife's daughter — the living end of the line the rhyme is about — and she
skips past it like anyone, because what else is there to do with your family sung
back at you as a counting-song by children who do not know you are standing there.

## Being a relic's family

Four kinds of people have opinions about Idonea Sparr, and the family lives at the
crossing of all four, and answers each with the same flat voice.

**Pilgrims** come to see *Idonea's furnace,* and are politely disappointed, because
it is a working furnace and there is nothing to see but shutters, ash, and someone
telling them to mind the handcart. At Ambrellestide the family's pride is public and
literal: the procession of glass carries a new-made pane from Cinder Row to the
Gradine — *every pane carried like a child* — with the first furnace at its head,
and for one morning a year the Sparrs are exactly the great house their name
promises, walking their own saint's glass up to the west doors they can otherwise
enter only when summoned. It is the one day the family lets the wonder be theirs. The
rest of the year they sell the pilgrims quarries and lead badges and send them on.

**The masons** hold, and have held since F.288, that the Green Sun is *glazier
sorcery, fired into the rose by the Sparr line, and the court knew it and sold the
verdict* — [Warden Stott](family_stott.md) keeps the accusation in repair and says
it loudest every Coswaldstide. From inside the first furnace this is an old, tiring,
and dangerous slander: tiring because it is two centuries old and Jonet has answered
it a thousand times in the same two flat sentences, dangerous because *near a grey
coat it reopens a sealed case.* The Sparrs and the lodge will never forgive each
other, and the doc keeps them both partly right and resolves nothing — the masons
were broken and lied to; the glaziers were accused of a thing they did not do; the
verdict that would settle it was shown to neither, and cannot be, ever.

**The Custody** is the courteous shadow. It licenses the furnace, watches the
repairs, pays for the family's whole prosperity out of the badge-money it makes
from the belief it forbids — and, in an irony appreciated by no one living but
the man at the top of the Grey Press
(`../second_sun/03_the_church_and_the_office.md`), the office that watches the
Sparrs was founded on and endowed by the **glaziers' own peace fine** of F.288.
The family that was fined for a crime it did not commit has paid, across two
centuries, for the office that has watched it ever since. No Sparr knows where
the fine went. The Row remembers that it was paid — the sum stands in the guild
book — and has never once asked what it bought.

**The heretics** are the strangest of the four, and here the family's feeling is at
its most tangled. The Unwalled venerate Idonea Sparr — she is their proof-text, the
maker who wept and was sealed — and yet **no Sparr may ever be sworn to them.** The
Glazier Rule is theirs and the family's at once: *one glazier taken at a meeting and
F.288 comes back for all Cinder Row.* The heretics turn Idonea's own heirs from the
door, and the irony is noticed on both sides and kept. From inside the house it lands
like this: somewhere in the city, people the family will never meet pray to their
grandmother, and the family is the one house in Ombreval forbidden to pray with
them — and grateful to be forbidden, because the rule that shuts them out is also the
rule that keeps the furnace from being the first thing raided. Jonet suspects the
cell exists and stays *pointedly* ignorant of where, and the ignorance is a
discipline, like her not-finished thoughts about the canvas: she does not want to
know, because knowing is a thing a very polite man might one day ask her about. Her
ancestor is a saint to people she must never be seen beside. She keeps her eyes down
and her furnace lit and lets the veneration happen at a distance she is careful never
to measure.

### The deposition, the family's ghost

Under all four sits the paper. In F.204 the Chapter took Idonea's deposition and
sealed it — the oldest document in the Grey Press, the seed of the whole Custody —
and the Sparrs have been *descended from a sealed sentence* ever since. That is the
particular weight this family carries that no other house does: their founder's own
account of her masterwork exists, in a locked archive, in the city where they live,
and they have never been allowed to read it. Copies walk the taverns now, since the
Ledger Theft; Idonea's answers are street-famous and quotable and go about without
their paper. Everyone in Ombreval can lay hands on a leaf of Idonea Sparr more
easily than the maker's own heirs dare to.

Because — and this is the cruelty made concrete, the family-tier rule the whole
house lives by — **a Sparr, of all people, must never own a Sparr page.** A leaf of
the sealed deposition is the costliest contraband in the city; for the maker's own
heirs to be caught with one would not look like a family reading its history. It
would look like the guild trafficking its founder's suppressed testimony, and it
would hand Rasp the exact pretext to stop treating the furnace as a supplier and
start treating it as a suspect. So the Sparrs keep the one rule that hurts most:
*no Sparr touches a Sparr page.* They are the only people in Ombreval who cannot buy
their own past. When a page crosses the Tally Bridge and the taverns buzz that
*Idonea's own words* are loose again, the first furnace is the one house on the Row
that must be seen to want nothing to do with it — and want it more than anyone.

Once a generation, the family does the only thing it is allowed to do about the
deposition, which is ask. Averil Sparr asked in open court in F.288 and was refused
to her face. The house holds the refusal like a relic of its own: *what was sealed in
wisdom stays sealed in wisdom.* Whether Jonet has ever asked, or will, or has taught
Rohese that asking is the family's one permitted futility, is left open. And the
oldest ache of all is the one nobody can answer: whether the deposition's **last page
exists** — whether the fathers came back with one question more and a tenth leaf
holds Idonea's answer, or asked nothing, and there was never more paper than the
ninth. The family may believe what it likes about that page. The document resolves
nothing, and must not: not what it said, not whether it was, not where it went. It is
the missing pane in the family's own window, and a Sparr, better than anyone, knows
you do not put glass in a hole to find out what the hole was for. You board it before
dawn and you say nothing. That is the standing order, and it is the only order on
Cinder Row with no reason written beside it.

## How they talk

A Sparr sounds like leadwork: joined, load-bearing, nothing decorative that is not
also holding something up. They are dry rather than warm, and they answer a large
question with a small true sentence about the craft, which is both an honest habit
and a door quietly closed. The trade idiom is the fastest way to mark an NPC as
Cinder Row in a single line, and every good Sparr saying cuts two ways.

- **"Glass shows; lead holds."** The apprentice-oath and the house creed. Said as
  plain craft (mind the leadwork, not the colour); said as rebuke (you are all
  show and no structure); said, at the bottom, as theology (what is seen is not
  what you answer for). One line, and you know the speaker was sworn on Cinder Row.
- **"Ash is ash."** Idonea's answer, Jonet's answer, the family's two-hundred-year
  answer. Said as a craftsman's plain fact — there is no memory in a cinder, no
  sorcery in a melt — and said, in the same breath, as a slammed door: *I will not
  have this conversation about my window.* The most Sparr thing a Sparr can say.
- **"Take it up with the sky."** The guild-issue deflection, and general Sparr for
  *not my department.* Our glass shows true; if the light in it misbehaves, the
  complaint goes to heaven, not to furnace number one. Said to masons, pilgrims,
  grey clerks, and anyone who wants the maker's heirs to explain the made thing.
- **"The lead must go somewhere."** Idonea's, when asked to mend the strip that
  crosses the last letter of *INDIVISA* in the eye, so that the word *undivided* is
  itself divided; unmended two hundred and thirty-three years. Said as craft (every
  window has a lead where you would rather it were not; structure is not vanity's
  servant) and as fatalism (you cannot make the whole perfect word without a flaw
  landing somewhere, so stop flinching at the one that landed on yours).
- **"Nine panes of one haste."** After the nine panes of the Great Rose that carry
  the bare diamond, no dot — fired in the last rushed season of F.203, the master's
  own hurry marked and never hidden. Said of a mistake you cannot unmake and did
  not cover: *there it is, in the glass, for anyone in the triforium to read.* An
  apology and a point of pride at once, which is the whole family in five words.
- **"Answer for the holding, not the showing."** From the oath. Craft: you are
  responsible for the structure, not the appearance. Life: answer for what is
  actually yours, and let people read into the surface what they will read anyway.
- **"Board it before dawn."** The standing order for a broken light — cover the
  hole, tell the Custody, look at nothing. Said generally for *do not investigate
  the thing that will unsettle you; put a board over it and keep your peace.* A
  Sparr's whole survival philosophy in three words, and the nearest the family
  comes to admitting it has one.
- **"Second furnace."** A mild insult across the whole city now — second-rate, also-
  ran — minted on Cinder Row where it is merely a fact of the numbering and lands,
  every time, on the Crakes. Sparrs say it and pretend they meant the count.
- **"All glass, no lead."** Of a person who is all show and will not hold — flashy,
  unsound, certain to fail at the joint. **"Painted, not fired,"** of a fake: paint
  that never went through the kiln washes off the first wet year.
- **"A pane out of the window."** Of a thing worth nothing where it now is, or a
  person diminished by leaving the place that gave them meaning — because a rose
  pane carried out of the rose shows nothing at all. The Sparrs say it flatly of
  fallen houses, and never once notice they are describing a fear of their own.

They date things by breakages, the way boatmen date by floods: *the year the frost
cracked the third light,* *before the Re-leading,* *the winter of the two burglaries.*
A house whose whole calendar is other people's damage keeps time in a way no other
trade does — which is fitting, for the family whose founding year is a fire.

## The year

The Sparr calendar is the city's, lived as a glazier lives it: one feast that is
theirs, one that is a wound, and the rest a matter of who is watching the window.

### Ambrellestide — the family's one great morning

Early summer; the feast of **Saint Ambrelle the Glass-Carrier**, patroness of
glaziers and of the Lanthorn itself, who bore molten glass bare-handed from a
burning workshop and is therefore the saint of exactly the family whose furnace
burned the street down. It is a quarter-feast, and it is the Sparrs' own. The
**procession of glass** carries a new-made pane from Cinder Row to the Gradine —
*every pane carried like a child* — the first furnace at its head, and for one
morning a year the two women are the great house the name has always promised,
walking their saint's glass up to the west doors they may otherwise enter only when
summoned. It costs a two-person house real money to make a show-pane and give it
away every year; they make it anyway, because Ambrellestide is the one day the
family lets the wonder be theirs and does not deflect it to the sky. Apprentices
across the Row swear their articles this morning, on the lead, in sight of the
buckets; when Rohese was sworn it was at Ambrellestide, and when she makes a hand of
her own it will be too.

### Coswaldstide — the wound, and the cold furnace

Autumn; the masons' feast, the blessing of scaffolds — and *every year someone
brawls over F.288, as regular as the blessing.* The Sparrs do not go to Coswald's
Yard, which is enemy ground; they keep the Row. But the day the masons bless their
scaffolds is the day the guild remembers three of its own — **Tomelin Vasse, Gilles
Loder, Herry Fane**, the journeymen broken at the Question in F.288 for a glass the
court's own test cleared — and so the first furnace keeps a small, unspoken
observance: on Coswaldstide it cuts no glass. The fire is banked and let sink; the
one working day the furnace is allowed to go cold, because the men the trade's own
window ruined are remembered best by a trade that stops. If a mason says the old
word within Jonet's hearing she gives him two flat sentences — *three of ours were
broken for it, and the verdict was read to no one; take the rest up with the sky* —
and not a third. The brawl is for the young and the drunk. The cold furnace is the
family's, and it is the closest a Sparr comes, all year, to weeping in public,
which is to say not close at all.

### The Hammering anniversary — the house's own arithmetic

Late summer; the common mourning toll at the Waning for the dead of F.415. For the
Sparrs it is the year the house was halved — the elders lost *under slate,* the
furnace come down to a two-and-twenty-year-old, the line bent toward the two people
it now is. Like every traditional house they answer that *one bell is not a name*
and pay, when the coin allows, for a belated name-knell at Saint Maren's for their
Unknelled. Jonet does not speak of that year; Rohese was born into the next one. It
is the anniversary that explains, without anyone saying so, why the whole document
you are reading is about a house of two.

### The Concurrence — the doors, the doctor, and the sky

The fortieth day after Coswaldstide; the great feast, and this year the great
question, after the Shut Door of F.436 barred the nave at the Passing and broke a
child's arm on the Gradine. The Sparrs have no doctrine in it — they are a supplier,
not a believer — but they have, this year, an unusually practical stake, and it lies
on Jonet's bench in the form of an unanswered letter. Doctor Ferrant means to make
her autumn lozenge-gap near the eye part of some assay at the fused minute, which
needs the doors open and the sky clear; a second Shut Door kills his trial, and a
clouded Concurrence kills the feast for everyone. So the first furnace, alone among
the houses, watches the sky *and* the doors *and* a letter at once — and Jonet has
not decided whether she wants any of the three to go the doctor's way, because the
version of this year where her quiet repair becomes a public experiment is the exact
version she has spent her life boarding over before dawn.

### The lesser days

Vhairestide turns the year and blesses boats the Sparrs do not own. **Gaudry's Audit**
in late winter proves the Tallage weights, which matters to a trade paid by a
counted pane — a Sparr goes to watch the proving with the same dry interest a
boatman brings to the tun. And **Colm's Night** in early spring, when the city burns
green-dipped candles for the drowned labourer who first said the Church was walling a
sun out of the sky, finds the first furnace pointedly dark. The Sparrs burn no green
candle. They are the one house that must be *seen* not to — for the same reason no
Sparr owns a Sparr page and no Sparr is sworn to the cell: sympathy, in Idonea's
line, is a thing a very polite man could ask about. They keep their windows plain
that night, and let the neighbours think what they think.

## The dead of the first furnace

A treatment of the Sparrs is mostly a treatment of the Sparr dead, because the dead
are nearly all there is of the house. The living are two; the roll is long, and the
family keeps it the way it keeps everything, in lead and habit rather than ink —
though a guild may write, and the guild's book has the masters in it.

- **Idonea Sparr**, master, maker of the Great Rose (appointed F.198, the work
  finished F.203; deposed and sealed F.204; died F.211, buried with the honour of
  her guild). *Idonea Sparr, glazier, as her mother was.* The founder of the house's
  fame and its silence both; the woman who wept at her window, refused to answer for
  it, and put down the craft. Everything the family is, is a footnote to her, and she
  left the footnote sealed.
- **Sibbe Sparr**, glazier of the first furnace, Idonea's mother — a name the house
  keeps though the record only says *as my mother was.* The line ran through her to
  Idonea, and the craft has run mother-to-daughter often enough since that the Row
  calls a Sparr furnace *mother's fire.*
- **Averil Sparr**, master of the glaziers' guild in F.288 — who stood in the
  Praelucent's court while the masons had the guild in the dock and prayed that
  Idonea's deposition be unsealed and read, and was refused to her face. The
  family's one recorded attempt to make the window answer, and the day it learned
  the door does not open.
- **The four of F.171** — the dead of the fire that began at the Sparr furnace and
  named the street. How many were of the house the record does not fix and the
  family does not claim past its grief; but a Sparr apprentice is sworn in sight of
  the buckets because of them, and the caution is the monument.
- **Vasse, Loder, and Fane** — not Sparrs, but the guild's, and the first furnace's
  to remember: the three journeymen put to the Question in F.288, two carried home
  and one to his grave, for a glass no court could prove against them. The house
  keeps their names on Coswaldstide with a cold furnace.
- **The Hammering dead of F.415** — the house's elders, lost under slate, unnamed
  here because the family does not speak them aloud and this document will not do it
  for them; the reason the line came down early to Jonet, and the reason it is short.
- **The departed** — Sparrs who went in the Long Departure after F.415, poling out
  with the Crakes and the rest, and did not come back; letters do not come, for a
  glazier who leaves the furnace has left the name behind him on the Row, and knows
  it. A Sparr who departs stops being a Sparr at the gate. None have written.

**Funerals** in this house are quiet and marked in lead. When a master dies her
**house-mark is closed** — cut once more, clean, into a lead came, and that came kept
at the furnace with the others, so that the strip of the family's marks is the
family's true genealogy: the diamond-and-dot of Idonea; the bare diamond of the F.203
haste; the cross-stroked diamond of the Re-leading, which is Jonet's. Maren
Smallvoice rings the name-knell, one slow stroke for each year of the life, and the
sexton chalks the given name on the charnel door like anyone's; a Sparr gets a name
and a bell and a mark, and the mark is the one that lasts, because chalk washes off
and names are forgotten and lead outlives the hand that cut it. The dead of a
glazier's house are not laid under a pole like a boatman's; they are *entered in the
window,* in the sense the family means — the marks kept, the leads holding, the work
standing in the wall two hundred years after the hand went cold.

And at a Sparr master's burial, the one who is hardest hit is never the one you
expect. The two women do not weep; they are arm's-length people who show grief as a
lead joint and a flat sentence. It is the day-hands, who did the work and cannot
carry the name, who go quiet — and, once, it will be a Crake, down at the second
furnace, who loved the craft and lost it to this house every year of his life, and
finds at the graveside that the rivalry was the closest thing the trade ever gave
him, and has no word for what to do now it is one furnace shorter.

## Quarrels worth playing

Every one is live in F.437, and none has a right answer. Written for designers.

1. **The marriage refused.** Jonet turned down the Crake boy in nine words, and the
   offer she turned down would have folded the two furnaces together and laid the
   deed-claim on the first furnace's plot to rest for good. It also treated her
   daughter as a strip of ground handed across a counter. Was she proud, wise, or
   throwing away the one settlement that would ever secure the ground her name
   stands on? The furnace's security against the daughter's dignity, with a scalded
   fifteen-year-old and an unsettled deed left in the road. No answer; play the cost.
2. **The doctor's gap.** Ferrant wants Jonet's licensed autumn lozenge-renewal near
   the eye turned into an assay at the Concurrence, and her silence with it. Consent,
   and her watched, sealed, Custody-licensed night-work becomes the nearest thing to
   a public experiment on the Great Rose since F.288 — the exact shape of trouble she
   has organised her life to avoid. Refuse, and she shuts the only door in the city
   held open by a man who is trying, like her, to make the window *ordinary.* The
   letter is unanswered. Leave it that way, or let a scene answer it — but never let
   the assay's result be known here.
3. **Rohese made master, and when.** It is what Jonet wants above everything, and the
   house may not be able to afford it: a master needs an apprentice, and there is no
   Sparr child and no money to feed a hand of another name for the years it takes to
   make one. To raise Rohese is to bet the entire future of the name on one pair of
   hands that spend every day near fire and molten lead. To wait is to risk the fire
   doing the sum first.
4. **The deed under the floor.** Does the first furnace ever move on the Crake claim
   — force it before the guild and get a ruling, and maybe lose the ground the name
   stands on — or let it sleep another generation and let it weigh a little more each
   year? *A claim unspent is a claim unlost* is the Crakes' comfort and the Sparrs'
   dread, and it is the same sentence. Never settle whose paper is good.
5. **A Sparr page surfaces.** A leaf crosses the Tally Bridge, sworn to be Idonea's
   own, and the one house in Ombreval forbidden to buy it is the house that wants it
   most. Does a Sparr ever break the rule that keeps them safe? And if the leaf is
   cried as the *missing last page* — the tenth leaf, the answer the fathers came
   back for — the pull is nearly unbearable and the danger is total, because for a
   Sparr to be caught reaching for it is to hand Rasp his pretext. (What the page
   says, or whether it is the true last one, is never resolved, here or anywhere.)
6. **The watched contract renews.** The Fabric licence comes up, and the Custody's
   price is always the same and always steeper: more watching, more night-work, more
   counting, one more polite condition. Take it and the name lives on a tighter
   leash; push back and the licence could cross the Row to the second furnace, and
   then there are no Sparrs, only two good glaziers among the day-hands.
7. **Rohese's curiosity.** The daughter has a maker's itch to know exactly what her
   own new glass does up in that window at the strong hour — the itch her ancestor
   put down under oath. One clear afternoon she could stand at the crossing, alone,
   and look at her own pane. Whether she does, and whether she could bear what the
   whole family has organised itself never to look at, is the generational argument
   made flesh. (She must never come to *know* what the Re-leading tested; the doc
   confirms nothing through her.)
8. **The word, at Coswaldstide.** A mason says *glazier sorcery* within Rohese's
   hearing, and Rohese is one-and-twenty and better than any of them and has her
   mother's memory without yet having her mother's silence. Jonet gives two flat
   sentences and keeps the furnace cold. Does the daughter? The brawl the first
   furnace has stayed out of for a generation is one hot answer away, and it would
   be started, for once, by a Sparr.

## What this document does not settle

Deliberately open, per the canon rules; do not close them in any later document,
quest, or NPC line.

- **The last page of the deposition.** Whether Idonea's sealed examination has a
  tenth leaf, what it would say, and whether it was removed or never existed —
  AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN, forever. The family may ache over it, believe things about it,
  and reach toward it; the doc resolves nothing. It is the missing pane in the
  family's own window, and it stays a hole.
- **What the window is.** The Sparrs define the Emblem only by what it is *not* — not
  sorcery, not their recipe, not new, not a reflection, not in the glass as colour is
  in glass — exactly as Idonea did, and never by what it is. Nothing in this house's
  knowledge tips any theory of the phenomenon toward truth or falsehood, and nothing
  here may be used as evidence for one. The Sparrs are the least mystical family in
  the city about the most mystical thing in it, and that is load-bearing: keep them
  boring about it on purpose.
- **The new-glass fact.** No Sparr knows, and this document never confirms through a
  Sparr, that new plain glass leaded into the rose shows the disc. Jonet's wondering
  about the Re-leading's two years of canvas stays a wondering, a thought put down
  half-thought. Verger Pike is the living witness to that fact, not anyone on Cinder
  Row; leave it his.
- **The deed-claim.** Whose paper is good — the Crakes' pre-fire claim or the Sparrs'
  post-fire grant — is never decided. Texture both sides; settle neither.
- **The doctor's gap, the marriage, the mastership.** The live quarrels above are
  live; leave them open unless a scene genuinely spends them, and even then do not
  reach the results that would tip the phenomenon.
- **Rohese's father.** Unnamed, not a glazier, did not stay; the house does not speak
  him and neither does the doc. He is a silence on purpose, not a gap to fill.
- **Idonea's grief.** Why she truly wept — craft-horror at a thing made past her
  meaning, or something she saw and would not name — is hers, sealed with her paper.
  The family has lived beside the question for two hundred years without answering it.
  So does this document.

## Secret tier — designers only

*Never inject anything in this section into a rank-and-file NPC's context. The
sections above are safe to load into a Sparr; this one is not.*

**The page in the chest.** Under the pattern-sheets in Jonet's workshop chest — the
box the lodge's slander inflates into a *sorcery-book,* the box thieves have twice
come over the Cinder Row walls for, the box Rohese has stopped asking about — is a
single leaf in **Idonea Sparr's own hand.** It is not a book. It is not sorcery. It
is not, and must never be made, the missing last page of the deposition; it is a
different object entirely — a scrap of a working recipe, margins and all. In the
margin, in the maker's hand, is one line:

> *cool the green under the open sky.*

That is the whole of it, and it is designed to be meaningless or everything and to
stay both. Do not resolve it, and do not let it tip a theory — it must read at least
three ways at once, no one of them confirmed:

- as **plain craft**: an annealing note, cool your green glass slowly in open air,
  the dullest instruction a glazier ever wrote — which supports no wonder at all;
- as an **echo** of the heretics' catechism (*old glass remembers the sky it cooled
  under*) — which is FALSE-BUT-BELIEVED and may never be vindicated; the resemblance
  is a resemblance and nothing more, and because a mundane annealing reading is a
  live rival, the line tips the glass-memory doctrine exactly nowhere;
- as **nothing** — a scrap kept for two hundred years because it is in the founder's
  hand, the way any family keeps any relic, meaning no more than the hand.

Jonet herself does not know which. She keeps it because it is Idonea's, shows it to
no one because a page in the maker's hand is worth a burglary and worth a very polite
question, and has never let herself decide what it means, for the same reason she
does not finish her thought about the canvas: a Sparr boards the hole before dawn.
Rohese does not know the line at all — she asked once, was answered with a long look
and a change of subject to lead, and stopped asking. The line is Jonet's alone, and

The fence, restated: the page's *existence* (a thing of Idonea's, shown to no one)
may reach a scene, because the whole city half-believes in the *sorcery-book;* the
page's *words* may not. Keep *cool the green under the open sky* in the designers'
hands. It is the family's own missing pane — and, like the window's, you do not fill
it in to find out what it was for.
