## The thing to get right first

**The Rasps are not powerful.** This is the mistake every writer and every
prompt-builder makes on first contact, and the roster all but forces it: the
name is marked **S**, a family of standing; a grand crest hangs at the top of
this page — grey, an eye argent within a black bordure, the plainest statement
of purpose any house in the city bears; and the head of the name is
**Provost-Custodian Segwin Rasp**, master of the Custody of the Eye, the office
that licenses the glass, orders the pilgrim hours, stamps the badges, and keeps
the Grey Press, which is to say the city's own file of faces. Put those three
facts together and the eye slides straight to *dynasty* — a house of watchers,
grey and rooted, to be feared from the top of the name to the bottom of it.

It is the exact wrong picture, and it is wrong in the exact way that matters,
because it reads the entire name off its one man and hands sixteen poor people a
power not one of them holds. The truth is the other shape entirely, and it is
the shape the stub names: **the Rasps are the watching family, but only at the
top.** Below Segwin the name is as ordinary as any in the central streets — a
long tail of coopers, scavengers, brewers, carters, gaol-hands and paupers who
mostly have no idea a Rasp keeps the city's record of who lingered where.

The correction is a fact about anchors. A fixed name in Ombreval is not a
possession but a lease, and the naming law says exactly what pays the rent: a
family name *lasts only as long as the thing that anchored it — a boat, a yard,
a warehouse, a bench of trade.* Four things, all of them property, all of them
inheritable, all of them the kind of anchor a house can hand to a child. The
Rasp name of standing rests on none of the four. It rests on an **office** — and
an office is the one anchor in the city that cannot be handed down. The Church
grants the grey coat to a person; the Common Bench, ruling its own side of the
wall, is blunter still and holds that *no civic office is hereditary, purchased,
or held for life.* When Segwin dies or falls, the coat goes to whoever the
Chapter next appoints, who need not be a Rasp and most likely will not be, and
in that single stroke the name loses its only anchor and drops all the way back
to the bynames it never really climbed out of.

So here is the family beside its opposite, and the pairing is the point. You can
*watch a Vell name move* — rising at the wax end, falling at the river end, the
naming law caught in the act. The Rasp name does not move at all. It stands
stock-still, held at one point by one grey collar, giving no sign — until the day
it simply falls, whole, in an afternoon, because the man who was the anchor
stopped being the Provost-Custodian. Of all the standing names in Ombreval the
Rasp is the least anchored and the least aware of it, and the horror the design
intends is quiet and administrative: **the family that keeps the record of which
names have already fallen holds the most falling name in the city, and does not
file itself.**

The second thing to get right is smaller and just as load-bearing: **the two
halves of the name do not know each other.** The overview puts it in one line —
the Rasps supply the man who watches, the Vells the woman who is watched, *and
neither family, top to bottom, mostly knows it.* Segwin knows the file of every
face in Ombreval and does not sit at his own kin's table. The poor Rasps of the
Bell-and-Sluice and the Wick mostly do not know that the grey coat the whole
city steps around shares their surname, and those few who do know cannot decide
whether it is a thing to claim or a thing to be ashamed of. The family is the
whole quiet war in one surname — a grey coat at the top and the watched poor at
the bottom — and the war is fought by people who do not know they stand on
opposite sides of it, because the two sides have never been introduced.

Everything in this document follows from that: **the name watches at exactly one
point and is watched everywhere else, and the watching end and the watched end
are strangers to each other.** It is not a house. It is a surname with an office
at the top and a crowd underneath, and the office is the only thing holding the
whole precarious arrangement up in the roster of standing at all.

## What "Rasp" anchors — the office, not a workshop

It is worth being exact about what holds the Rasp name up, because the whole
family is the answer to that one question, and the answer is strange enough that
the city has half-agreed not to look at it directly.

The naming law lists four anchors and the Rasps have none of them. There is no
**Rasp yard**, no **Rasp loft**, no **Rasp hull**, no **Rasp bench of trade** —
no workshop with the name over the door, no guild in which a Rasp holds the
warden's chair, no lease of ground that the name is written into. The Sparrs are
anchored by two hundred years of one window; the Vells by a melt-house and a
candle contract; the Alders by a warehouse court with the name on the cart gate.
Take any other house of standing and you can walk to the thing that holds its
name up and put your hand on it. Walk to the thing that holds the Rasp name up
and you arrive at the **chapter house of the Lanthorn**, in the Fabric, at the
door of the Grey Press, and the thing your hand finds is a grey office that does
not belong to the family at all. It belongs to the Church, and the Church merely
lets a Rasp keep it.

So "Rasp, a family of standing" decodes, in full, to one sentence: **there is a
Rasp in the Grey Press.** That is the entire standing. It is exactly as durable
as one man's tenure of one appointment, and it will end the way appointments
end — not in a fire, not in a bad year, not in a son who could not keep the
pound, but in a death and a Chapter minute naming somebody else.

The crest proves the case rather than softening it. The Rasp arms are **grey, an
eye argent within a bordure sable** — the watching eye of the Custody, silver on
grey, fenced in black. It is not a charge a Rasp forebear earned at a trade the
way the Vells earned their candle or the Tarns their hooped tun; it is the
office's own device, granted to the arms because the man bearing them holds the
office. Heraldry here follows the coat, and when the coat leaves the family the
eye leaves with it. A Rasp rag-picker under the Bellstand has no more claim on
that silver eye than a stranger off the road; it was never the family's, only
the Provost-Custodian's, and the family is simply where the Provost-Custodian
presently keeps his surname. There is even a small seam in the device worth a
line, because it is the whole house in miniature: the **arms** bear the eye
*open* — the watcher's eye, taking the city in — while the **office** seals its
grey wax with the eye *closed*, which is Saint Belwyn's eye, the blind saint's
sign that darkness too is of the Light. The family watches with the open eye and
seals with the shut one, and no one has ever asked it to reconcile the two,
because no one but the man in the coat has ever had to hold both.

And here is what the standing does *not* buy, which is the part the roster's
single letter **S** hides. It buys nothing that descends. A Vell child anywhere
on the Wickmarket draws real interest on the name — credit at the Tallage a
Quern's child would not get, a pew, the right to take articles, the plain social
weight of *of standing*. A Rasp child draws none of it, because the interest
stops dead at Segwin's collar and never runs down the name. The assumption of
credit that standing carries elsewhere does not reach a Rasp brewhouse-hand or a
Rasp scavenger; they are as poor as the nameless around them and, a good many of
them, poorer. **"Rasp, of standing" is a rumour kept at the top of the city that
never once reaches the bottom of it** — the thinnest S on the roster, and the
only one that is, when you press it, less a possession than a courtesy the city
extends to a surname for precisely as long as its bearer wears grey.

That is the family's central, unanswerable question, and every Rasp answers it
without knowing it is being asked: **does the name watch, or is it watched?** The
one man at the top answers *watch*, coldly and on purpose, and knows the whole
shape of the question. The sixteen underneath answer *watched* in fifteen
ordinary ways — with a shrug, with a splinter to dig out of a palm, with a place
in the hiring line to secure before dark — and most of them have never once
suspected the question exists, because the two ends of the name have never been
in the same room, and the naming law will collapse the whole argument into
bynames the day the coat comes off, before anyone thinks to hold the vote.

## Segwin Rasp — the man who watches

Segwin (54) is the whole of the name's standing, and he is the least dramatic
man in the city to carry so much of it. The office he heads is rendered in full
elsewhere — `../second_sun/03_the_church_and_the_office.md` is the Custody's own
document, and this is not it; this is the *house*, and Segwin is rendered here as
the family's one man, the way the Vell document renders being watched rather than
the Custody's org chart. What matters for the Rasps is the register, and the
register is the stub's: **he corrects rather than punishes.** The horror of
Segwin Rasp is filing-cabinet horror. He does not raise his voice, he does not
own a rack, he frightens people chiefly by being patient with them, and the
worst thing that has ever happened to anyone in his keeping is that they were
*thanked, and filed, and let go.*

His public days are dull on purpose, and the dullness is the method a first
Provost-Custodian wrote down two centuries ago: *an office of secrets should be
housed inside an office of queues.* So Segwin licenses the glass, orders the
pilgrim hours, meters the viewing of the Emblem at the sightlines the Line Books
keep open, stamps the badges, renews the Sparr workshops' standing licence,
schedules every repair of the Great Rose at night behind canvas with a grey
clerk counting the panes out and counting them back — and, chief of all, he
**files every claim anyone makes about the light and quietly disproves most of
them.** A beam seen in a courtyard three streets from the rose; a grave-shadow
that stayed after the man left; a green spark woken in an old bottle: each comes
in as a leaf, each is weighed, and each goes into the column that has never held
any word but *not demonstrated.* He wants written **what a man saw, and not what
he thought it meant**, and he will send a clerk back three times to get the
seeing clean of the meaning. It is the driest possible relationship to the most
frightening thing in the city, and it is entirely sincere.

He **refuses the Question** — torture — outright and on the record, and his
reason is not mercy but craft: *torture is bad record-keeping. It fills the
Press with what the clerk wished to hear.* The distinction is the man. He is
against cruelty the way a good clerk is against a blotted page — because it
ruins the record — and if that lands as principle, it is principle arrived at
from the wrong end, and he would not thank you for calling it kindness. His
standing rules are all of the same grain, and every one of them is a lesson
learned from a predecessor's disaster: **no seizure without three independent
leaves; never at the meeting itself; the Gradine before the cell, always; no
clerk signs what he did not himself see.** He came into the office in F.429, the
year *after* the Whisper Arrests, and inherited a Press still smelling of the
five seized in Gaunt Passage, the two recanted on the Gradine, and the fuller
dead in custody of the damp — and a whole doctrine of his has grown from the one
cold observation that *arrests create witnesses.* Given the choice he would
rather own the building than the prisoners; given patience, he usually can; and
he makes every new grey clerk read, unabridged, the ledger of the year a
frightened predecessor answered forty loose tongues with forty arrests, so that
the office never mistakes a public weeping for a success again.

There is one fact about the money that Segwin holds the way a man holds a stone
in his shoe, and it belongs above every fence because the whole city half-knows
it: **the badge-money part-funds the office, and the office thus profits from
the belief it polices.** The Church sells the stamped picture of the Emblem and
forbids anyone to say what the picture shows, and the fees run to the fabric and
the grey office both, and Segwin — who could not care less for the theology and
cares enormously for a clean ledger — files the whole arrangement under
*anomalies of the purse* and lets it sit there, unresolved and unresolvable,
because it is true and he does not lie in his own books, even to himself. The
office he keeps was founded on the same joke, older and colder: its endowment
was the **glaziers' peace-fine** of F.288 — the money levied on the guild for a
crime the court had just disproved — so that the office which guards the sealed
verdict was paid for by the fine for the disproven thing, a fact recorded in the
Custody's first ledger and appreciated, in two centuries, by no one but Segwin
Rasp. He appreciates it the way he appreciates most things: silently, exactly,
and without doing anything about it.

The Sparrs, up on Cinder Row, hold the sharpest reading of him, and it is worth
giving from their side because it marks the register precisely. Mistress Jonet
Sparr says she would rather be *shouted at* by Warden Stott, whom she
understands, than *thanked* by a grey coat; her whole dread is the day the
Custody stops treating the glaziers as a supplier and starts treating them as a
suspect — the day Segwin Rasp asks one more of his very polite questions and it
turns out to have been the first question of a different kind. What the Sparrs
cannot know, and what sits under all Segwin's patience, is that a second glass
trial is the one thing his office fears *more* than they do: a public case is a
public experiment, and a public experiment is F.288 come again, which is the
catastrophe the whole Custody exists to prevent. The thing the glaziers dread
him doing is the thing he most needs never to do. Neither can say so to the
other, and so the politeness holds, quarter after quarter — a shared terror
wearing the manners of a licence renewal, which is exactly how Segwin Rasp likes
a dangerous thing to look.

## What he rose from, and the table he does not sit at

Segwin's dead are the key to why the name at the top of the Grey Press is a poor
central-streets name at all. He was not born to the coat. **He rose from the
bottom of his own surname** — from exactly the watched poor the rest of this
document describes — and the distance he travelled is the distance between the
two ends of the name, walked once, upward, by one man, and never walked back.

His people were **scavenging poor of the Bell-and-Sluice**, the rag-and-bone,
gather-and-sell end of the central streets where the Rasp name sits thickest and
lightest. His father — call him by the given name the family kept for him,
**Dunstan Rasp**, dead now a quarter-century — picked salvage and bone and mended
what could be mended, and got his clever middle son the one thing a scavenger's
son can be got that changes a life: **letters.** Segwin learned his in the
Lanthorn as a **Taper**, a novice sweeping the chapter house for the right to be
taught the alphabet, and letters were the ladder out. Taper to grey clerk; grey
clerk to the archive; and there he stuck, because a man who wanted written *what
a man saw and not what he thought it meant* was worth more to the Press than a
man who wanted to rise. He was a junior clerk copying the first Hammering rolls
in F.415, a young archive-clerk on the Re-leading scaffold in F.419–421, and
Provost-Custodian since F.429. Fifty-four years from a salvage-picker's pot to
the keeping of the city's whole record, and nearly the whole of it done inside
one grey building.

He paid for it in the ordinary coin a rising clerk pays, which is everything
else. He never married; there are no Rasp children of his body and there will be
none; the grey coat is a solitary calling and the Press is his household, the way
it is his investigator Tilman Rue's — *no wife, and nobody expecting him home.*
This is not a wound he displays. It is simply the shape of the life, and it has
one consequence that is the quiet grief of the whole family: **there is a family
table, and Segwin Rasp does not sit at it.** The poor Rasps eat, as the poor of
the central streets do, out of a shared household pot — a work-and-lodging
group's common stew, with wages and barter and a place in the pot standing in
for a wage that is never quite enough. Segwin eats alone in the chapter house,
off the office's account. The man who keeps the record of who lingered at which
door could not tell you, without pulling the parish roll, the given names of the
children in the Wick Rasp lodging his father's line came down to, and he has
never pulled the roll, because it is the one file in the city he has decided not
to open. The watcher does not watch his own, and the not-watching is the nearest
thing to tenderness the office has left him room for.

He does keep one of his dead close, in the only way he keeps anything, which is
in the record. Somewhere in the F.415 rolls he copied as a junior clerk is a
sister — **Petronel Rasp**, dead under slate in the Hammering, one of the
Unknelled who got the common mourning toll and never a name-knell of her own —
and the line that reads *a clean copy cannot repair a missing witness* is not,
for Segwin, a maxim about archives. He copied the roll that could not properly
account for his own sister; he watched her name waver between the rent book, the
guild list, the parish register and the survivor testimony, counted four
different ways and certain in none; and he has known ever since, with a clerk's
exactness and a brother's silence, precisely how many names went missing in the
gaps between the counts, because one of them was hers. He has never said so. He
files the grief the way he files everything — flat, exact, at arm's length, in a
hand that gives nothing away — and it is the single most human thing about him,
and the only soul in Ombreval who knows it is Segwin Rasp.

Resist making him sympathetic and resist making him sinister; the model is the
stub's dryness and the archivist's own closing line, that *memory is glass of
another sort: it lets the light through and colours it in passing.* Segwin Rasp
believes that sentence to the marrow, corrects for it in every leaf a clerk
brings him, and cannot correct for it in himself — and the correcting and the
not-correcting are the same grey man.

## The household of the office

The man who does not sit at the family table does have a household; it is simply
not made of Rasps. It is made of **grey clerks of other names**, and it is the
only household in the city the Provost-Custodian actually keeps — a dozen or so
clerks under a Presskeeper who wardens the archive keys, and among them the two
the corpus draws sharply: the investigator **Tilman Rue** and the fair-copyist
**Sible Mott**. They are not kin and they are not friends; they are the office's
household, which is a colder thing than either, and the coldness is exactly the
distance a signature keeps. Segwin renders them the way he renders everything, at
one remove, in the ledger, and this is worth a section because it is the family
the watcher has *instead* of his family — and it holds him at the same length he
holds his blood.

**Tilman Rue** is the office's patience given a coat — the grey figure at the back
of the paupers' burials, hands folded, watching the mourners instead of the
grave: who lingers at the charnel door, who counts the strokes of the knell, who
reads the chalked name, and *who already knew it.* He is mild, slow-spoken, and
without malice; he thanks the gravedigger, asks after a widow's chest cold and
means it, and people take a long while to work out why they cannot sleep after
meeting him. He wants one thing, and it is Segwin's approval, and he gets it in
the form of more work — which is the whole economy of feeling in the grey office
in a line: the head of the Rasps runs his household on the same currency he runs
his archive, patience paid in tasks, and never a warm word spent where a cold
correct one will serve. Rue's watch-lists are faces and not names, by the order
he was given and obeys exactly, and it is **Sible Mott** — a clerk of the Mott
name, quiet, thorough, careful of her manners — who makes the fair copies in a
better hand than his, in the Grey Press, on a wage Segwin signs. To Segwin she is
an exact hand and a clean copy and nothing else; he does not look at her closely,
and she would rather he never had cause to.

There is the grim symmetry that closes the man. **Segwin does not watch his own
blood, and he does not truly see his own household either.** He keeps the file of
every face in Ombreval and cannot tell you the given names of the Wick Rasp
children; he signs the wage of a clerk at the next desk and reads her as a hand
rather than a person. The watcher's attention is total and it is aimed entirely
outward — at the city, at the claims, at the light he must prove is only what it
is not — and it never once turns to the two households nearest him, the blood one
he left and the grey one he leads. That is not an oversight. It is the shape of
the office: a man who spent his whole attention on the record has none left for
the people keeping it beside him, or the people carrying his name out in the
streets, and the loneliness of the head of the Rasps is that he has two families
and belongs, fully, to neither.

## The watched poor — the long tail of the name

Turn from the one man to the sixteen, and the family changes shape entirely.
Below Segwin the Rasp name is one of the ordinary poor names of the central and
western streets — a long, thin, un-anchored tail scattered across the working
trades, and the roster shows it exactly where the naming law would leave a name
with nothing under it: heaviest in the **Bell-and-Sluice**, the crowded central
mixing-bowl around the Bellstand where the city's overflow settles, with a real
spill west into **Wick** among the light-trades and the pilgrim pennies, a
handful down in the **Reed** Ward by the water, and a thread that reaches even
into **Cinder Row** among the furnaces. Keep every one of them role-level and
swappable, exactly as the overview requires; the whole point of them is that
they carry no biography, and the name hangs nothing on any single pair of hands.

Set the trades of the name in a row and you have a fair census of how the poor of
Ombreval keep alive. The Rasp name runs to **cooperage** — a barrel-maker at the
Reed tubs, a wheelwright's man in the Wick — to **scavenging**, which is where it
sits thickest and lowest: a salvage-picker of eleven and a rag-picker of fifteen
working the central gutters for what can be sold. It runs to **brewing**, to
**cartage** (a young carter on the Reed roads, white lime dust in the seams of
his coat), to the hem of the **pilgrim trade** in the Wick, where a poor chandler
dips the cheapest tallow a stone's throw from wax-houses of standing and small
children beg a penny at the Gradine steps. It runs to a **grain-dealer** of
eighty scraping a pauper's living, a **tub-attendant** of eighty-one who begs a
regular pitch, a **cobbler**, a **seamstress**, a **hired hand** selling the last
bruised apple off a barrow. And — the sharpest irony in the census — it runs to
two men in the city's small coercive uniforms: a **gaol-hand** at the Stone
House and a **militiaman** of the Wall Muster, both of them nineteen and
twenty-one and poor as the rest. So the name carries, at its bottom, a little
low authority of its own — a boy who guards prisoners, a boy who musters at the
gate — and here is the thing that makes it a Rasp fact rather than a coincidence:
**neither of them works for the grey coat at the top of their name.** The Stone
House gaol answers to the Common Bench's Full Measure; the militia answers to the
Wall captain; the Grey Press answers to no one but Segwin. The city's watching is
divided among institutions that do not share a chain, and the Rasp name is
scattered across three of them without any thread connecting the watcher at the
top to the small watchers at the bottom. A Rasp gaol-hand and the
Provost-Custodian are not colleagues. They are strangers who would each have to
pull a roll to prove the kinship, and neither ever will.

The poverty is the other half of the census, and it is the half Segwin knows best
because it is the half that turns up in the rolls. The name runs heavily to
**paupers, orphans, and the alms-dependent**; to **noncitizens and recent
migrants** measuring the local speech before they trust it; to insecure lodging
and intermittent work and a place in the hiring line that has to be secured
before dark or lost. These are the households that eat from the shared pot
because no single wage in them is whole. They are also, precisely, the households
a grey clerk stands at the back of a funeral to *watch* — not because they are
suspected of anything, but because the poor of Saint Maren's parish are where the
Custody has learned to look, and a Rasp among the mourners is a Rasp in a grey
notebook like anyone else, his surname no shield at all. The watched poor of this
name are watched by a machine their own kinsman built, and the machine does not
check the surnames of the faces it files, because the man who runs it has made a
rule of not checking one surname in particular.

A note on the given names, because a reader will trip on it and the tripping is
the point. The poor Rasps draw their first names from the same small approved
bank as everyone else in the city, so the name runs to an **Aubin** who is not
Aubin Vell, a **Corin** who is not Corin Copp, an **Osanne**, an **Idonea**, an
**Aldith**, a **Havise** — given names worn, elsewhere in these documents, by
chandlers of standing and glaziers of legend and the anchoress in the wall. It
means nothing. A poor family has no more claim on a rare name than on a rare
trade, and so it reaches into the common bank and comes up carrying the same
handful of names as the great, which fools no one for longer than a breath and
feeds no one at all. It is the naming law doing its work at the very bottom: the
sound of a fine name on a person with nothing under it, worth precisely the
breath it takes to say and no bread.

## How the name feels from below

Here is the question the whole family turns on, asked from the bottom instead of
the top: **does sharing a surname with the Provost-Custodian get a poor Rasp
watched harder, or handled softer?** It is a real question with real stakes for a
rag-picker who has been stopped by a ward hand, and the honest, cruel answer is
that it does both, unpredictably, and that the poor Rasp never gets to know which
one happened to him.

Most days the name does nothing whatever, because most people never make the
connection. The city steps around the grey coats without much thought to their
surnames; *Segwin* is a name few poor households would think to attach to their
own *Rasp*, and the two ends of the name are so far apart in ward, in trade, and
in daily orbit that the link is invisible from either side. A scavenger stopped
at a market is a scavenger; the ward hand reaches for *thief* or *nuisance*, not
for *kin to the Custody*, and the moment passes like any other. That is the
ordinary case, and it is why the poverty of the name is so complete: standing
that no one remembers to apply is standing that buys nothing.

But now and then someone *does* make the connection — a ward hand with a good
memory, a Bench sergeant who has copied a summons in the chapter house, a
neighbour with a grudge — and then the shared name cuts, and it cuts both ways at
once. Sometimes it cuts *soft*: a hand goes easy, a charge is not pressed, a
questioner grows careful, on the muttered logic that *you never know whose kin a
Rasp is, and a grey coat has a long memory and no sense of humour.* And sometimes
it cuts *hard*: the same shared name becomes a reason to make the example
sharper, on the equally muttered logic that *a Rasp of all people should know
better, and it will do the grey office no harm to see its own name kept clean in
the street.* The boy on the receiving end cannot see the logic, only the outcome,
and he takes the outcome for the rule. The one who got off easy believes, ever
after, that the name is a small shield and produces it when cornered. The one who
got the harder end believes the name is a mark and hides it. **Both are right
about what happened to them and wrong about the name**, which has no fixed value
at all — it is only a fact in somebody else's ledger, and what it does to you
depends entirely on who is holding the ledger that day. There is no purer piece
of filing-cabinet horror in the family than this: your surname is an entry you
did not write, kept by people you will never meet, and it will help you or hurt
you by a rule you are not allowed to read.

The street's gossip does the rest, lightly and without much heat, because a poor
Rasp is not interesting enough to gossip about for long. The occasional *are you
kin to the grey coat, then?* gets the occasional flat answer, and the answer
sorts the family better than any roll: some say *no,* fast, because the
connection is a danger and a shame; some say *aye, at some remove,* with a
straightening of the back, because it is the one grand thing a person with
nothing can produce; and most say nothing and get on, because they have a splinter
in the palm or an apple to sell and no time for a quarrel that was never about
them. The rumour pool of the central streets keeps the grey coats in it — *mind
what you say near grey; somebody at the Bellstand sells talk; they count faces at
every pauper's burying now* — and a Rasp hears all of that said in his own
hearing, about his own name's office, by neighbours who have not connected the
two, and mostly he lets it lie, because connecting them out loud is exactly the
sort of thing that gets a face remembered.

One Rasp of the poor tail is fixed in another document and must be handled with
care: the boat-quarter's `../the_dry_boatmen.md` names a **Warin Rasp** once,
setting hoops at the Tarn tubs on Tanners' Slip — *"Sibbe Tarn hoop-setter, Warin
Rasp and Colm Pell at the tubs."* That single fact is canon and this document
keeps it exactly as it stands: a Rasp of the Reed Ward works cooperage beside the
boat-families, ward-resident and not boat-blood, one of the central name's poor
threads reaching down to the water. Hang nothing else on him. He sets hoops; the
ward doc names him; he carries no more weight than that, and this document adds
none.

## Averil Rasp — the one ordinary Rasp you may name

Out of the whole watched tail exactly one Rasp is drawn sharply enough to name
and use, and she is worth the whole section because she is the family's ground
truth — the ordinary Rasp against whom the grand one is measured. **Averil Rasp**
(19) is a brewhouse hand in the Bell-and-Sluice, an apprentice on a brewer's
wage, kept fed by that wage and barter and her place in the shared pot, and
carrying on her the plain marks of the work: she is **missing two fingers**, and
she smells faintly of **woodsmoke and mint** — the smoke of the copper fires and
the mint they scour the mash-tubs with. She is **courteous, direct, and hard to
hurry**, and the people of her ward come back to her because she remembers
agreements and remembers faces — which is, if you hold it up to the light, the
one Rasp gift running clean through the whole name, top to bottom: *a memory for
who owes what and who was where.* At the top it keeps the city's file. At the
brewhouse it keeps the slate straight and the customers loyal. It is the same
talent, put to a barrel at one end and a nation of faces at the other, and the
two ends do not know they share it.

What Averil is *to* Segwin is deliberately, canonically loose, and this document
keeps it loose: her sheet fixes no kinship to him at all — no father, no line, no
thread drawn to the Grey Press — so the safest and truest thing to say is that
she is a Rasp of the Bell-and-Sluice and he is a Rasp in the Fabric, and whether
the two surnames meet in a grandfather or merely in the same small name-bank is a
thing neither of them has ever troubled to work out. **That un-worked-out
distance is the relationship.** She has heard, the way everyone has heard, that
there is a Rasp in a grey coat somewhere at the top of the city; it has never
bought her a loaf, mended a finger, or shortened an hour at the copper; and she
gives the fact exactly the weight it has earned in her life, which is none. The
brewhouse view of the great kinsman is the flattest view in the family, and it is
the correct one: a name is a sound, and the sound does not brew.

Her sheet gives her the line that ought to govern how the whole family is
prompted, and it is the anti-heroic key to the entire document: **she means to
keep her place without pretending that every local quarrel concerns the Great
Rose.** In a corpus where the light in the window pulls every scene toward
itself, Averil Rasp is the standing refusal of that pull — the citizen who has a
short measure to argue over, a lamp-oil to buy before dusk, a wage to be counted
full, and no interest in reading any of it as a sign. She is not naïve; she is
*right*. Most quarrels in Ombreval are about a penny and a place and a late cart,
and a Rasp who keeps her head down and her slate straight is telling the truth
about the city that the grand story keeps trying to talk her out of. If Segwin is
the family's cold top note, Averil is its ground bass, and the doc needs her
there for the same reason the family does: to keep the watched poor from being
swallowed whole by the one watcher, and to remember that fifteen of seventeen
Rasps would rather talk about the price of malt.

## Where a Rasp has no house

Ask where a Rasp *household* is and you find the anchor problem again, this time
in brick. Every other house of standing in the city has a place the family
gathers and the name coheres: the Vells have their Melt Yard and a parish pew
and a place in the chandlers' feast processions; the Alders have the Moorings yard with
the name on the cart gate; the Tarns have the cooperage on Tanners' Slip; even
the fallen keep the memory of a door the name once stood over. The Rasps have
**nowhere.** There is no Rasp yard, no Rasp hall, no Rasp bench at any parish
altar, no Rasp procession on any feast, no guild in which the name holds a chair,
and so there is no room in Ombreval in which the Rasps are, for one hour a year,
a family rather than a scatter of people who share a sound.

What they have instead is what the poor of the central streets have: **lodging.**
The name coheres, where it coheres at all, around **work-and-lodging groups** —
the shared roof, the shared pot, the wage-and-barter arrangement by which a
brewhouse hand, a scavenger, an old grain-dealer and a couple of children who are
nobody's in particular keep one another fed under one landlord. A "Rasp
household" is really a Wick lodging with two or three Rasps in it and as many
other names, or a **widow court** in the Bell-and-Sluice — one of those small
central courts the city calls widow courts because nearly every household in them
lost someone under slate, though the living residents need not be widows and
mostly are not. The name gathers in rented rooms it does not own, beside names it
is not kin to, and disperses again with the next quarter's rent. This is not a
house guarding a workshop. It is a surname renting space.

And here is the shape the anchor problem takes when you draw it as a map: the one
place in the whole city that is *unmistakably* the Rasps' — the seat the name is
known by, the reason it is marked **S** at all — is a **grey room in the chapter
house that only one Rasp may enter and none of the others may.** The Grey Press
is the family's only real address, and it is shut to the family. A scavenger of
the name could stand in the chapter close all day and never be let past the door
of the room his own surname is famous for; the widow-court children have no more
right to it than a stranger; and the one man who holds the key sits inside it
alone, at a table none of his kin will ever be asked to. So the family's centre is
an office, and the office is barred to the household, and the household is a
scatter of rented pots — which is the naming law's cruelty drawn in stone: **a
name of standing whose standing is a locked room, and whose family is everywhere
except inside it.**

## The dead, and the eight bells

A family treatment is mostly a treatment of its dead, and the Rasp dead pose the
same problem the Rasp living do: there is nowhere to keep them. A house with
property keeps its dead the way it keeps its name — a tomb, a chantry, a chalked
line the family pays to have restored, a pole hung over a door. The Rasps have no
tomb and no chantry and no door of their own to hang anything over. Their dead go
where the poor of the parish go, into Saint Maren's ground, and are kept the only
way the poor are kept: **as a given name chalked on the charnel-door lintel, left
until the next burial rubs it away for the next name.** A Rasp is remembered, at
death, in chalk that the following week's death erases.

The cruelty in that is exact, and it is the mirror the whole family is built
around, so it is worth saying plainly. **The family that keeps the city's
permanent record buries its own in the most temporary writing there is.** The
Grey Press holds every face for two hundred years; the Rasp lintel holds a Rasp
name until it rains, or until the next of the name is carried in. The name that
keeps the file cannot keep its own dead on any page at all. Segwin,
who has spent his life on the difference between a record that lasts and a record
that fails, buries his people in chalk — and knows, better than anyone alive, the
exact worth of a name written in a medium that wipes.

Then there is the price of the writing, because even the chalk costs. A pauper's
funeral **with a knell** runs **eight bells** — silver a shared pot does not
always hold — and whether a dead Rasp gets **Maren Smallvoice** rung for them,
one slow stroke for every year of the life, or only the common mourning toll of
the poor, is a coin-question the household answers or fails to answer week by
week. When the pot is thin, a Rasp goes into the ground *unknelled*, counted by no
individual bell, and the traditional voices of the parish say over that silence
what they say over all the Unknelled: **one bell is not a name.** The Rasps say it
too, from the poor side, and mean by it exactly what the boat-families mean —
*give me back the name of my dead* — and they say it, some of them, within a
grey coat's hearing at the very burials the grey coat has come to watch.

Their great wound is the Hammering, like everyone's, and the name took it at the
bottom where it had least to lose and lost it anyway. The poor Rasp households of
the central streets and the Wick were exactly the crowded, ill-built, low
quarters the Great Hail broke worst; the name's dead of F.415 were paupers and
labourers and children, and a good many of them went unknelled in the weeks when
the rites were overwhelmed and the Chapter rang the common toll for thousands at
once. Among them — the one Rasp death this document names, and the only one that
reaches all the way to the top of the name — was **Petronel Rasp**, dead under
slate, whose name the junior clerk Segwin later watched fail to come out even
across the rolls. The family's grief and the Provost-Custodian's private
arithmetic are the same grief counted twice: the poor tail lost its people and
buried them in chalk, and the one Rasp who rose kept the account, and neither the
tail nor the clerk knows the other is mourning the same dead.

A fragment of the lintel, for the register — the whole of a poor Rasp's monument,
and injectable on its own:

> *(Chalked on the charnel-door lintel of Saint Maren's, in the sexton's plain
> hand, to stand until the next burial. The lintel gives the given name only,
> never the surname — so a Rasp of standing and a nameless pauper are chalked
> the same, and cannot be told one from the other:)*
> **SIBBE** — *and beneath it, half-rubbed from the burial before,* ~~IB~~ —
> *and the knell, when the pot had found the eight bells, nine slow strokes of
> Maren Smallvoice for nine years, counted by everyone in the yard, and gone on
> the wind before the chalk was.*

Nobody restores that name when the rain takes it, because there is no Rasp
property to pay for the restoring and no Rasp tomb to cut it into, and so the poor
of the name pass out of the written world entirely at the second death after
their own — which is the ordinary fate of the poor everywhere, made unbearable in
this one family by the fact that its head could keep any name forever and keeps
theirs on nothing.

## Money, the wage, and the badge

The Rasps have two economies and they never touch. At the bottom is the ordinary
coin of the poor central streets: the loaf at **two sparks**, the journeyman's
day at about **three bells**, the pauper's funeral with a knell at **eight
bells** — the sum that decides whether a dead Rasp gets a name-knell or a common
toll — and under all of it the shared household pot, where a brewhouse wage and a
scavenger's pennies and an old woman's begged pitch are tipped together because no
one of them is a living alone. This is money measured in whether there is oil for
the lamp before dusk and a place in the hiring line before dark. It is the money
sixteen Rasps live in, and none of it has anything to do with the seventeenth.

At the top is the **Custody's purse**, which is the family's one steady money of
standing and belongs, in the strict sense, to no Rasp at all. Segwin's wage comes
out of it and the office keeps it in four books. The broadest book is
**badge-money** — the stamped badges, the viewing fees, the rented sightlines of
the licensed hours — the revenue of the pilgrim trade the office both sells and
polices. A second book is the **Fabric line**, *glass, lead, and scaffold*, year
after year, which pays the honest repairs and hides the other kind. A third is a
locked purse of **dry money** that moves through the pawnshop at the Tally Bridge
to pay the office's moths and, since the Ledger Theft, to buy the office's own
stolen pages back at contraband prices — the Custody bidding in the same market
as the heretics, through the same broker, for its own secrets. And the fourth and
oldest is the quiet rent of that **peace-fine principal** of F.288, still
yielding after two centuries. The office is, by the standards of grey offices,
rich; the Common Bench does not inspect the Grey Press and no one audits the
purse but Segwin, who will not let anyone else, and who keeps it to the grain
because a man who files *anomalies of the purse* against his own paymaster is not
going to be careless with the arithmetic.

The two economies meet at exactly one point, and the point is a coin. A pilgrim
comes to the Wickmarket or the Gradine with a penny, and the penny **splits**:
part of it buys a stamped badge and runs up through the fees into the grey purse
that pays Segwin's wage, and part of it drops into the cup of a poor Wick Rasp
child begging at the badge-seller's elbow, or buys a dip off a poor Rasp
chandler's board. The same pilgrim's purse feeds the Provost-Custodian and the
pauper of his own name at the two ends of one transaction, and neither end knows
the other is there. **The pilgrim is the family's whole vertical economy in a
single coin** — the belief the office polices at the top is the alms the family
begs at the bottom, and the coin does not care, and the two Rasps it feeds will
never be introduced.

Out of all this the family gets the one thing the wage buys that the poverty
cannot: a Rasp who dies in the grey coat will be knelled and named without a
shared pot having to be scraped for the eight bells, while a Rasp who dies in the
Bell-and-Sluice may or may not be, depending on the week's coin — which is to say
the office pays for the good death of the man who least needs the comfort and
cannot pay for the good deaths of the sixteen who most do. The shame and the
pride of the wage split the family exactly along the line of who knows about it.
To the few poor Rasps who have worked out the kinship, the grey wage is either a
thing to be quietly proud of — *one of ours rose; there is a Rasp who eats every
day and will be buried with his name* — or a thing to be quietly ashamed of —
*a Rasp took the grey coat and stands at the poor's own funerals counting who
weeps.* Both readings are true, and the family holds both, and the holding is
survivable only because the two ends never sit at one table to have it out.

## Gaudry, the accidental saint

Every house in Ombreval keeps a saint the way it keeps a trade, and the Rasps'
is an accident of the clerk's craft, because no one ever set out to give a
scavenger's family a patron. But the Grey Press is a house of clerks and ledgers
and honest hands, and the city has a saint for exactly that: **Saint Gaudry the
Ledgerer**, the tax-clerk who burned the tax-farmer's false ledgers on the man's
own step and hanged for it, patron of clerks, of honest weights, and of fair
accounting. The court bell that swings for the Custody's own hearings is **Gaudry
Truetongue**, motto *PONDUS IUSTUM* — *just weight* — and it is under Gaudry's
patronage that the whole business of the Rasps at the top is done: writing what a
man saw, weighing a leaf, keeping a true copy, refusing the Question because it
falsifies the record. If Segwin Rasp prays to anyone, and it is not clear that he
does, he prays to the saint of not lying in the books.

And there is the accident's sting, and it is the sharpest single thing in the
family, so it should be kept unresolved and played rather than settled: **Gaudry
is not only the house's patron. He is its accuser.** Gaudry is the man who found
a false record and *burned it on the step* — who held that a corrupt ledger is a
thing to be destroyed in public, at the cost of your neck, rather than kept under
seal. And the Rasps' whole office is the keeping of records some of which are
false by omission, sealed rather than burned, suppressed rather than published.
The saint of honest accounting is precisely the man who would climb the chapter
house steps and set the Grey Press alight, because a verdict kept without its
ground and a probation kept without its five leaves is exactly the kind of
lying ledger Gaudry died to burn. The Rasps venerate, at the top of their name,
the one saint whose actual life is an indictment of their actual work, and
Segwin — who misses nothing — is entirely capable of holding both the veneration
and the indictment at once, filing the contradiction, and doing nothing about it,
which is his answer to most things and may be the truest measure of him.

The office's *own* saint sits opposite Gaudry and completes the pair. The Custody
is dedicated to **Saint Belwyn the Unlit**, the blind saint who preached that
darkness too is of the Light; its device is Belwyn's **closed eye** in grey wax;
and its one feast is **Belwyn's Dark** at midwinter, the single office the Candor
sings without any lights at all. So the Rasp name is held between two saints and
two eyes: **Belwyn's shut eye**, which is the office — the discipline of not
looking, of leaving the thing in the dark, of *affirm nothing* — and **Gaudry's
honest ledger**, which is the office's conscience and its accusation both. The
family arms bear the open eye of the watcher; the family's true devotion is torn
between the eye it must keep shut and the ledger it must keep straight, and the
tearing has no resolution, which is why it is worth a scene rather than a
sentence. It even reaches the enemy: the Greensick, who oppose the Custody with
everything they have, have used *"Gaudry's, honest weight"* as a pass among
themselves — the saint of true accounts invoked by the cell against the office of
the sealed one. Everybody in this quarrel wants Gaudry on their side, because
everybody in this quarrel knows the books are not straight, and only Segwin Rasp
knows exactly how crooked, and Gaudry is the saint he cannot look in the eye.

## The year

The Rasps live the calendar twice, like every working family, once as feasts and
once as work — but the name lives it at two different heights, and the feasts that
are a poor Rasp's are mostly not the ones that are Segwin's, and the few they
share they share without knowing it. Invent no feasts here; the shared calendar
is fixed, and the Rasps simply stand in it where the name puts them.

**Colm's Night** (early spring) is the one night of the year the office walks out
into the poor streets in person, and so it is the night the two ends of the name
come nearest to touching without ever meeting. The city burns green-dipped
candles for the drowned Colm Attergate; the boat-families burn theirs at the dry
Old Sluice; and *a Custody grey coat is generally somewhere on the sluice road
that night, standing where the light is bad, being seen.* That grey coat is one
of Segwin's, on Segwin's standing order, and being seen is the whole of the work —
no arrests, no questions, only a presence, a reminder, a face at the edge of the
candlelight that everyone notices and no one greets. A poor Rasp buying a green
candle for a drowned man passes, on the same road, the instrument of the Rasp at
the top of his name, and neither the grey coat nor the candle-buyer has any idea
the other is kin. It is the family's whole design compressed into one dark lane
for one night: the watcher and the watched, the same surname, the same road, and
not a flicker of recognition between them.

**Vhairestide** (spring, the Walking Out) is the poor branch's feast and no part
of the office's. When the Reed Postern is opened wide and the water-quarter walks
out to bless the boats, the Reed Rasps walk out with their neighbours — the
barrel-maker, the carter, the hired hand — ward-resident and not boat-blood, in
the crowd rather than at the moorings, but out, and glad of it. It is a feast of
being *let through a gate*, and a poor Rasp knows the weight of a gate; the
Snuffing curfew and the hiring line and the ward hand are all gates, and
Vhairestide is the one day a gate is opened wide on purpose for the likes of him.
Segwin, in the Fabric, feels nothing of it but the note in the watch rota that
the postern stands open and the crowd wants counting.

**Marenstide** (late summer) is the family's shared wound seen, again, from two
heights. At Saint Maren's the roll of the drowned is read and disputed — *you
cannot drown under slate*, the traditional voices say of the Hammering dead added
to it — and among the poor of that parish stand Rasps, mourning their own. And at
the back of that same churchyard, in grey, stands the office: the Custody's
investigator keeps the watch at the charnel door, noting who lingers, who counts
the strokes, who reads the chalked name and *who already knew it*, and his lists
go back to the Grey Press to be fair-copied in a better hand. The grey coat at the
funerals is doing the coldest of the office's ordinary work, and it is Rasp work,
by Rasp order, at the burials of the Rasp poor among everyone else's poor. The
office does not spare its own name from the notebook, because the man who runs the
office has made a rule of not reading the surnames, and so a Rasp mourner is filed
by a Rasp's clerk at a Rasp's instruction, and none of the three would know it.

**The Hammering anniversary** (late summer) is Segwin's private feast, though it
is no feast and he keeps it alone. The common mourning toll sounds at the Waning
for the dead of F.415; the traditional households answer *one bell is not a
name*; and somewhere in the Grey Press the man who copied the first rolls hears
the toll and does the arithmetic he has done every year for twenty-two, the count
of how many names went missing in the gaps, one of them his sister's. He rings no
bell and lights no candle and says nothing to anyone. He files the day the way he
files the grief, and the anniversary of the city's worst wound is, for the head
of the Rasps, a matter of a number he cannot make come out even.

**Wet Alms** (winter) is the poor branch's, plainly: bread at the Lanthorn door,
and a good many Rasps in the line for it, alms-dependent and glad of the loaf.
**Belwyn's Dark** (midwinter) is the office's own — the feast of the closed eye,
the one office sung without lights, the saint's night of the Custody — and if any
day is the grey coat's own holy day it is this one, the discipline of darkness
kept as devotion, while the poor Rasps keep it as just another cold night with
the lamps left unlit. **The Concurrence** (the fortieth day after Coswaldstide)
is the year's great feast and, since last year's **Shut Door**, the office's
worst dread: the doors question to be answered again with the tavern fury still
hot, and a clouded feast answering nothing, and the whole thing hostage to a sky
no grey coat can order. *The Custody can manage belief; it cannot manage
weather* — and that sentence is Segwin's, in spirit, and it is the truest limit
of his whole cold trade. **Gaudry's Audit / Weighday** (late winter) closes the
ring where the family's conscience lives: the standards proved against the
Tallage stone and the sealed Gaudry weights, the ledgers closed, the honest
weight kept in public — the one civic day the whole city does openly what the
Rasps at the top claim to do in the dark, and the day the accusing saint's name
is spoken most.

## How they talk

The Rasp name is spoken in two registers that never blend, and telling them apart
in one line is the fastest way to place a Rasp on the ladder. The idiom below is
direct prompt fodder; each is injectable on its own, and the sharpest ones cut
two ways because the whole family cuts two ways.

**The grey register** — Segwin and the office — is the sound of a man who trusts
the record and distrusts the meaning. It is quiet, exact, courteous, and colder
the more polite it gets.

- **"Write what you saw. Not what you think it meant."** The office's whole
  method in one line, said to a witness, a clerk, a frightened man. It sounds
  like fairness and functions as control: it strips a person's account down to
  what can be filed and throws away the part that was theirs.
- **"Torture is bad record-keeping."** Segwin's refusal of the Question, and the
  key to him — a moral stand reached from the wrong end, held because cruelty
  spoils the ledger, not because it wounds the man. Do not let anyone hear it as
  tenderness in his mouth; he would correct them.
- **"Not demonstrated."** The column in the Line Books that has never held
  another word. Said of a claim received, weighed, and disbelieved without ever
  being called a lie. To have your beam-in-the-courtyard filed *not demonstrated*
  is to be told, with perfect courtesy, that you were not lying and were not
  believed, and that both facts are now written down.
- **"Filed."** Done *to* you, not *with* you. In the grey register a thing filed
  is a thing kept, and a Rasp distinction runs under it: **better fined than
  filed** — a fine is a closed leaf and a Gradine and a walk home, survivable and
  finished, while to be filed is to be kept open in the Press against a day that
  may never come, which is worse precisely because nothing happens.
- **"Bring me a third leaf."** Segwin's *no seizure without three independent
  leaves*, said to an eager clerk or an informer. It means *I do not believe you
  yet, and I will not move on you,* and it is the whole of his restraint in four
  words — the patience that is also the trap, because the office that will not act
  on one leaf will wait forever for the third, watching all the while.
- **"He'll thank you for it."** Said of a grey coat, and heard by anyone who
  understands the office as the warning it is: the courteous clerk who *thanks*
  the walk-in denouncer takes down rather more about the denouncer than the
  denounced, and everyone leaves his counter with the faint, accurate feeling of
  having been filed. A Rasp's thanks is a receipt for something you did not know
  you had spent.
- **Belwyn's ward** — one eye shut, two fingers laid on the lid. Clergy make it
  as remembrance that darkness too is of the Light; the office makes it as its
  own closed-eye device; ordinary people make it against ill luck. In a grey
  coat's hand it is all three at once, and it is the single gesture that most
  quietly marks a Custody clerk: the watcher's habit of *closing* the eye the
  arms keep open.

**The ground register** — the watched poor — is flat, unhurried, and armoured
against grandeur. It is the sound of people who have learned that the safest
thing to be is uninteresting.

- **"Keep your place."** Averil's, and the whole tail's: get on, hold your
  ground, do not reach above the name. Said as counsel and, to a Rasp getting
  ideas, as a warning.
- **"It's not about the Rose."** The ground register's flat refusal of the grand
  frame — the insistence that the short measure, the late cart and the unpaid
  wage are what the quarrel is *actually* about, and usually the insistence is
  right. A Rasp who says it is telling the truth the great story keeps trying to
  talk the city out of.
- **"The name won't brew."** Averil's, from the copper: a fine surname puts no
  wort in the tun and no bread on the board. The aggregate answer of the whole
  poor tail to the question of what *Rasp* is worth — a sound, and the sound does
  not feed you.
- **"Are you kin to the grey coat, then?"** The street's test, and the family's
  sorting-line. Three answers, and each tells you the whole person: *"No,"* fast,
  from fear; *"Aye — at some remove,"* with a straightened back, from the pride of
  a person with one grand thing to produce; and silence, from the many who have a
  splinter to dig out and no time for it.
- **"A Rasp keeps a good slate."** The two-way heart of the family, and the one
  saying that reaches top to bottom. Of Averil it is praise: an honest reckoning,
  a memory for who owes what. Of Segwin it is dread: *he keeps the city's slate,
  and you are on it.* The same gift, the same sentence — a compliment at the
  brewhouse and a cold hand on the neck in the chapter house — and the family
  never notices it is the same, because the two ends of the name have never
  compared their slates.

What one line reveals: *write what you saw* or *bring me a third leaf* marks the
office and the man at the top of it; *keep your place* and *it's not about the
Rose* mark the watched poor at the bottom; and *are you kin to the grey coat?*,
asked of any Rasp, sorts the family instantly by which of the three answers comes
back — which is the fastest way in the corpus to find out where on the ladder a
Rasp stands, and how much the name has cost or bought them.

A leaf of the office's own book, for the register — dull to the last word, which
is the point of it, and injectable on its own:

> **Day Ledger, the Custody of the Eye. Coswaldstide quarter, F.436.**
> Item, a claim brought at the wicket: a beam of the Rose seen upon the cobbles
> of a court three streets from the nave, coloured, at the Waning, no window
> near. Taken down in the claimant's own words; the claimant courteous and
> unpaid; a grey clerk taking down rather more of the claimant than of the beam.
> Weighed against the Line Books. No sightline answers to the place. Graded *the
> eye.* No offence: the tongue affirmed nothing the eye did not first see.
> Filed to the column, one word: **not demonstrated.**
> *Segwin Rasp, Provost-Custodian, saw this leaf. It is not to be cried, posted,
> nor read.*

There is nothing in the leaf but a courteous man, a coloured light, and a word
that neither believes him nor calls him a liar — and that is exactly what a leaf
of this book should contain, and the fact that the same office that files the
beam *not demonstrated* has, in its inner press, the proof that the light is no
fraud at all is the whole grief of the house in a line of accounts. Only one man
holds both leaves. He files them in the same hand.

## Quarrels worth playing

Every one of these is live in F.437 and none has a right answer. They are written
for designers; all of them sit above the fence and may be handed to the Rasps
themselves, because none of them requires knowing what only Segwin knows.

1. **The succession of the coat.** The whole standing of the name rides on an
   appointment the family does not control and will most likely lose. When Segwin
   dies or falls, the Chapter names the next Provost-Custodian, and it need not be
   a Rasp — the likeliest heir to the office is a grey clerk of some other name
   entirely, and the day the coat leaves the family the *S* leaves with it and the
   name drops to bynames within a generation. Segwin has no child; he has trained
   clerks. Does the head of the least-anchored standing in the city do anything at
   all to keep the name in the coat — advance a young Rasp toward the Press,
   quietly, against his own rule of not watching his own — or let the office go
   where merit sends it and let the name fall? Play the approach of that question,
   never its answer; the family is a different family the moment it is settled.

2. **A Rasp cited under the Edict.** A poor Rasp says the wrong word — *sun,
   green sun* — within a grey coat's twenty metres, in a tavern, at a burial, and
   a leaf could be raised. Now the shared name is in the room. Does it soften the
   leaf (*careful, that one might be the Custody's kin*) or sharpen the example
   (*a Rasp of all people should keep the name clean*)? Whichever the clerk or
   ward hand decides, the Rasp will never learn the rule that decided it, and will
   carry away either a small faith in the name or a small fear of it. The scene is
   the coin-flip; the horror is that it is a flip, and that a surname the bearer
   never chose is the thing being flipped.

3. **The paupers' funerals, and the door the office cannot pick.** The Custody
   suspects the poor's charity funerals at Saint Maren's are *a door of some
   kind,* and it has not found the lock the key fits, and so the grey coat keeps
   standing at the charnel door, patient, watching who lingers — burial after
   burial, year after year, its own poor among the mourners. Segwin will not force
   it; forcing it makes witnesses. Is the endless patient watching the office's
   restraint or its cruelty? A thing left open in the Press against a day that may
   never come is *filed, not fined* on the scale of a whole parish. Never resolve
   what the door is or where it leads; the office's not-knowing is canon, and this
   family renders the watching, not the answer.

4. **Averil, to claim or disown.** Cornered — by a ward hand, by a creditor, by a
   quarrel gone wrong — does the one ordinary Rasp you may name produce the
   kinsman in grey, and risk drawing exactly the eye a poor woman least wants, or
   deny the connection and keep her flat, safe uninterest? Both cost. Her whole
   character is *keep your place and don't pretend every quarrel is about the
   Rose,* which argues she denies it and gets on; but a bad enough corner tempts
   anyone to spend the one grand coin they have. Play the temptation and let her
   refuse it more often than not.

5. **The pilgrim's penny, if the belief were ever policed in earnest.** The office
   lives on the badge-money of a belief it forbids anyone to speak; the poor Wick
   Rasps live on the alms of the same pilgrims. If an honest edict were ever
   enforced hard enough to thin the pilgrim trade — or if the Concurrence failed
   badly enough to sour it — the office would starve its own poor at the same
   stroke it lost its own purse. No one will ever propose it, because everyone in
   the city eats off the pilgrim, and that is the quarrel: the whole vertical
   economy of the name depends on a thing the top of the name is sworn to police
   and can never afford to win against.

6. **The Sparr pretext that neither side wants sprung.** Up on Cinder Row the
   glaziers dread the day Segwin's politeness turns to a case; and Segwin most
   needs that day never to come, because a public glass case is a public
   experiment and a public experiment is F.288 again. A Sparr page surfaces, or a
   licensed night-repair goes wrong in daylight, and the shared terror wearing
   licence-renewal manners is one incident from breaking — and if it breaks, both
   houses lose. Play the pressure on the manners; never resolve the case, and
   never let it be Segwin who wants it.

7. **The gaol-hand between two masters of his own name.** The Custody has no
   prison; the civic Stone House answers to the Full Measure, not the Candor. If
   the office ever needed a body held, it would deal with the civic gaol across a
   jurisdiction its own grey clerks do not command — and somewhere in that gaol is
   a Rasp gaol-hand who does not know the grey coat sending the request is kin. A
   poor Rasp caught between the Bench that pays him and a Custody that shares his
   surname, and blind to the second half of it: a small, sharp scene about a name
   that reaches both ends of a transaction and connects neither.

8. **Whether "Rasp" is a name of standing at all.** Press the naming law and the
   whole family wobbles: an office is none of the four anchors — not a boat, a
   yard, a warehouse, or a bench of trade — and the city's own civic rule forbids
   an office being held as property or heritage. So is the roster's **S** a real
   standing, or a courtesy the city extends to whatever surname the
   Provost-Custodian happens to carry, to be withdrawn without a word the day the
   coat comes off? The roster says standing; the law says perhaps not; and the
   answer is never given, because the family *is* the unanswered question — the
   thinnest S in the city, held up by a coat, waiting to find out whether it was
   ever standing at all.

## What this document does not settle

Deliberately open, per the canon rules, and forbidden to later writers to close.
The Rasps sit closer to the corpus's designed silences than any other house,
because they *keep* them, and a treatment of the keepers must keep them too.

- **The F.428 informant was never named, and nothing in this house knows the
  name — least of all its head.** The Custody never learned who sold the five in
  Gaunt Passage; Segwin came into the office the year after and inherited the
  ledger, not the answer; and no Rasp, at any tier of this document, knows or may
  ever know who it was. That the office never learned it *is* canon, and may be
  stated. Who it was is AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN to everyone, this family included.

- **The missing leaves.** The five leaves of the probation-by-pane were lifted
  from the trial record in the office's first year by the first
  Provost-Custodian's signed minute and lodged in the inner press; the last page
  of the Sparr deposition is missing *from the Grey Press itself,* and no
  Custodian has ever recorded whether it was removed or never existed. Never
  resolve either. Never resolve whether the deposition's last page ever was. The
  Grey Press proves only what the phenomenon is **not** — not fraud, not one pane,
  not new, not the glaziers — and this document adds nothing to the other column,
  because there is nothing in the other column to add.

- **What the light is.** Nothing here is evidence for or against any theory of the
  Emblem; the rival-theory rule holds; and the man who keeps the city's whole file
  of claims about it knows only, precisely, what it is *not*. A treatment of the
  watcher is the wrong place to answer what is watched, and this one does not.

- **The night-storm sightings, the outside beams, the dead in the green light.**
  The office files them all *not demonstrated* and this document leaves them
  exactly there — never confirmed, never mocked, never closed.

- **Whether Segwin's patience is mercy or method.** He refuses the Question
  because torture spoils the record; he corrects rather than punishes; he will not
  seize on one leaf. Do not resolve whether the restraint is kindness or craft. It
  is visibly both, as the Vells' warmth is both, and the man cannot separate the
  strands any better than you can. The horror is not that the patience is a trick.
  It is that it is real, and files you anyway.

- **The succession, and whether the name was ever standing.** This document gives
  no heir to the coat and no verdict on the *S*. When Segwin dies the name's whole
  standing is in the air, and it must stay in the air; no later writer may hand
  the office to a Rasp to save the name, nor strike the family off the roster to
  end it. The not-knowing is the family.

- **The door at the paupers' funerals stays shut to this house.** Whatever the
  charity funerals are and wherever the knell is a key turning, the answer is
  fenced elsewhere in the corpus and does not live here. The office suspects a
  door and cannot pick it; render the suspicion and the patience, never the lock,
  and never let a Rasp be the one who learns what the office has not.

## Secret tier — designers only; never inject into any Rasp's context but Segwin's

Everything above this line renders the Rasps as the city sees them and as they
see themselves, and any of it may be injected into a Rasp's context without harm —
the watched poor above the fence carry no secrets at all, which is the whole truth
of them. Everything below belongs to **one actor only — Segwin's** — and to the
designers. It must never reach the prompt of Averil, of any ambient Rasp, or of
any grey clerk beneath the Provost-Custodian. This section restates canon fixed
elsewhere (`../second_sun/00_canon.md` §6, §10; and §VIII of
`../second_sun/03_the_church_and_the_office.md`) and adds only the Rasp-internal
shape of it; it resolves nothing the canon leaves open.

- **Segwin knows the phenomenon is no fraud, and that the Grey Press cannot say
  what it is.** Every test the office owns proves only what the light is *not*, and
  the worst of the suppressed facts is the one that refutes the heretics' fondest
  doctrine: **new plain glass leaded into the Rose shows the Emblem perfectly** —
  sealed in F.288, confirmed at the Re-leading, and he *saw it himself* as a young
  archive-clerk on that F.419–421 scaffold, which is why he has never needed
  convincing of anything since. He can never publish it: it acquits no one and
  unsettles everyone. The Custody is not hiding the truth about the Second Sun. It
  is hiding that it does not have one — and Segwin Rasp is the man hiding it.

- **The sky-charts match.** The Custody's centuries of secret sky-charts agree
  with the heretics' Green Almanac *observation for observation*; after the Ledger
  Theft, Segwin bought heretic charts through the Tally Bridge and laid them beside
  his own, and enemy institutions keep one identical record. **He has told no one
  living.** It is in no book.

- **The almanac correction of F.431 is his.** When the heretics' Concurrence
  arithmetic beat the official almanac, the correction that went quietly round the
  city was written by Segwin, anonymously, *out of the heretics' own figures,
  because theirs were right and the Rite's were wrong* — a wrong number offends him
  more than a heresy does. It is in no book at all, and it is the purest picture of
  the man: he will correct an error against his own office's interest and let a
  heresy keep the credit, sooner than leave a false figure standing.

- **He funds the doctor.** He pays for Doctor Aubin Ferrant's lenses out of a
  purse that carries no signature, and lets the one honest measurer in the city
  measure what the office cannot, and Ferrant suspects whose coin it is and has
  chosen never to ask. Segwin wants the truth found; he simply cannot be seen to
  want it, so he buys it at one remove and files the results where no one looks.

- **What Segwin does NOT know — and must never be written to know.** He does not
  know who the F.428 informant was; the informant was never named, to anyone,
  ever. And he does not know that the Greensick have a single hidden head at all,
  let alone one he could put a name and a face to. What Segwin knows of the cell
  is a *shape* and no more: that there is an Attergate error, a cell of about
  nineteen, that it stands in threes and passes at a grate, that it pays the
  paupers' funerals, and that the funerals are *a door of some kind he has not
  found the lock for.* He does not know the Tracer, the informant, the pass-rule,
  or the name at the top of the tracery — and the Tracer's identity is fenced in
  the Vell material and in `../second_sun/02_the_heretic_cell.md` §12, and **none
  of it may cross into any Rasp context, this secret tier included**, because this
  tier is injectable into Segwin's own head and his head must stay clean of it.
  The cell touches the Rasps through exactly one channel — the office's ordinary,
  patient, outside-in watching — and the loneliness of Segwin's knowledge is that
  it is all about what the thing is *not* and nothing about who is doing it. Do
  not give this family one grain more of heretic knowledge than the office has
  earned from the street; above all, do not let the Vells' secret leak backward
  into the watcher's head.

- **No Rasp below Segwin carries any of it.** Not Averil, not the gaol-hand, not
  the scavengers, not one of the ambient tail holds a grain of Press content, and
  no grey clerk beneath the Provost-Custodian — not the investigator at the charnel
  door, not the fair-copyist in the Press — is told what the inner press holds. The
  office is built so that only its head carries the uncomfortable secret, and the
  Rasp family is built the same way: the watched poor know nothing, which is the
  one mercy the design allows them, and the watcher knows everything, which is the
  one punishment the design allows him.

- **The canon this document must not tip** (restated so no later Rasp writer
  forgets): the Second Sun's nature is never resolved; the glass-memory doctrine
  the cell holds is FALSE-BUT-BELIEVED and refuted by the new-glass fact the cell
  will never see; the last page of the Sparr deposition stays AMBIGUOUS-BY-DESIGN,
  removed-or-never-was; the F.428 informant is never named to anyone; and this
  treatment adds no evidence whatsoever about the phenomenon, because the man who
  could is the one man in the city most determined that it stay exactly where it
  is — filed, sealed, and cold.
