# The Great Rains, the Hammering, and the Long Departure

> Shared canon for the disasters that reduced Ombreval from a city of roughly
> 15,000 people to one of roughly 5,000. This is author-facing history, not a
> single in-world account: public records, family memories, and institutional
> versions disagree in emphasis and sometimes in honesty.

## Canon in brief

Three names describe three different scales of the same decline:

- **The Great Rains** were the three weeks of flooding in F.362 and the years
  of river works that followed. They broke the Cut, displaced the old
  waterside economy, and began a long population decline.
- **The Great Hail** is the formal name for the extraordinarily localized
  hailstorm of F.415. In ordinary speech it is **the Hammering**. It lasted
  perhaps ten or fifteen minutes and killed thousands through impact, falling
  roofs, fire, flooding, injury, and exposure.
- **The Long Departure** is the demographic aftermath: first the slow loss of
  river households after the Rains, then the much larger departure of
  survivors in the twenty-two years after the Hammering.

The hail was physically real. Its cause is not established. It may have been a
natural event of appalling improbability; no evidence proves that it was
supernatural, and no later event answers the question. It did not make the
impossible light change, act, choose, or explain itself.

## The Great Rains, F.362

Before F.362, Ombreval held approximately **15,000 people**. The Cut still
carried the Serle through the southern third of the city, feeding the Tallage,
Maren's Green, warehouses, cellars, workshops, and old river households whose
buildings opened directly onto the channel.

In F.362 rain fell for three weeks under an unbroken overcast. The Serle rose
into streets and ground floors. The Cut's banks and retaining works failed in
several places; cellars filled, stores spoiled, timber footings shifted, and
lower masonry remained wet long after the surface water withdrew. Boats moved
through Maren's Green. Grain, wool, salt, fuel, and household winter stores
were lost together. The damage was not one dramatic breach but a city-wide
accumulation of wet foundations, fouled wells, rotten timber, debt, and work
that could no longer be done where it had always been done.

The Rains also have a separate place in the history of the Great Rose. For the
three clouded weeks, the cold disc visible through the Rose was the only thing
in Ombreval that looked like sunlight. The Chapter called this proof that the
Light kept faith; forty people were arrested for drawing another conclusion.
Wet Alms, the winter giving of bread at the Lanthorn door, began afterward.
These facts matter to the Custody and the Unwalled, but they did not drain one
cellar, replace one beam, or feed the households that left.

### Closing the Cut, F.363–369

The damaged Cut settled an old argument about the river. Over the following
seven years it was stopped and filled, and the Serle was brought into the
navigable bed it still keeps beyond the south wall. Freight thereafter landed
at the outer wharves and entered by cart and porter. Bridges, moorings, slips,
and the Old Sluice kept their water-working names on dry ground.

Some interests prospered from the new arrangement. The Tallage continued to
collect, carriers gained an extra journey, and the strongest boat and
warehouse families acquired abandoned rivals' business. That prosperity was
uneven. Old bank-side houses lost their reason for being, small boat families
left or changed trades, warehouses stood too far from the new landing places,
and poorer owners could not both shore flood-damaged foundations and adapt to
overland freight. The official boast that “the boat-families cursed the carts,
the Tallage cursed the cost, and both prospered” remembers the winners.

By the end of the works, Ombreval probably held **11,000–12,000 people**. No
single reckoning survives. The difference includes deaths, households that
moved with the river trade, and families that left during seven expensive
years of rebuilding.

## The weakened city, F.370–414

Ombreval remained a functioning and important market city. It did not become
a ruin, and trade did not cease. It also never recovered its former
population. Rents fell in the old river quarters while cartage and toll costs
rose. Empty upper rooms became cheap lodgings or stores. Owners patched
shifted walls instead of rebuilding them; neighbours shared party walls whose
legal ownership was clearer than their physical condition.

The most dangerous legacy lay out of sight. Flood-weakened footings carried
new jetties, tile, lofts, hoists, and decades of small alterations. The Common
Bench, guilds, Chapter landlords, and private owners all knew that parts of
the city needed shoring. Surveys were incomplete, orders were bargained down,
and repairs followed wealth. Nothing in an ordinary year forced every hidden
weakness to fail at once.

## The Great Hail, F.415

The surviving civic reckoning places the storm on a **late-summer
Highmarket**, shortly before the **Waning** bell. The Wickmarket, Coswald's
Yard, and the Draper's Reach were crowded; workshops were open; carts and
porters filled the approaches. Crops and orchards in Ombreval's nearest fields
were still standing.

Accounts agree on a darkening sky and very little else before the first
impacts. Some witnesses remembered a low roar. Others insisted the first
warning was tile breaking beside them. The hail fell over the walled city and
its nearest fields for perhaps ten or fifteen minutes. Its edge was
extraordinarily local, but not a clean circle, and later witnesses moved it to
fit whatever meaning they gave the disaster.

The largest stones were far beyond ordinary hail. Comparisons in testimony—an
apple, a fist, a loaf, a paving stone—are neither consistent nor useful as
measurements. What is fixed is what the stones did: they broke slate and tile,
punched through shingles and thin roof boards, smashed shutters and glass,
injured people beneath awnings, and broke scaffold poles and joints. No
hailstone survived to be weighed.

### How the built city became the weapon

The direct blows were only the beginning:

- People in pinched streets could neither see the storm coming nor move a
  crowd quickly toward shelter.
- Jettied houses shed tile, slate, plaster, timber, tools, and gutters into the
  lanes below. One roof's wreckage struck the next storey down.
- Sudden impact and accumulating ice brought flood-weakened footings, party
  walls, old jetties, and overloaded lofts down together.
- Chimneys broke while hearths and furnaces were burning. Roof and loft fires
  began behind streets already blocked by ice, wreckage, animals, and people.
- Water conduits and cistern covers broke. Ice heaped in courts and passages;
  its meltwater then flooded cellars, crypts, stores, and people trapped below
  collapsed floors.
- Market roofs and warehouse coverings failed over grain, wool, salt, fuel,
  and winter provisions. The fields immediately outside the walls lost crops,
  fruit, and many animals at the same time.

For a few minutes, nearly every ordinary protection offered by a dense city
became another source of injury.

### Bells, shelter, and responsibility

Ombreval had no bell pattern for falling ice. Coswald Ironthroat was rung for
the first major collapses and fires; the full backward ring called the Ruin
only after there were fires, blocked streets, and rising meltwater enough to
name a familiar kind of emergency. Critics later called the delay fatal.
Ringers answered that the first hailstones were the only warning anyone had.
Both statements are true.

Covered passages and stone arcades saved many lives. They also drew crowds
under structures that had not been built for impact and sudden weight. At the
Draper's Reach, the lower arcade and some roofed bays held while adjoining
roofs, lofts, and upper walls failed. A central bonded wool store remained
comparatively dry. The Chapter's Long Book later reduced the whole disaster to
the sentence that hail broke the cloth-hall tiles and the Reach kept the wool
dry. The sentence is factually defensible and deliberately obscene in what it
leaves out.

Responsibility was broad enough for every institution to point elsewhere.
Property owners had resisted shoring orders. Guilds had defended their own
inspection customs. The Common Bench had neither completed its post-Rains
surveys nor found money to enforce them. Chapter-owned houses, Fabric
scaffolds, and church property failed alongside private buildings. Relief
afterward favoured sound ledgers, established households, bonded stores, and
buildings whose owners could make claims.

The Long Book's miniature entry is deliberate institutional minimization. No
surviving order proves that one officer dictated it; it expresses the Chapter's
collective interest in remembering weather, not preventable weakness, as the
cause of death.

### The Lanthorn and the Great Rose

The Hammering broke ordinary cathedral glazing, roof slate, carved figures,
shutters, gutters, and lesser scaffolding. The Great Rose's stone tracery held.
Many pieces cracked in their leads and portions of the leadwork loosened, but
the Rose did not fall and no great public hole stood open in it. Emergency
canvas and boarding went up before unrestricted access to the nave resumed.

The repairs culminated in the full F.419–421 Re-leading. Publicly this was the
proper repair of the Fabric's most valuable window after the Hammering.
Privately the Custody used the work for its largest controlled test of the
appearance in the Rose. The Chapter's ability to fund two years of meticulous
glass work while households still lived beneath patched timber remains a
source of resentment. Nothing about the cold disc changed during the hail or
the repairs.

## Deaths and the unknelled

No exact death roll is possible. Entire households died together; bodies were
not all recovered; migrants, servants, lodgers, market visitors, and workers
from outside the walls were poorly recorded. The best author-facing estimates
are:

- **2,000–3,000** killed during the hail or the first collapses and fires;
- perhaps **another 1,000** dead in the following weeks from crushed and
  infected wounds, exposure, later collapses, spoiled provisions, and hunger.

Churchyards and ordinary funeral labour could not receive so many. Bodies were
laid in shared graves, including emergency trenches beyond the walls and old
burial ground among the reeds. Clerks and parish officers assembled
**Hammering rolls** from household books, guild lists, rent accounts, and the
names supplied by survivors. Their spellings, omissions, and duplicate names
are still disputed.

At Saint Maren's, the name-knell ordinarily gives one slow stroke of Maren
Smallvoice for every year of a buried person's life. That counting could not
be performed for thousands of dead. The Chapter authorized common mourning
tolls while rescue and burial continued and later declared that common rite
sufficient for those who had received no individual bell.

Many observant and traditional households have never accepted this. They call
their dead **the unknelled**, or collectively **the Unknelled**, and repeat the
objection **“one bell is not a name.”** Families still pay for belated
name-knells when an omitted relative is identified in a Hammering roll or when
they can finally afford the rite. At the anniversary's Waning bell, some
people count the years their dead should have been given; others refuse the
common toll altogether. The grievance is strongest among traditional
boat-families, poorer parishes, funeral workers, and clergy who believe correct
observance matters even when the Chapter prefers closure.

This grievance concerns human remembrance, not proof that the dead answer or
that any rite has a supernatural effect.

## The Long Departure, F.415–437

The Hammering did not empty Ombreval in one day. Survivors first moved in with
kin or into sound ground floors. Then households began leaving for nearby
farms, the outer wharves, Brede, Harne, Salorge, and wherever a trade or family
connection offered a roof. Approximately **3,000–4,000 people** departed over
the following twenty-two years, although births, ordinary deaths, arrivals,
and returns make this a trajectory rather than an account that sums exactly.

Their reasons were material:

- repairs often cost more than a damaged house was worth;
- whole households dying together left ownership divided among distant,
  unknown, infant, or competing heirs;
- fewer customers and apprentices weakened trades already damaged by the
  river's diversion;
- food, fuel, rent, guild or fraternity relief, and credit all became harder
  to secure in a smaller and riskier city;
- outsiders increasingly treated Ombreval as unlucky, reducing marriage,
  investment, and long-term settlement even while ordinary trade and
  pilgrimage continued;
- each departing household made the next departure easier: a sibling already
  in Brede, a cousin at Salorge, an empty neighbouring house, one fewer reason
  to remain.

The Long Departure is not a curse or compulsion. People left because the city
was damaged, expensive, economically weaker, and widely believed to be
accursed. Approximately **5,000 people** remain in F.437.

## Ombreval in F.437

Twenty-two years later, the city is neither restored nor abandoned:

- Roofs carry mismatched slate, tile, shingles, scavenged lead, and repairs
  whose seams are visible from the street.
- Some ground floors remain occupied beneath empty or storage-only upper
  storeys. A family may heat two rooms in a house built for three households.
- Soft limestone, statues, bells, shutters, gutters, and old metalwork retain
  pits and flattened edges called Hammering scars.
- Several small courts are called **widow courts** in local speech because most
  households there lost someone. It is a description, not one official place
  name.
- Residents move toward arches and covered passages when a summer sky darkens,
  even while knowing that some such shelters failed. Market awnings and stall
  roofs are built conspicuously heavy.
- Building disputes turn on pre-Hammering walls, shared repair bills, absent
  heirs, occupied ground floors, and owners who left in the Long Departure.
- The Draper's Reach still trades, measures cloth, and shelters a working
  route, but several bays and upper rooms never returned to their former use.
- Outsiders call Ombreval unlucky. Residents resent the judgment, exploit it
  when bargaining, and sometimes believe it in private.

Flood history survives chiefly below the waist: salt bloom, blocked bank
doors, filled steps, mismatched lower masonry. Hammering history survives above
the eye: patched roofs, shortened jetties, braced walls, scarred stone, and
empty upper windows.

## Speech and shared knowledge

Everyone raised in Ombreval knows the Great Rains broke the Cut, the Hammering
killed thousands, and the Long Departure left the city too large for its
people. They do not know an exact death toll or a single agreed cause. Adults
old enough to remember F.415 usually remember one place, one sound, one person,
or one piece of shelter—not an aerial account of the whole city.

Useful ordinary phrases include:

> “That roof has stood since before the Hammering.”

> “Her mother went under slate in the Hammering.”

> “They went downriver in the Long Departure and left us their empty house.”

> “One bell is not a name.”

> “Hammer-weather.” — a dark, abruptly cold summer sky, whether or not hail
> follows.

Formal Chapter and civic writing normally says **the Great Hail**. Ordinary
speech normally says **the Hammering**. A person insisting on the formal name
is likely reading, defending an institution, or avoiding a personal word.

## Authoring guardrails

- Do not establish a supernatural cause, an agent behind the storm, a surviving
  impossible hailstone, or a future revelation that solves it.
- Do not make the Hammering an action of the impossible light. The light did
  nothing and behaved exactly as it always did.
- Do not replace diffuse negligence with a mastermind, planned sacrifice, or
  deliberate mass killing. The institutions minimized their failures; they did
  not arrange the storm or desire the deaths.
- Do not make every resident's defining story the disaster. It is shared
  history; individual lives select different parts of it.
- Keep the death figures approximate in author-facing prose and more uncertain
  in dialogue.
- Preserve the difference between public euphemism, family memory, rumor, and
  writer-level fact.
- Let the aftermath remain useful to ordinary stories: rent, inheritance,
  roofs, trade, burial, migration, grudges, repairs, and who has the key to an
  empty room.
