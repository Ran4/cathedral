# The Long Book: Annals of Ombreval

> Use: Diegetic chronicle the player may find chained in the Lanthorn's chapter house; inject whole or in dated slices into sidecar NPC context as the city's shared public timeline (it contains no Custody secrets, only what the city was allowed to remember).
> Canon: 00_canon.md

*Pasted inside the front board, in a small dry hand:*

*This is the Long Book of the Great Church of Saint Ambrelle at Ombreval, begun with the first stone and continued by such canons as could be spared to it. Its years are the years of the Fabric. It has been written by many hands, some better than others, and some honester. Since the founding of the Custody of the Eye the quires are read in the grey office before they are sewn; the reader is asked to remember this, and to read the margins.*

---

**F.1.** In this year the first stone of the great church was laid at the old ford-market, upon Saint Vhairé's ground, the Serle running high. Two oxen were given to the poor of the ford. The stone was laid dry; the rains came after, which was taken well.

**F.9.** The Serle rose over the ford and took the timber bridge and eleven souls, of whom the Maker keeps the count. Saint Vhairé was carried about the walls, and the water went down in its own time.

**F.22.** A great dying in the city, called after the sweating death. It spared neither the fisher nor the canon. The dead of the ford-quarter were laid in one pit among the reeds, and the grass there is greener to this day.

**F.38.** The Serle froze at the ford, and wolves came over the ice into the shambles. Three were killed under the watch-bell at the Bellstand, and one was not.

**F.54.** The lord of Harne closed the river below the city and took the salt-boats, claiming the Serle tolls. Peace was bought for four hundred marks, of which the town says the Church gave nothing. The town lies, but not by much.

**F.61.** The quire was vaulted and sung in, and the Praelucent said the first rite at the high altar, sixty years from the first stone. The masons stood at the back and wept, those that were not on the roof.

**F.68.** The old watch-bell at the Bellstand cracked in the ringing of an alarm that proved false. It was recast; the proclamations were cried unbelled for a season, and less believed.

**F.83.** In this year the cutting of the Serle was begun, to bring the water straight through the city; and in six years it was done, and is called the Cut. The Tallage was appointed beside it for the weighing of tolls, and in the year following the Tally Bridge was raised there, of timber.

**F.97.** The church of Saint Maren of the Reeds was consecrated upon the Cut, for the boatmen and the poor. Her crypt stands low, and the water knows it.

**F.112.** A calf with two heads was shown at Lowmarket and died. A star with a tail stood over the works the better part of a month, and the masons would not go up until it went.

**F.132.** The great scaffold at the crossing fell in the octave of Coswaldstide, and seven masons and a boy fell with it. The lodge buried them in one grave with their tools. Since that year the scaffolds are blessed at Coswaldstide, every pole and every knot.

**F.146.** The sun was eaten at midday, and the day went to ash, and the birds fell silent. One Belwyn, a Taper of this church, would not turn his eyes from it, and was blind after, and preached until his death that darkness too is of the Light. He is now among the saints, and the eclipse is in his picture.

**F.157.** Fire among the tanneries. The reeds burned down to the water, and the stink outlived the flames by a year.

**F.171.** In this year the glaziers' street burned, the fire beginning in the furnace of the Sparrs, and four died, and the street was rebuilt in stone below and timber above, and is called Cinder Row since. The ash of that fire, some say, went into the melt for years after; the glaziers say ash is ash.

**F.183.** The rye failed twice. Bread was cried at the Bellstand at a price not to be written. The Chapter opened its granary in the hungry weeks of spring, later than the town wished, sooner than the town says.

**F.198.** In this year the great rose of the west front was begun under Master Idonea Sparr of Cinder Row, a woman, and the best fire of her line. The Chapter gave her all she asked, which was much, and worth it.

**F.203.** On the day the last panel of the great rose was set, Master Sparr went down and looked back through her finished window from the crossing, and wept before witnesses, and said that she had made two of something there was one of. She never fired glass again. What she meant she did not say, and the Chapter did not ask.

**F.204.** The Serle rose at Lowmarket and stood in the crypt of Saint Maren nine days. An otter was taken in the nave of that church, which was thought a wonder, but it was only an otter.

**F.207.** Hail broke tiles from the Bellstand to the water. A quiet year otherwise, and the Book is glad of it.

**F.211.** Master Idonea Sparr died, and was buried with the honour of her guild. In the same year a recluse was enclosed in the anchorhold in the north wall of the Ilvane Chapel, the first of that line, which has not broken since.

*At the foot of these years, a later hand:* From the setting of the last panel to the year 219 the Book has nothing of the Rose. Whether there was nothing to see, or nothing it was wise to write, the Book does not say, and I will not guess. — T.

**F.219.** In this year a Taper wrote in the margin of a choir-book that the sky doubled behind the rose. The Chapter ruled it a fault of the eye, and set him to the kitchens, and the margin was scraped. It is the first such writing. It was not the last.

**F.229.** The Cut froze from the Tally Bridge to the reeds and bore carts, six weeks together. An ox was roasted whole upon the ice at the Tallage; the butchers feasted and the boatmen went hungry, and neither has let the other forget it.

**F.240.** In this year pilgrims came out of number, to stand in the nave at dawn for the [*here a word is scraped, and* Emblem *set above in a later hand*] that stands in the rose. The Chapter appointed hours of viewing, and badges of lead were struck and sold at the west door, and the great church, which all men now call the Lanthorn, was never empty at first light.

**F.242.** The Chapter appointed the Feast of the Sole Light, to be kept the fortieth day after Coswaldstide, when the crossed beams stand in the eye and every shadow in the nave is made one; the feast to declare that the Light is one, whatever stands in the glass. It was kept the first time this year, in fair weather, and the city wept, no man could well say why.

**F.244.** In this year came the Edict, cried and posted at the Bellstand: that there are not two suns, but one Light and one Emblem of it, granted by grace; and that to say of the Emblem *sun*, or *second sun*, or *the green sun*, is matter for the court. The fines are set out in the Edict. The words of the folk are not.

*In the margin, a later hand:* The Edict reaches forward, not back. The old leaves stand as written, save where some officious brother has scraped them.

**F.247.** The Tally Bridge was rebuilt in stone, the timber being rotten and the tolls being not.

**F.251.** A dying of children in the city, called the child-pox. In the year after, Maren Smallvoice was cast for the passing-bell of Saint Maren's, and the custom begun of the name-knell, one stroke for each year of the life. For the children the strokes are few, and heard.

**F.263.** The gallery of the cloth halls was roofed over, and is called the Draper's Reach. The drapers paid for it themselves and have mentioned it since.

**F.271.** A sturgeon of eleven feet was taken at Maren's Green and sent to the Praelucent's table. The boatmen kept the roe, and said nothing, and were right to.

**F.288.** In this year the lodge of the masons accused the guild of the glaziers, before the Praelucent's court, of sorcery in glass. Three journeymen were put to the Question, and were after carried out, not walking, two to their houses and one to his grave. The court made a trial of the glass itself, behind doors. What it found was not read out. The glaziers paid a fine of peace, for a crime no man was hanged for; the masons paid nothing, and got nothing. Neither guild has forgiven the other, nor the court, and Coswaldstide has a brawl in it every year since, as regular as the blessing.

**F.289.** The Custody of the Eye was founded and seated in the chapter house, under the patronage of Saint Belwyn the Unlit, its emblem a closed eye: to keep the Great Rose, to order the pilgrims, and to hold such records as are the court's. Its clerks go in grey. What else it holds, it holds.

**F.290.** The Ilvane Chapel being found unsound, its doors were mortared up by the Custody's masons. The anchorhold in the north wall may not be closed upon a living recluse; the squint stays open on the lane, and the recluse within it, and alms may be left as before.

**F.301.** In this year one Colm Attergate, a labourer of the west towers, preached at the Bellstand that the towers were coffin lids, and that the Church was walling a sun out of the sky, course by course. He was taken and banished the city. Coming back by water at night, he drowned at the Serle sluice. The folk have made more of him than he was. *A later hand:* Or less.

**F.309.** In this year the bourdon was cast in Coswald's Yard, at the third pouring, the first two cracking in the pit; and was named Great Ambrelle, called Gravemouth, with SOLI LUCI CANO upon her waist; and was raised into the south tower with treadwheels and prayer, and first rung at Coswaldstide, and heard, they say, at Harne.

**F.311.** The procession of glass at Saint Ambrelle's feast went this year for the first time from Cinder Row to the Gradine, as it goes still, every pane carried like a child.

**F.312.** Four lesser bells were cast for the ring, and baptised, and their names entered in the register of the sacristy, where names are safe.

**F.323.** The Serle rose in the spring, and the crypt of Saint Maren flooded, and the dead were disordered. Since that year the sexton chalks upon the lintel of the charnel door the name of each soul buried, and leaves it until the next, that the names be not lost to the water.

**F.334.** The Cut froze again, though not as in the great frost of F.229, which stood nine weeks. *A later hand:* Thus in the Book; the entry of that year says six. Frost grows in the telling.

**F.341.** Fire in the sacristy, from a censer left swinging. It took vestments, the wax-stores, and a leaf of the bell-register, wherein one of the four bells of F.312 had her name. She is called the Orphan since, for no man living remembers her baptism, and the folk will not be wed under her voice.

**F.347.** The fortieth day after Coswaldstide was clouded past noon, and the shadows did not fuse, and the feast was said and not seen. The folk called the year unblessed, which is forbidden, and said it anyway.

**F.352.** A murrain of cattle out of the east. The Tallage stank of hides until the frosts.

**F.362.** In this year fell the Great Rains, three weeks together and no sun over Ombreval, the Serle at the door-sills and the boats rowing in Maren's Green. The Cut broke, stores and lower houses were drowned, and foundations took water that some hold yet. In all the city the only sunlight stood in the nave, where the Emblem burned through the wrack as a pale coin, morning to evening, every day of the three weeks. The Praelucent preached that the Light kept faith with the city, and the city believed it, being wet. Bread was given at the nave door, and is given still upon that day, which is called Wet Alms. Certain persons who preached another sense of the matter were taken and corrected.

**F.369.** In the seven years after the Great Rains the broken Cut was stopped and filled, and the Serle was brought to the navigable bed it keeps beyond the south wall. The boat-families cursed the carts, the Tallage cursed the cost, and both prospered. The Tally Bridge, the Eel Bridge, the Alder Moorings, and the Old Sluice kept their names though water left them, because an Ombreval clerk will sooner move a river than alter four ledgers.

**F.371.** Two burned houses in the weavers' lane were rebuilt, each a half-step forward of its old line, by whose greed the lane is now a shoulder's width. It is called the Needle since, and a proverb has grown in it, and neither owner will yield the half-step, and neither can now sell.

**F.383.** A great storm at midday in high summer, such that lightning stood upon the towers like candles. Those at prayer in the nave say that at every stroke each light of the rose went white, save where the Emblem stood, which did not change. This is written as it was sworn to the Chapter, by the light that does not lie.

**F.390.** The crown of the north tower was scaffolded for the finishing of the Fabric, which is at hand.

**F.398.** In this year the Fabric was declared complete, three hundred ninety and eight years from the first stone, and the great praise was sung in the nave with every bell in the city. The scaffold on the north tower crown was left standing, for the little that remained. It stands yet.

**F.401.** Came the Luminary's letter from Ostrelle, blessing the completed Fabric: three years in the coming, and six weeks on the road, the canons said, the delay being in the writing and not the walking. It was read from the Gradine to those who stayed to hear it.

**F.407.** Tallow fire at the Wickmarket; six stalls burned, and the wax ran in the gutters, and the poor scooped it while it was warm, and the chandlers let them.

**F.415.** Hail broke the tiles of the cloth halls; the Draper's Reach kept the wool dry, and the drapers boasted of it for a year, which was long.

*In the margin, a later hand:* The wool was dry. The people under the western loft were not.

**F.419.** In this year the Great Rose was scaffolded and canvased for the re-leading of the panes, cracks and loosened lead having been left by the hail, the Fabric's greatest care since the completion; and the work held two years. The dawn crowds were turned from the nave, and the badges sold poorly, and the pilgrims were bitter. The Custody's clerks kept tally of every pane, it is said, to the least piece of it. In F.421 the canvas came down at Saint Ambrelle's feast, and the rose was as it had been, in every particular, and the city let out its breath.

**F.428.** On a night after Lowmarket the Custody took five persons at the Gaunt Passage, behind the salt-cellars. Two abjured their error upon the Gradine and were received back. A fuller died in the Custody's keeping, of the damp, before he could be tried. Who gave the word was never said. The city has not done whispering about the whisperers, nor will soon.

**F.431.** By a fault of the computists, the Feast of the Sole Light was cried for the wrong day: the shadows fused upon the eve, before a few at their private prayers, and on the feast noon the bells rang to an empty crossing, and the eye stood plain. The almanac was mended for the year following. No fault was published, and no thanks either, though the mending came from somewhere.

**F.433.** In this year a clerk of the Custody died, as the Custody gave out, or went out of the city, as the street gives out; a satchel of papers went abroad in either case. Since that year there is grey custom at the pawnshops, and a leaf of old writing fetches more at the Tally Bridge than a silver spoon, which is against nature.

**F.436.** At the Concurrence this year the Chapter, fearing the press of the crowd, barred the nave doors at the hour of the Passing, which was never done in any year before, fair or foul. There was a crush upon the Gradine, and a child's arm was broken in it, and other hurts beside. Within, before some hundreds, the shadows fused as ever; without, some thousands saw a shut door, and have talked of nothing else since. The taverns are not yet empty of it. *Written in the octave of the feast.*

**F.437.** This present year. The rye stands fair; the Serle keeps its bed beyond the south wall; the scaffold on the north tower crown stands, as it has stood since F.390, and the ravens nest in it. The Concurrence comes on, the fortieth day after Coswaldstide, as always. Whether the doors will be barred again the Chapter has not said; the Praelucent, Havise Dorn, lamp-named Serene, is asked it in the street, and does not answer. All watch the sky, for a clouded feast answers nothing, and answers are wanted this year as bread was wanted in F.183. Here the Book waits, as the city does.
