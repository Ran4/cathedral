# Beyond the Walls: Ostrelle, the River Roads, and What Outsiders Say

> Use: structural/meta reference on the wider world — excerpt into context for merchants, pilgrims, boat-folk, and any NPC asked about elsewhere; the travelers' block at the end is directly injectable. Everything beyond the walls is secondhand in Ombreval, and stays that way: no scene is ever set outside the city.
> Canon: 00_canon.md

Ombreval's map of the world is short, and the city likes it short. The world beyond the walls exists in this corpus only as far as a boatman can describe it — a river with two ends, one lord's town, one far capital, and a widening ring of people who have heard something about a window.

## The Serle, above and below

The Serle rises in the Combs, the sheep-hills west of the city — nobody in Ombreval has seen the spring, and the boatmen say it does not matter where a river starts, only where it lets you tie up. The head of navigation is **Brede**, the wool-stack town three days' pole upstream. Wool comes down from Brede; the cloth halls full it, tenter it, and measure it against the ell of Ombreval; finished cloth goes back out both ways, up to the hills and down to the sea. This is the cloth road, and half the Draper's Reach lives on it.

Below the city the Serle runs east through flat country, past Harne, and finds the sea at **Salorge**, among the salt-pans — six days down with the current, twelve back up against it. Salt comes up: pan-salt in barges, weighed at Lowmarket, stored in the cellars off the Tallage that keep the Gaunt name. This is the salt road, and Grigor Ashe's lawful living. The river keeps its one name from the Combs to the pans; nobody renames water, and the boat-folk accordingly think of the world as one water with many tolls on it.

Pilgrim traffic, surging since the F.240s, follows the trade: up with the salt, down with the wool, afoot along the towpaths in the dry months.

## Harne

Four leagues below the city stands Harne, a lord's town with a castle over the river and, in the old days, a chain across it. In F.54 the lord of Harne closed the river, took the salt-boats, and sold peace back to Ombreval for four hundred marks. "To buy peace of Harne" still means paying an extortionate price you cannot refuse, and the present lord — who takes a lawful toll and nothing more — inherits the curse with the castle.

Harne is also the far edge of the city's voice: when Great Ambrelle, called Gravemouth, was first rung in F.309, she was heard, they say, at Harne, and no farther place has ever claimed her. On still winter mornings Harne folk hear the bourdon across the flats and say, "Ombreval is preaching again."

Being nearest, Harne is the least impressed. Its version of the Emblem is the most accurate one spoken outside the walls and the most dismissive: "a light in their window, and a fine if you name it." Harne is privately certain the fine, not the light, is the point — a town that once sold a river back to its neighbors assumes everyone else is selling something too (false as judgment, fair as history).

## Ostrelle

Far off — six weeks' carrying by the Lantern Road, through two toll-gates — sits primatial Ostrelle: the Luminary's seat, the great school, the mint that strikes the gold lantern. Nobody in Ombreval has seen it, or nearly nobody; the painter of the rose panel that shows the Luminary in Ostrelle never went, and painted its cathedral as the Lanthorn drawn again, smaller, which is how the whole city imagines it.

What Ombreval knows of Ostrelle fits in a pocket: a coin, a rank, and a letter. The coin is the lantern, mostly money of account. The rank is the Luminary, supreme head of the Candor, invoked, feared, ignored — and it is a standing fact, remarked at every installation, that no Luminary has visited Ombreval in living memory. The blessing of the completed Fabric came from Ostrelle in F.401, three years upon the road; the carriers make the same road in six weeks, and the Tallage draws its own conclusion about the difference between freight and grace. (That the Custody prefers the distance, and reports annually in formula that the Emblem is venerated in good order, is Custody knowledge — secret.)

The great school is where Magister Emeric Sault reads the mathematical arts, and it exists for Ombreval chiefly as the far end of Doctor Ferrant's correspondence. Ostrelle itself remains what the canon calls it: nearly a legend. It is never visited on the page. It writes.

## What outsiders say

Four versions of Ombreval circulate on the roads, and the corpus keeps all four in play.

**The pilgrim's version: a licensed wonder.** On the pilgrim roads Ombreval is a fixed station: come for the dawn-showing, stand where the vergers put you, hold a drop of tallow in the cold beam, pay Verger Pike for the gallery if your purse runs to it, and buy the stamped badge on the Gradine steps before you go. The badge shows what it is an offence to describe, and the pilgrims find this less strange than the residents do; every shrine has its rules. What travels home with them is the tester's unease: other wonders on the road weep, or heal, or bleed on the feast — they do something. Ombreval's does nothing at all, lets you look straight at it, and is there again tomorrow. Pilgrims rank it oddly high for that reason and cannot say why.

**The merchant's version: a toll town with a strange church.** To the carrying trade Ombreval is honest weights, chained and proved — the Gaudry weights' reputation runs the whole river — fair tolls, and one word you do not say near grey coats. The fine under the Edict is the only toll on the Serle, the bargemen joke, that can be dodged for free, by holding your tongue. Merchants like Ombreval. The window is a crowd, and a crowd is custom.

**The Ostrelle scholar's version: a provincial curiosity.** In the schools, such of them as have heard of it, "the Ombreval glass" is filed among reported marvels: thick old fire-fluxed glass and two hundred years of schooled eyes teaching each other what to see. It is the standard gloss, delivered without having been there, and it is the gloss Magister Sault himself carried until he wrote to Ferrant — his letters aside, the schools have not revised the entry. His colleagues say the provinces have kept him; report has it he no longer argues (secondhand at every step; no scene in Ostrelle, ever).

**The folk-rumor, by distance.** Closest is truest. At Harne: a light in a window and a fine (near enough). At Brede they say the green light fills the whole church like water in a jar (false). At Salorge the pan-workers say Ombreval has two noons and pays double tithe for the second (false, and they laugh telling it). On the far stretches of the Lantern Road, carriers swear a green sun stands over Ombreval, plain from the road at night (false; nothing shows outside, ever). And in Ostrelle itself, mostly nothing: the capital has grander wonders nearer to hand, and Ombreval is a toll-stamp on a cloth bale, a bell in a chronicle, and one magister's folly. The rumor grows with the miles and dies of thinness at the end of the road.

## One sun, everywhere else

Here the corpus states a boundary plainly, because it holds everywhere and always: **nowhere else has anything like the Emblem, and no tradition beyond the walls remembers a second sun in any sky.** No shrine on the wool road or the salt road shows a doubled light. No grandmother in the Combs, no salt-raker at Salorge, no chronicle in the libraries of Ostrelle keeps so much as a tale of two suns, in this sky or any other, ever. The boat-folk, who see more sky than anyone, give the flattest testimony: one sun in the water, the Combs to the sea, every day of every year (true, and stated without wonder, the way they state depth).

The Unwalled hold that the green sun once stood in the open sky above everyone's grandmothers; the roads are where that doctrine goes to be quietly contradicted by every grandmother available. Told so, a neighbor answers that four hundred years is long and grandmothers die — the doctrine absorbs the miles as it absorbs everything else. State the fact plainly; let it lie; resolve nothing.

## The travelers' block (injectable)

Speakable lines for arriving and departing NPCs. Markers as in the gazetteer: unmarked lines are common knowledge; (never confirm) and (secret) as noted.

**A boatman of the Serle:** "Three days' pole up to Brede, six down to the pans. One sun in the water the whole way, and I'll swear it on the toll-stone." (true)

**A wool-carrier from Brede:** "They say in Brede the green light fills the church like water in a jar. I've stood in the nave. It doesn't. It's worse — it's small, and it looks back at you being looked at." (first half false; second half opinion)

**A salt-hand up from Salorge:** "Two noons, we say on the pans, and double tithe for the second one. It's a joke. Mostly it's a joke." (false, told laughing)

**A Harne bargeman:** "A light in their window and a fine if you name it. My grandfather said the fine came first and the light was invented to collect it. We hear your big bell at Harne, mind." (rumor false; the bell true)

**A carrier of the Lantern Road:** "Six weeks to Ostrelle, two toll-gates, and past the second gate nobody's heard of your green anything. On the far stretch they'll tell you it stands over the city at night, plain from the road. Twenty years I've made the run. There's nothing over the city but weather." (true; the far rumor false)

**A pilgrim, outbound:** "I've walked to shrines that weep and shrines that heal. This is the only one that just lets you look. Buy the badge — you can't say what's on it, but you can point." (true)

**A pilgrim, homebound, quieter:** "I held the tallow in the cold beam myself. It didn't soften. I don't know what I verified. I verified it, though." (true)

**Anyone asked about the Luminary:** "In Ostrelle, and in Ostrelle he stays. None of them has come here in living memory. The Fabric's blessing took three years on a road a salt-barge does in six weeks — draw your own conclusions about how fast grace travels." (true)

---

*A last note for the shelf: the city that named itself for shadow is, to the world, a stamp on a salt-ledger, a bell heard once at four leagues, and a rumor that thins with every toll-gate. The world has one sun and is sure of it. So has Ombreval, everywhere but one room — and the roads, which go everywhere else, have never once had to disagree.*
