# The Four Answers: A Comparative Doctrine of the Second Sun

> Use: inject into sidecar NPC context so actors hold, argue, and mangle the four positions on the Second Sun consistently; doubles as the writers' reference for what each faction believes, practises, and forbids.
> Canon: 00_canon.md

Ombreval has one phenomenon and four answers. Everyone holds one — or two at once, and hopes no one asks. Truth values follow 00_canon.md §10: [T] true, [F] false-but-sincerely-believed, [A] ambiguous-by-design, never resolved. Knowledge marked *secret* is uncommon; an NPC should be shaped by secrets, not volunteer them. The four: the Candor's Emblem, the Unwalled's walled sun, the measurers' doubled ray, the street's Green Sun of the dead. A fifth — the masons' — is officially no answer at all, and appears in the quarrels at the end.

---

## I. The Candor's Answer: the Emblem

### The doctrine

The Church of the Sole Light teaches that light was the first creature and the only one that never disobeyed. On this rests the legal order of Ombreval: every contract, testimony, and verdict is sworn *by the light that does not lie*. The stake fits in one sentence: if light can be double, testimony can be double, and every oath in the city is unsworn.

Therefore the Candor teaches: there are not two suns. There is one Light, and by grace one **Emblem** of it — a granted image, "a painting of the Light upon the eye of the Church." The formula is ***Una lux, gemina species***: one light, twofold appearance. Whether this is true, the game never says [A, #16]. The doctrine is elegant precisely because it explains nothing and forbids the question.

The parish teaching is the **Wick Catechism**, drilled into children:

> *Q. What stands in the Rose?*
> *A. The Emblem, which is a granted image of the Light upon the eye of the Church.*
> *Q. Is the Emblem a sun?*
> *A. There is one sun, as there is one light. The Emblem is neither kindled nor quenched; it burns nothing and warms nothing; it is not to be counted.*

The founding text is the **Gradine Homily of F.244**, preached by **Praelucent Odo Fenn**, lamp-named Vigil, the day the Edict was cried at the Bellstand — quoted to this day, usually at people:

> "You have not seen a second sun, for there is none to see. You have seen how much image one Light can afford. Do not count manners among things. ***Species transit, lux manet*** — the appearance passes; the light remains."

The collect of the Feast of the Sole Light:

> "O Light one and undivided, who hast set thine Emblem in the eye of thy church as a seal is set in wax: grant that we may read the seal and not worship it, and keep our oaths by the light that does not lie."

At a clear Concurrence, as the doubled shadows fuse, the Praelucent gives the versicle from the crossing: *V. One light. R. And undivided.*

### How it answers the three questions

**(a) Why only through the Rose?** Because the Great Rose is the consecrated eye of the Church, and a granted image is granted to the Church gathered — not to the open sky, not to a hole, not to the street. Ask a canon why grace chose that window and he will answer that grace is not interrogated about its address.

**(b) Why the wrong color?** Because the Emblem is of no hour. The Homily: "It wears no hour of any sky, that you may not mistake it for weather." That it keeps its color through every pane [T, #2] is preached as proof: created light obeys the glass; the granted image obeys only its Original. That it may be looked at without pain [T, #3] is glossed the same way — the true sun chastens the eye; the Emblem is *permitted* to it.

**(c) Why the doubled shadows?** The grave-shadow is *the shadow of the appearance*: one light, twofold appearance, therefore twofold shadow — nothing doubled in the world, only in the seeming. The fusion at a clear Concurrence is the doctrine's liturgical vindication: watch, and the appearances return into one. A clouded Concurrence, when nothing fuses [T, #7], is not discussed from the pulpit. It is discussed everywhere else.

### The two layers

Publicly: the **Edict of F.244** — calling the Emblem a "sun," "second sun," or "green sun" is citable; fines, and for the stubborn, recantation on the Gradine, theatre deliberately survivable. Beneath it, the gentler **Custody Doctrine**: *to see is no sin; to affirm is* — free absolution for the sight, "So do we all, child; affirm nothing." Privately — *secret* — the Church at its top is not hiding a truth it knows: it has tested the thing for a century and a half [T, #11], knows it is no fraud and no art of man, and is holding a fact it cannot metabolize. Doctrine is the vessel it is held in.

### What it demands

**Practice:** swear by the light that does not lie; keep the Feast of the Sole Light; buy, if you like, the licensed pilgrim badge of the Emblem — never saying aloud what the badge shows. **Taboo:** the three forbidden names; unlicensed images of the disc; counting suns, even in jest, even before children. **Duty:** quiet correction — mend your neighbour's vocabulary before a grey coat hears it. For clergy, one duty more, secret and absolute: whoever is raised Praelucent is taken alone to the crossing at the strong hour, made to look, and made to sign the Attestation [T, #12].

## II. The Unwalled's Answer: the Walled Sun

### The doctrine, in full

The Unwalled hold that the green sun is **real, and the elder of the two** — it once stood in the open sky above everyone's grandmothers, and the Church's four centuries of building were masonry against heaven [F, #14]. The Lanthorn is a wall raised course by course to shut it out; the towers are, in Colm Attergate's words from the Bellstand in F.301, coffin lids. The Great Rose is the wall's one flaw: old glass "remembers the sky it was cooled under," so the walled sun shows through the rose as through a keyhole [F, #15]. Their proof-text is Idonea Sparr, who wept at her finished window, never fired glass again [T, #18], and was deposed and sealed by the Chapter within the year — before any public sighting. There they are simply right: the cover-up is older than the first record of the thing covered.

### What they get right

That the Church is lying about *something* [T]. That the phenomenon is objective, repeatable, identically described for two hundred years [T]. That the Custody sits on suppressed test records [T, #11]. That their arithmetic is sound: in F.431 their Concurrence date beat the official almanac by a day [T, #22]. And one thing they will never know: the Custody's secret sky-charts match their Green Almanac observation for observation [T, #23] — two enemy institutions keeping one identical record.

### What they get wrong — on purpose, per canon

Everything about mechanism and history. No chronicle, ruin, or elder testimony attests a green sun in the open sky, ever; their treasured "recovered testimonies" are forgeries, some bought, one unknowingly commissioned [F, #21 — *Colm's Last Letter* is Corin Copp's work, and no member knows it]. The glass-memory doctrine is falsified by a fact almost no one living has seen: new plain glass leaded into the rose shows the disc perfectly [T, #10, *secret* — proven at the Glaziers' Trial of F.288, again on the F.419–421 scaffold]. And the wall-theory cannot survive its own keyhole: a thing walled out should show through a *hole*, and a missing pane shows one ordinary sky [T, #9]. Confront a neighbour with the broken-pane fact and the answer comes back smooth as river stone: "the wall heals its own holes." The doctrine absorbs every disproof. They half-know it, and keep it anyway, because the alternative is that the whispering goes nowhere.

### How it answers the three questions

**(a) Why only through the Rose?** The keyhole. Old glass remembers the sky it cooled under; the rose's glass, fired on Cinder Row, remembers hardest of all. Every other window is younger, or lesser, or lies.

**(b) Why the wrong color?** It is not wrong; it is *elder*. The green-white is the walled sun's own light, unstained by the panes because it does not pass through them as ordinary light does — the glass is not a window to it but a memory of it. And that you can bear to look at it means it was made to be looked at.

**(c) Why the doubled shadows?** The grave-shadow is your *true* shadow — the one everything would cast if the sky were unwalled, restored only where the keyhole light falls. This is why the bede-roll is whispered facing the rose: the dead stand in the elder light entire, and the living borrow a corner of it.

### What it demands

**Practice:** attend your light of three; know your lead and no one else; pay your dues — paupers' funerals at Saint Maren's, page-buying at the Tally Bridge. Learn the fresh pass-name from the charnel door and the name-knell; answer the Wicket at the grate in a whisper, for every voice in this city carries twenty meters. Whisper one name of your own dead through the keyhole; give your week's sightings to the sky-drawing; rehearse, low, *Lucem non murabis*. **Taboo:** nothing written, ever; never speak the rule of the pass, only the name; never guess at the Tracer aloud. **Duty:** by faction. The **Keyhole** faction owes the rose protection at any cost — watch every scaffold, every "repair." The **Breach** faction says the *building* is the wall and talks of opening it: all talk, no capability, and talk that could hang all nineteen. The bleak joke neither side of the window can see: the Keyhole faction and the Custody of the Eye are guarding the same glass.

## III. The Measurers' Answer: the Doubled Ray

### The theory and the instruments

**Doctor Aubin Ferrant**, physician-astronomer off the Tallage, teaches — taught — the *doubled ray*: glass fluxed with the ash of the F.171 fire splits the sun's image "as a stick bends in water." It is the only answer in Ombreval that proposes a mechanism, which is why it is the only answer that can be killed.

His tools are patient and cheap: sighting-rods and plumb-lines; a string-grid across the nave floor, mimicking the rose's leading, to chart beam-fall against the bell-hours; wax tablets of pane-positions; smoked glass, never needed [T, #3] — a fact he has underlined three times; and the dark box, a pinhole chamber that draws the nave on a white leaf. It renders the disc faithfully, which comforts him more than it should: whatever it is, it consents to be drawn.

### Where the reasoning breaks

He has killed his own theory five ways and recorded each blow.

1. Replica panes, fired to the old recipes with true Cinder Row ash, show nothing — anywhere.
2. A cracked cull of genuine rose-glass, bought quietly on Cinder Row, shows nothing held to open sky. The glass is not the thing. (He does *not* know the converse — new glass in the rose shows it perfectly — the one fact that would redirect his enquiry, and the one the Grey Press holds tightest.)
3. The killing stroke, his own last measurement: two observers, west doors and crossing, calling pane-positions at the same bell-stroke. The true sun stood two panes apart for them; the disc stood in the same pane for both eyes at once [T, #6]. No bent ray puts one image in one place for every observer — not an image's behaviour but a *thing in the glass* — yet its rays sweep the floor with the hour like any sun's.
4. The countergait [T, #5]: a duplicate must travel with its original; this one moves against it and crosses it once a day.
5. Color and storm: no refraction keeps its own color through a red pane [T, #2]; and when lightning whitens every pane, an image in transmitted light must brighten. The disc alone does not [T, #8] — a steady cold coin in a sheet of white.

The theory is dead. Ferrant keeps the corpse on his desk and keeps measuring, and in his notebook's margin stands the sentence the whole matter deserves: *"It moves against the sun; it stands in one pane for every eye; I have no theory. An impossible light, and the city at peace with it."* The phrase is already loose in the streets, garbled.

### How it answers the three questions

**(a)** Rose-only visibility: *unexplained* — the ash-flux account is falsified by his own replicas. **(b)** The color: *unexplained* — he once argued dispersion, but a prism spreads a fringe and obeys the pane, and this does neither. **(c)** The doubled shadows are the one thing his ruin still handles: two sources of light cast two shadows, whatever the second source is. His problem was never the shadows; it is the second source.

### What it demands

**Practice:** measure; measure again with a different instrument; write the failure in the same ink as the success. **Taboo:** affirm nothing unmeasured; admit no explanation from grace, ghosts, or glaziers' malice, for an explanation that cannot fail is not an explanation. **Duty:** the record, published or not — and it may not be, quite: his instruments are funded, at one remove, by Custody money [*secret*]; Ferrant suspects, does not ask, and knows what his not-asking is worth.

## IV. The Street's Answer: the Green Sun of the Dead

### The belief

In the streets the disc is the **Green Sun**, Edict or no Edict, and it shines for the dead. Speak your dead's name in its beam and they hear you [A, #29 — never confirm, never mock]. The street does not argue mechanism, which is why it cannot be refuted; it answers a different question: *what is it for?*

**(a) Why only in the church?** Because the church is the house of the dead's names — where they are prayed for, knelled, and chalked on the charnel door; the dead's sun keeps to the one roof where the living still say their names. You would not look for the ferryman anywhere but the ford.

**(b) Why that color?** Because it is drowned light — dawn through river ice, which is what daylight looks like to the drowned looking up. Saint Maren floated upstream against the Serle; Colm Attergate went under at the sluice; the Green Sun wears their water. It does not hurt the eyes because the dead would not hurt you for looking.

**(c) Why two shadows?** The rhyme is the doctrine, and every child has it:

> *One sun for the living, one sun for the flown,*
> *one for the tallow and one for the stone;*
> *Colm went a-walling and Colm went below —*
> *count both your shadows wherever you go.*

The grave-shadow is the one you will cast when you are flown — or your dead walking beside you while the green light lasts; both readings are held at once, usually by the same grandmother.

### What it demands

**Practice:** speak your dead's name in the green beam, on Bellday if you can; hold a newborn in the doubled light so the child has a spare shadow to spend; carry a fever into the beam to cool; let a bier rest where the green light falls, so the dead take their second shadow with them; children play twinning until a verger's patience ends; river-parish widows wear a green thread in the hem for a mourning-year. **Taboo:** a grave-shadow on a bride is ill luck — weddings are timed around it, whatever the wick-priest says; and no bargain is struck in the doubled light, for a bargain under two shadows binds twice — which is why pilgrims haggle for badges in the porch and pay in silence within. **Duty:** small coins to the vergers, and small kindness to the Spared — pitied as folk whose dead do not look back, envied for the same reason, and quietly not asked to hold the lantern at funerals [A — whether the Spared see nothing or say nothing is never resolved].

## V. Where the Answers Collide

Ordinary days rub the four answers against each other; seed arguments for actors, each speaker consistent with their own answer.

**The oath.** Every verdict at the Tallage is sworn "by the light that does not lie." A smirk surfaces in every generation: *"Which one?"* Two words, a citable offence, and the whole stake of doctrine in one breath. The clerk's standing reply, worn smooth: "The one you'll be fined by."

**Coswaldstide.** Every autumn, someone brawls over F.288. The masons' answer — "it is in the glass; glass is a lie you can see through" [F, #13] — is not one of the four, and Warden Stott's lodge will die holding it, because the verdict that disproved it was never shown to them. The glaziers' guild-issue rebuttal: "Our glass shows true; take it up with the sky."

**The wedding hour.** A bride's mother wants the nave floor clear of the grave-shadow at the vows. The wick-priest refuses to consult the hours of "a thing that is not." Verger Pike, for a small coin, knows the hour anyway. All three go home satisfied, which is Ombreval in miniature.

**The fever child.** A mother carries her burning child into the green beam. A grey clerk notes her name; her confessor absolves the sight before she has finished kneeling. She has affirmed nothing, only *used* it — and there is no edict against use, a gap the whole street walks through daily.

**The doors, and the sky.** Since the Shut Door of F.436, the taverns hold one argument on repeat: will the Chapter bar the nave again at this year's Concurrence? Folk say the doors belong open on the dead's own day; the Candor says order is also a form of reverence; the post-Shut-Door heretic intake say quietly that a church that locks its doors at the Passing has told you what the building is *for* — and that sentence, above a whisper, is how the Custody finds new files. Over it all hangs the weather: a clouded Concurrence fails everyone. Folk call the year unblessed, the Candor preaches that the Light owes no weather, the Unwalled remember the Great Rains — when "the clouds did not know it" and the only sunlight in the city stood in the nave [T] — and Ferrant sets up his string-grid regardless, because a failure, properly recorded, is also a measurement.

Four answers, one disc, no verdict. Keep it that way: nothing tips the balance — the Second Sun never performs, never chooses, never answers.
