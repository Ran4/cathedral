# The Mouth of the City: Folk Culture of the Second Sun

> Use: inject whole or by section into sidecar NPC context as Ombreval's shared oral culture — verses, sayings, and songs are speakable or singable verbatim by TTS; the italic annotations are for designers and the sidecar, never spoken aloud by NPCs.
> Canon: 00_canon.md

The Edict of F.244 governs what may be written and cried; it says nothing of what may be skipped to, hummed, or muttered over a cradle. So the city put its memory in its mouth. None of what follows is doctrine; most of it is older than anyone who says it, and says more than any speaker knows.

---

## Children's rhymes and skipping songs

The root rhyme, known to every child in Ombreval (see 00_canon.md; no child knows who Colm was):

> *One sun for the living, one sun for the flown,*
> *one for the tallow and one for the stone;*
> *Colm went a-walling and Colm went below —*
> *count both your shadows wherever you go.*

The verses below follow it at the rope; Ede of the Needle keeps them all, and corrects adults who get them wrong.

**The ladder verse.**

> *Colm had a ladder and Colm had a line,*
> *Colm counted courses, one to nine;*
> *nine for the tower and ten for the sky —*
> *down came the water, and don't ask why.*

*What it keeps: F.301 (see 04_chronicle_of_the_city.md) — a labourer of the west towers, ladder and plumb-line; banishment and drowning in one falling line; the town's learned silence in the last four words. Children think "don't ask why" is about the rope turning.*

**The sluice verse.**

> *Under the sluice the eels are fed,*
> *say your names to the green instead;*
> *if the gate is shut, come round by the wall,*
> *if the wall is shut, don't come at all.*

*What it keeps: where and how Colm died — back by water at night against a shut ban. "Say your names to the green" is the folk practice of naming the dead in the beam (never confirm, never mock); the last line is the banishment turned skipping advice.*

**Weep, Weep, the Glass-wife's Daughter.**

> *Weep, weep, the glass-wife's daughter,*
> *what do you see in the noonday water?*
> *I see a window, I see a sun,*
> *I made it two that was made but one.*
> *Latch the shutter, latch it twice —*
> *dawn comes green through the river ice.*

*What it keeps: F.203 nearly verbatim — Idonea Sparr weeping at her finished rose, saying she had made two of something there was one of; folk memory has slid the maker down a generation. The last line preserves the canonical colour — dawn seen through river ice. The rhyme says "sun," which is citable; the Custody has never once cited a child.*

**Three Went In Walking.**

> *Three went in walking, walking, walking,*
> *three went in walking and nobody talking;*
> *two came out riding and one came out cold,*
> *and the glass was good as gold, gold, gold.*

*What it keeps: the Glaziers' Trial of F.288 — three journeymen put to the Question, two carried home, one to his grave — and, in the last line, the verdict never published: the glass was found innocent. This skipping rhyme is the only public text in Ombreval that states the trial's outcome, and no one who sings it knows it states anything. How it learned a sealed verdict, no one can say.*

**Rain on the Reeds.**

> *Rain on the reeds and rain on the wall,*
> *three weeks' raining and no sun at all;*
> *one little candle in the big stone room —*
> *who kept it burning? Don't say whom.*

*What it keeps: the Great Rains of F.362 — the pale disc the city's only sunlight for three weeks, garbled to a candle — and the forty arrests in four words: "don't say whom."*

**The twinning chant**, said while playing twinning — contorting in the doubled light until the two shadows' hands touch:

> *Shadow, shadow, take my hand,*
> *one to kneel and one to stand;*
> *if we touch before the bell,*
> *nothing-never-no-one tell.*

*The last line is nonsense that scans; some grandmothers say it used to be different, and cannot say what. Leave that alone.*

## Counting-out rhymes

For choosing who is out.

> *Pin, Ib, Cobb and Sef*
> *went to the church and one was left:*
> *one for the wick and one for the stone,*
> *one for the water and one came home,*
> *and the one who came home came home alone — OUT.*

> *Gravemouth, Smallvoice, Orphan — three,*
> *which of the bells will ring for thee?*
> *Not the great and not the small;*
> *the nameless bell won't ring at all,*
> *and out you fall.*

*The second keeps F.341: the Orphan's baptismal record burned, and the folk conviction that an unnamed bell cannot ring you anywhere good.*

## Superstitions and omens

*All are sincerely held somewhere. Those marked (sooth) describe render-true facts from 00_canon.md §2; the rest the game never confirms nor mocks. The contradictions are intentional: quarters disagree and keep their own versions.*

1. A grave-shadow across a bride is ill luck until the next Concurrence fuses it away — and a clouded Concurrence leaves it on her another year; careful weddings keep to the aisle bays, out of the window's sight.
2. A newborn held in the doubled light gets a spare shadow to spend; what the spending costs, ask no midwife.
3. Fever carried into the green beam cools; the vergers keep a bench in it.
4. Speak your dead's name in the beam and they hear you.
5. Speak a living name in the beam and he will hear of it by Bellday; the nave is a bad place to gossip.
6. Wax will not soften in the cold light; a Wickmarket tallow drop proves it to any pilgrim with a penny. (sooth)
7. You may look straight at it and take no hurt; grandmothers say the hurt is being saved up. (the looking is sooth; the saving is not)
8. Never count your shadows aloud past two.
9. A mirror leaving the nave goes face-down; carried face-up it shows an empty sky till sundown.
10. To meet one of the Spared is lucky in the Wickmarket and unlucky at Maren's Green, and both squares have proofs.
11. No one weds under the Orphan's voice; a bell without a baptism cannot bless one.
12. A candle guttering in still air is a grave-shadow passing; snuff it and say nothing.
13. Step on the X at the Kiss and lose a year; stand on it with both feet and gain a wish; the vergers have given up saying which.
14. A clouded Concurrence is an unblessed year. Saying so is forbidden. It is said.
15. A green dawn through river ice means a drowning by thaw, say the boatmen; a fat eel year, say the fishwives; both watch for it.
16. Where a pane is out, the window holds its breath; do not stand beneath a held breath. (that a gap shows one plain sky is sooth; the breath is not)
17. A child born at the hour of the Passing will never lie, or will never be believed; the midwives split by parish.
18. If your cold shadow falls darker than your warm one, you owe the dead; pay it into Saint Maren's box before the next knell.
19. The ravens nesting in the north tower scaffold keep the tower unfinished; if ever they leave, something worse is finished instead.
20. A beam from the rose once came down in Skinners' Court, three streets from any glass; nothing has been seen there since or before, and the rents are lower even so.

## The warding gesture

**Belwyn's ward**: shut the left eye and lay two fingers flat on the shut lid. The wick-priests teach that it is only giving the dark its due, after Saint Belwyn, who preached that darkness too is of the Light; the folk use it as a latch on the soul. It is made when the thing in the window is named aloud, when crossing a doubled shadow not your own, when the Orphan rings, and at the midpoint of the Needle, which is held to "cast wrong" — folk hurry through the pinch eyes-down, a habit certain parties find convenient.

Variants, each certain it is the original: boatmen shut the right eye, because the left minds the water; masons shut both for one breath, since stone doesn't look; chandlers draw a thumb across an unlit wick.

The old strong form is **making the Kiss**: cross the two forefingers and throw their crossed shadow on the ground — the Passing, made small and answered. Grandmothers keep it for the worst occasions; children have made it a dare. Whether making the Kiss inside the nave itself is the surest protection in Ombreval or the plainest provocation divides households.

The Custody can hardly object to a shut eye, its own emblem being one; the grey clerks are said to use Belwyn's ward themselves, which either proves it works or proves nothing.

## Sayings, oaths, and curses

- **"Past the Needle"** — beyond saving. Said of a debt, a marriage, a man.
- **"One shadow to your road."** — the travel blessing, given at doors and moorings. Answer: **"And one light over it."** It is not said indoors, and never in the nave.
- **"Chalked on the lintel"** — as good as dead: "he just hasn't heard the bell."
- **"Sold at the Tallage"** — betrayed, at a fair price.
- **"There's a moth in every tavern."** — mind your tongue. Most who say it could not tell you what a moth is, and are right anyway.
- **"Peace with the impossible, and dinner at noon."** — no use puzzling; get on with the day. Also heard as "it's impossible light" (not worth arguing) and "the city's at peace with it" (drop the subject). *All three are drift from Doctor Ferrant's margin line (00_canon.md §3d); no speaker knows the phrase has an author.*
- The lawful oath is **"by the light that does not lie."** Four pots in at Maren's Green, a boatman will swear *"by the light that does not lie — and the other one, that does not answer,"* which costs a fine if a moth is thirsty.

Curses, mild to mortal: **"May the Orphan ring you in."** **"May your shadow go home without you."** **"May you stand at the Kiss and cast nothing."** The last is not said to children. And there is **"fuller's luck"** — the worst luck, luck of the damp — used in low voices, and never within earshot of a grey coat.

## The boatmen's song

**The Water Knows One**, sung at the Hungry Ox, the boatmen's tavern by Maren's Green — the ox verse explains its name. The song never names what it is about, which is the craft of it; it is sung louder when grey coats pass.

> *Old Maren came floating the wrong way home,*
> *upstream to her mooring through the weir and the foam;*
> *the river cried pardon, the reeds took her in,*
> *and she keeps the drowned tidy till we're drowned again.*
>
> *Haul on the dry rope, pole on the stone,*
> *the water knows one, boys, the water knows one;*
> *let the tall house keep what the tall house has won —*
> *the Serle never carried but the one, boys, the one.*
>
> *The year of the great frost they roasted an ox,*
> *the butchers ate brisket in their holiday smocks;*
> *the boats stood in ice and the boatmen stood by —*
> *we'll pay for that dinner when the river runs dry.*
>
> *A fish like a provost came up at the Green,*
> *the finest fat sturgeon the Tallage has seen;*
> *the chapter got dinner, the priest got the bone,*
> *and who kept the roe, boys? Ask the river alone.*
>
> *They've a light in the tall house too good for the street,*
> *it never gets wet and it never gives heat;*
> *count lights in the water when your day's work is done —*
> *whatever they've two of, the water knows one.*
>
> *So drink to the east gate that empties the town,*
> *it lets the freight seaward and it let a man down;*
> *sing low by the sluice, boys, and pole for the sun —*
> *the water knows one, boys, the water knows one.*

*Every verse is chronicle-true: Saint Maren upstream; the ox of F.229; the sturgeon of F.271, whose roe the boatmen kept and lie about in the song itself; the Cut's one reflected sun; and Colm at the sluice, unnamed, because the boatmen own that death. Legally the song is unimpeachable, and everyone in the room knows exactly what it means.*

## The ghost in Gaunt Passage

Told always as happening to a friend of the teller's cousin.

Since the year the Custody took the five, there is something at the dark midpoint of Gaunt Passage after last bell. You hear cloth being wrung — the slow twist, and then the dripping — on nights when the stones are dry as a sermon; the air goes river-damp around you, and there is a smell of wet wool where no wool is. It is the fuller, they say: taken there, dead of the damp before ever he was tried, come back to wring his cloth at the door they took him from. Do not stop to listen. A salt-porter who lingered went deaf for three days, his ears full of water that no one could see poured out. Walk the passage quick, and if you must pass at night, say "good night to the trade" — never guess at his name, for nobody living is sure of it, and the dead hate being guessed at wrong. The old women add one thing more, and lower: he is not wringing his cloth at all. He is listening for one voice, and when he hears it, the dripping will stop.

*Designer note: the fuller of F.428 died in the Custody's keeping, not in the passage; folk memory has moved the death to the arrest-site. The last line brushes against a true thing (00_canon.md, ledger 26) without knowing it — never let any NPC or system resolve it. The Unwalled neither started the tale nor correct it: it empties the passage at night better than any watchman, and Grigor Ashe has been heard to add details.*

## Trade customs

**The lamplighters.** The lamplighters of the five squares serve under the Wickmarket chandlers. The first flame of the round is taken from a coal carried in a horn box, never begged from a house-fire, for borrowed flame tells tales; a lamplighter never lets his own shadow fall on the lamp he is lighting, but stands side-on; at each corner he whistles the wick-call, three notes, so folk know the shape on the ladder is lawful. In every square one lantern is left dark each night, a different one by rote — **Belwyn's lamp**, the dark getting its due — which the Custody approves for the saint's sake, the folk for their own, and the thieves study. Lamps in sight of the west front are lit last: reverence or thrift, depending which lamplighter you ask. On the eve of the Concurrence every lamp in the five squares is lit, Belwyn's included — the one night the dark goes unpaid. The lamplighters hate it, and are paid double.

**The glaziers.** On Cinder Row an apprentice swears his articles on the lead, never on the glass — glass shows, lead holds. Burn scars are **Ambrelle's kisses**, after the saint who carried the melt bare-handed, and a first scar is toasted. No one whistles within sound of a furnace; a whistle calls a crack. On the eve of Saint Ambrelle's feast the furnaces are raked cold, and every fire on the Row is relit next morning from the procession's lantern, so the whole street burns one flame for a year. To say "glass remembers" on Cinder Row buys silence and no service; if the Row answers at all, it answers: **"Glass forgets; men remember."** And since the year of the trial no glazier will testify about light — sand, ash, and lead they will swear to all day, and go mute at "what does it show"; the Row's whole answer to that question is the mistress's formula, *our glass shows true; take it up with the sky*, and the courts have learned not to ask.

## What mothers tell children about the rose window

That a baby is carried into the doubled light to be given its spare shadow, and that the spare can be spent once — a fall survived, a fever turned. Children point to Ede of the Needle: she spent hers at the Shut Door, which is why the arm knit crooked but knit. Some mothers walk the child through the beam every month "to keep the spare fed"; others say once at birth and never again, or the window will think the child is being offered; the two kinds of mother do not lend each other flour.

That you do not wave at the eye, because it is not looking at you — and you do not ask whom it is looking at.

That the window minds the dead's side of the family, and you behave under it as at a grandmother's table; if you must cry there, cry quietly, for the dead sleep badly enough.

And that a child who loses his shadow in the dark can find it next morning in the nave, standing in the green, waiting to be stepped back into. Mothers say it to stop night wandering, and it works; and more than one grown citizen, on a bad morning, has stood a moment in the cold beam and come out saying nothing, with both shadows on.

---

*Nothing in this mouth-lore is authorised, and nothing in it will die. The Edict polices nouns; the rhymes gave the nouns away and kept the facts. If the Grey Press burned tomorrow, the children would still be skipping the trial, the rains, and the man at the water — one turn of the rope per century, and never a word wrong.*
