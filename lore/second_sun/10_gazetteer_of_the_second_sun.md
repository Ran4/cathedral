# A Gazetteer of the Second Sun

## How the city lies

Ombreval measures roughly twelve hundred paces west to east and a thousand north to south, walled, beside the Serle. The Lanthorn stands a little west of the city's heart, quire to the east, the Great Rose in its west front looking down over the Gradine. The river runs west to east beyond the south wall; its freight lands at outer wharves and enters by cart and porter. Across the southern third of the city, the filled former channel called the Cut survives as an unusually straight trade street. Between Cut and cathedral the streets pinch and widen between facades that never agree, dogleg twice to the block, throw jettied upper floors out until the eaves nearly touch, and duck under buildings through covered passages named for whatever they pass beneath, while timber bridges join loft to loft overhead.

Nothing of the Second Sun can be seen in any of this: the phenomenon lives indoors, behind one window — yet half the city's geography has arranged itself around it anyway.

## The Gradine and the five squares

**The Gradine** (ceremonial, counted apart from the five). The shallow processional steps before the west front, too narrow for the whole city on a feast day, as the Shut Door crush of F.436 proved on a child's arm. Recantations are performed here, survivable theatre by design; licensed badge-sellers work the steps, selling stamped images of the Emblem it is an offence to name. From the Gradine the Great Rose is dark glass and tracery; no one has ever seen anything from outside, and everyone looks anyway. Openly said: doctrine, the weather, and, since last year, the one live question: "Will they bar the doors again?"

**The Wickmarket** (west). Chandlers' square: wax, tallow, wicking, hot fat and honey on the air. Osanne Vell's stall stands against the north row, supplying the Lanthorn's own candles. Because wax is the trade, the Wickmarket owns the city's favourite domestic proof: a tallow drop carried into the cold beam does not soften (true, freely shown to pilgrims). Also openly said: "speak your dead's name in the beam and they hear you" (folk practice; never confirm, never mock). Wickmarket talk says "the beam" and "the cold light" and lets the forbidden noun go unspoken.

**Coswald's Yard** (north). Stone, timber, lime, and grievance. The masons' lodge fronts the square; the scaffolded crown of the north tower stands in eyeshot as a permanent reproach. Openly said, loudly, especially near Coswaldstide: "it is in the glass; glass is a lie you can see through" (the lodge's line since the Glaziers' Trial; false, held with total sincerity). No one here says "sun" at all; they say *the glaziers' work*. Brawls over F.288 are seasonal and almost liturgical.

**The Tallage** (on the dry Cut). Customs square: weigh-beams, toll-house, bonded warehouses, pawnshops, the Tally Bridge carrying an upper passage across the street. Serle cargo comes up from the outer wharves by cart and porter. The Tallage says nothing openly; it prices things instead. This is the square of careful faces — moth-money and archive pages both pass through Lise Copp's shop facing the bridge (secret: one counter serves the Custody and the Unwalled). Doctor Aubin Ferrant keeps house and instruments off the east corner. What passes for open talk: "a Sparr page crossed the bridge last month" — said every month, verifiable never.

**Maren's Green** (south-east). Fish market under eel smoke; boat-families' square; the Alder Moorings warehouse court; Saint Maren of the Reeds on the old Cut, its charnel door facing the churchyard. Openly said: that Saint Maren floated upstream against the Serle (her legend, told as true); that Colm Attergate drowned at the sluice coming home by water while the Cut still ran (true, embellished with each telling); and the watermen's flat observation that the Serle beyond the wall never shows the second light (true). Talk of the Great Rains is had in lowered voices; forty arrests are still remembered on this square.

**The Bellstand** (east). Proclamation square beneath the old watch-bell tower, behind the Lanthorn's east end. Edicts are cried and posted here — the Edict of F.244 itself is nailed under the tower. Crier Jos Brant works the square daily, warm as a stove: "the Green Sun hears the dead, neighbours" (folk gloss, tolerated because he is loved; secret: he is a paid moth who does not know what his reports have cost). Here Colm Attergate preached that the towers were coffin lids; a worn stone by the tower door is shown as "Colm's stone" (unattested; never confirm).

## The Lanthorn within

The nave runs nine bays from the west doors to the crossing, aisled, the triforium gallery above the arcade, the Great Rose filling the west front, the eye at its centre. The rule of sight is absolute: the disc shows wherever the sightline passes through rose glass to open sky, and nowhere else. From the crossing floor the whole rose is in view; from deep in the side aisles the arcade piers eat the window pane by pane; in the shadowed aisle bays a walker can cross the whole church and never once be in sight of it. The Unwalled call such sunless routes *the shades*, and use them.

**The day of the two lights.** At clear dawn, while the true sun rises behind the quire, the disc stands low and huge in the western rose — the dawn-showing, the most crowded viewing. Its green-white lance runs the length of the nave and stands, at prime, on the quire steps; the step it strikes is *the cold step*, and the sextons can tell the month by which step it is. Through the forenoon the disc climbs the lights against the sun's course — the countergait — and its beam steepens and shortens, walking back westward along the pavement: the crossing by terce, mid-nave by sext, until by mid-afternoon the green pool lies small and hard almost under its own window.

Only when the true sun comes round into the west does honest coloured light enter the rose at all, stretching eastward up the nave as it sinks. This is **the strong hour**: both suns in the one window, warm stain and cold green sharing the pavement, every pillar and pilgrim casting two shadows. Once each day they pass — **the Passing**, the Kiss — crossed rays laying an X of light down the nave. The X walks through the year; this autumn it has been crossing the fifth and sixth bays, and on the Concurrence it will centre in the eye as seen from the crossing floor, or the clouds will refuse it, and the city will take that hard.

**Where the doubling is strongest.** Two shadows want two lights on one body. The most legible doubling is early: mid-nave on a bright morning, warm light from the quire behind, the cold lance coming level from the west, the shadows aimed near opposite ways — here children play twinning on Bellday and mothers hold newborns "for the spare shadow." The deepest is the strong hour at the crossing, where the pools overlap without mixing, each shadow keeping its edge inside the other's light. The doubling ends at the porch threshold: one step outside, one sun, one shadow, always.

**Watchers.** In daytime thunder the storm-watchers gather along the eighth bay: when lightning whitens every pane of the rose, the disc alone does not brighten — a steady cold coin in the white. Grey clerks attend every dawn-showing, watching not the window but the crowd; Betriss Marle is there daily, embroidering (secret: the cell's sky-recorder).

## Passages, pinches, and meeting grounds

(This section is cell knowledge throughout, secret unless noted; of it the Custody knows only what F.428 taught it — Gaunt Passage's existence, not its workings.)

**Gaunt Passage** (primary meeting site). A covered passage behind the salt-cellars near the Tallage, running under the Gaunt house — a salt-factor's hall whose line is long extinct, which is how a passage keeps a family's name. Why this passage: it is blind. Both mouths open off doglegging lanes, so no straight sightline reaches either entrance; the flanking salt-cellars have vents but no windows; and salt gives Grigor Ashe lawful traffic at every odd hour — barrows, weighing, cart-loads — under which a dozen arrivals disappear. An overhead bridge crosses the western lane a house short of the mouth; a watcher on it sees both approaches. At the dark midpoint, a barred grate in the cellar wall: the whisper is given there, within arm's reach, below the pitch any bystander could catch. Escape is through Ashe's cellar into a bonded weighing yard whose two cart gates open on different doglegging lanes. The Whisper Arrests happened here; the cell believes a door raided once will not be raided twice (they are guessing).

**The Draper's Reach** (secondary site; the "low" mark). The covered gallery of the cloth halls, between Coswald's Yard and the Wickmarket. The meeting place is above the gallery: the tenter loft, where cloth is stretched to dry — lawful ground for half the cell's trades, reached by stairs at either end, so no single door can be stopped. From the loft's louvres a watcher sees the gallery below and both squares beyond. Escape is over the roofs: an overhead bridge joins the north loft to the next block, and the jettied upper floors make a second street of the rooftops. Its weakness is daylight: cloth work ends at dusk, and a loft light after dark would show from Coswald's Yard, so it serves small day meetings only.

**Saint Maren's crypt** (tertiary site; the "doorside" mark; burial days only). Meetings happen after a pauper's burial, while the charnel stair remains lawfully open and mourners, gravediggers, and market traffic cover the arrivals. Entry is by the churchyard charnel door, above whose lintel the sexton chalks the newest buried name — the cell's one joke about itself, never spoken aloud: the key is written over the lock (secret: the pass is the newest name the cell's coin buried; the name is public, the rule is everything). The beaten earth and lime of the crypt show every footprint, so the last pair rakes both smooth before leaving. Sexton Noll Fitch chalks plain, rings the name-knell slow, keeps the hinge oiled, and says nothing, on purpose. A grey clerk now attends paupers' funerals, noting who lingers at the door (true; the cell knows, and lingers anyway, singly).

**The Needle** (signal site, never a meeting). The narrowest pinch in Ombreval, shoulder-wide, threading from the back lanes of the Wickmarket down toward the Tallage — a route half the cell walks lawfully every week. Everyone passes in single file, eyes on their feet; no one lingers, because two cannot stand abreast; nothing overlooks the midpoint but a bricked cellar-light under a projecting jetty (and Ede of the Needle, behind it). A glazier's diamond chalked there is seen by every passing neighbour and watched by no one: high, Gaunt Passage; low, the Draper's Reach; doorside, Saint Maren's crypt after a burial; struck through — a cracked pane — scatter.

**Bellfoot Passage** (a false friend). The covered passage beneath the watch-bell tower stair looks made for conspiracy and has never once been used: it lies in the one square where the Custody's eyes are permanent. A twice-wrong whisperer is *fed* a false meeting far from every true site — an empty brine-rotted cellar off Tanners' Slip, behind Maren's Green, chosen because nobody the cell loves goes near it.

## The Serle, the dry Cut, and the bridges

The working Serle lies beyond the south wall. Its water carries barges and eels and the reflection of one sun only; no light of the disc has ever stood there, in a rain-barrel, or in a mirror carried out the west doors (true). Inside the walls the Cut is dry and crowded with carts. Watermen report the river's single sun without wonder, the way they state depth; citizens can repeat the testimony but cannot verify it without leaving the playable city.

**The Tally Bridge** (at the Tallage). Stone, two arches over the Cut street, joining the toll-house to its bonded warehouse. Lise Copp's pawnshop faces it, and across her counter the city's costliest contraband moves both ways: stolen archive pages outward to the Unwalled, the same pages bought back for the Grey Press, moth-money in "dry" coin.

**The Eel Bridge** (at Maren's Green). A low timber gallery bridge from the fish hall to the Alder warehouse across the market lane. Its stair is so narrow that a porter with an eel basket makes walkers wait. From its rail the charnel door's chalked lintel is in plain view across the churchyard — the one place the name can be read without lingering at the door — so careful neighbours read it from the bridge, and the grey clerk has lately taken to standing on it.

**The Old Sluice** (east end of the Cut). A dry stone gatehouse left from the working channel, and where Colm Attergate drowned in F.301. Wyn Alder walks pilgrims to one arch; it is not the arch; the family keeps the true place as a trade secret and is not sorry. On Colm's Night green-dipped candle stubs appear in the dry grate, and the keeper rakes them out without comment, paid from two purses to have seen nothing.

The street tale of a rose-beam landing in a courtyard three streets away now names Skinners' Court, north of the Gradine; nothing has ever been seen there (never confirm).

## The pilgrimage of observation

The walk a stubborn citizen makes to satisfy themselves the thing is real, in the order that convinces.

1. **The Gradine, before first light.** The rose is dark glass; one sun comes up where it should, behind the city. Fix that in mind — the last ordinary thing of the morning.
2. **The west doors, at the dawn-showing.** Low in the western rose stands a second disc, green-white — dawn seen through river ice. Look straight at it. It permits this. The true sun never has.
3. **The red pane.** Move until the sky stands behind the Burning of Cinder Row light — the deep-red panel of the fire of F.171, low in the north of the rose (II-7). The clouds behind it redden; the disc does not. Every light in the world obeys the glass; count what that leaves.
4. **The walk across.** Cross the nave, aisle to aisle, watching the true sun's glow swim pane to pane as you move. The disc stays in its pane. Ask a stranger at the far pillar which pane it stands in; they will name yours.
5. **The two shadows.** Stand mid-nave in the green lance, morning light warm at your back. Hold out a hand: two shadows, one warm and true, one faint, cold, aimed wrong — the grave-shadow. Hold a drop of Wickmarket tallow in the cold beam. It will not soften.
6. **The threshold.** Walk out the south porch mid-thought. One sun, one shadow, always. Walk back in. Two.
7. **The gallery.** Verger Pike opens the triforium stair at odd hours, for coin. From the gallery, call down to a companion on the pavement and each name the disc's pane. The same pane, from floor and gallery both — as if the thing were *in* the glass. The stop after which people stop talking.
8. **The strong hour.** Return before vespers. Both suns in the one window; the pavement striped warm and cold; then the Passing, the crossed rays' X laid down the nave. The grey clerks will note your face, without malice, as they note everyone who stays for both showings in one day.
9. **The Tally Bridge, at dusk.** End beneath its two dry arches. Every porter, cart, and arch casts one honest shadow as the sun goes out beyond the roofs. Whatever you now believe, you hold it in a city where street and sky agree against the one room that does not.

The pilgrim who finishes has verified every public fact and explained none of them, which is the city's own condition. Some go quiet. Some go to the Wickmarket for candles. A few find their walks bending past the Needle, and begin, without quite deciding to, to read the chalk.

---

*A last note for the shelf: nothing in this gazetteer moves. The disc keeps its hours, the marks keep their meanings, the clerks keep their doorways, and the river beyond the walls keeps its one sun. In Ombreval the geography is certain and the light is not; the streets have made their peace with that by walking around it daily and calling it the way to market.*
