# Letters on the Doubled Shadow

*Docket, in a hired hand, pinned to the wrapper: "Six letters. Two hands, one thread — the doctor's among them his own out-copies, kept against what he sent. Taken in pawn at the Tally Bridge; priced by seal; unredeemed." The letters are tied with glazier's cord; the fourth — the doctor's own copy — is burned along its foot. The dates run from the winter after the Shut Door to eleven days before this Coswaldstide.*

## The first letter — Magister Emeric Sault to Doctor Aubin Ferrant

*Ostrelle, the fiftieth day after the Feast of the Sole Light, F.436*

To Doctor Aubin Ferrant, physician, at his house off the Tallage in Ombreval: Emeric Sault, reader in the mathematical arts, sends greeting.

The carriers brought your city's disgrace before your letter could — doors barred at the feast, a child's arm broken on the Gradine. They brought also talk of the other thing, which your Edict prices at a fine and I will not name on paper that crosses two toll-gates. Call it your tenant in the west window. I hear it keeps its lease.

I write because you measure and the rest of your city gawps. My judgement, and hold me to it: your Tenant is a reflection in thick old glass, as a candle doubles itself in a night pane; or else a dazzle passed through a crowd, each eye teaching the next, as yawning passes down a pew. Both are known things. Neither survives an instrument.

So: instruments. Shadow tables at fixed hours by the bells, true against cold, length and bearing, a month together. Coloured slips before the eye — a reflection is a debtor and pays in its master's coin; put red before it and it must drink red. A pane out of the rose itself, tried against open sky. Do this and write, and we shall have dissolved your marvel by spring, as warm water dissolves a frost-flower.

Number your pages. Burn mine.

## The second letter — Doctor Ferrant to Magister Sault

*Ombreval, in the hard frost, F.437*

Emeric — your frost-flower has survived the warm water.

The verger admits me before the dawn-showing for a small consideration; I share the nave with pilgrims, who talk to the beam, and children, who contort to make their two shadows touch hands, and observe better. A plumb line at the crossing, marked at five feet, both shadows taken at fixed hours for a month; the mean of clear days:

| Hour, by the bells | The true shadow | The cold shadow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First light | three-and-fifty feet, toward the west door | four-and-twenty feet, toward the altar |
| Third bell | nine feet, toward the north aisle | fourteen feet, toward the altar |
| Noon | four feet, athwart the nave | nine feet, toward the altar |
| The strong hour | nineteen feet, toward the altar | six feet, toward the altar |

Mark it. The true shadow keeps the sun's law — long, short, long. The cold one only shortens, dawn to dusk: contrary hours. At the strong hour the two lie nearly in one line toward the altar and do not die into each other; each keeps its edge and colour inside the other's light. One staff laid across the doubled pool casts two shadows in contrary directions. I have drawn it. You will not like the drawing.

Your debtor pays in no one's coin. Through a red slip of Cinder Row glass the clouds redden, the tracery reddens, the true sun reddens: every light in the world obeys the glass. The Tenant stays green-white, the colour of dawn through river ice, through every slip alike. It suffers the eye: I looked straight into it for a count of a hundred and wept no tears; the true sun would have blinded me in three. The permission is worse than a burn — an unphilosophical sentence; I let it stand. And two pellets of one Wickmarket candle: in the true beam, thumb-soft by the third bell; in the green, hard at evening. Light, and no warmth.

The pane I had by fortune: a lozenge cracked in this frost, at Mistress Sparr's bench for renewal. She let me try it on her roof — glaziers like a test their glass can win. Against open sky it showed sky and nothing else, at any hour. And Emeric — the gap it left in the rose showed one ordinary sun, like any window in the world. A hole shows less than the glass around it. I do not like writing that sentence. When the new lozenge went in, the Fabric's men kept canvas before the light a week; when it came down I could not tell which piece was new. They are careful of their repairs, this Chapter.

I hold yet to rays — my doctrine, that the old fire-fluxed glass cleaves the sun's image as a stick bends in water. But replica panes fired with the very ash of Cinder Row show nothing, in the rose's own light or anywhere. A stick does not bend in one pane only of one window only. Send me a better theory or a better experiment.

## The third letter — Magister Sault to Doctor Ferrant

*Ostrelle, at the first thaw, F.437*

Not reflection, then: a reflection drinks the pane's colour and dies with the pane, and yours does neither. I abandon my candle-in-the-night-pane without shame; a theory is scaffolding, not fabric.

There remains the crowd. Your city has looked at that window two hundred years and told its children what is in it; the eye is a liar with honest men to swear for it. So, a witness no man has schooled: the dark chamber. A tent of double sailcloth at the crossing, a pinhole the width of a straw, a whited board within. The chamber does not dream, Aubin; the eye does. One sun on the board, and your Tenant lives in the crowd, and we publish nothing and are kind about it. Two, and it lives in the light — and the Light help us all. Strike that clause before you keep this letter. You will not.

Two trials more. Mirrors: a barber's mirror on a pike will hand the sight to a man with no line to the rose — then carry it out of the west door, watching, and tell me at what point precisely the world corrects itself. And chiefly, the Tenant's station. Two observers far apart, each noting at an agreed stroke the exact light in which each sun stands. A near lamp splays the shadows of neighbouring posts and shifts its window with the walker; a far light lays all shadows parallel and stands in one quarter of the sky for every eye. Near or far: everything that shines is one or the other. That is the whole of optics in one sentence; I taught it to better men than either of us. Your gap troubles me. A keyhole shows what a window shows. Yours shows less.

When the roads dry I shall come and see for myself, for the Feast of Saint Ambrelle if my dean releases me. Prepare the sailcloth, and a bed, and none of your physic.

## The fourth letter — Doctor Ferrant to Magister Sault

*Ombreval, the third day after the Feast of Saint Ambrelle, F.437*

*(burned along its foot)*

Emeric — the chamber does not dream. I have watched it not dreaming for six mornings. The tent stood at the crossing, the pinhole toward the rose; on the whited board, inverted and sharp, two suns. The true one crawls its lawful way; the pale one walks against it, painted as steady and as indifferent as the other, in a chamber that holds no crowd, no child, no priest, and no opinion. I put my own eye out of the question, and the question remained.

The mirror answers as you asked. A man kneeling blind of the rose in the north aisle was handed the sight at one remove, and dropped the pike. Carried out the west door, tilted with all care: the glass held the rose, the rose held sky, and the sky held one sun only. I cannot name the point of correction; there is no point; there is a threshold. I likewise threw the green beam along the pavement, a bright cold patch walking before me to the sill. It would not cross. Not faded, Emeric — absent, as a figure struck from a sum.

Now the station. My boy Ib stood at the font with a sighting-rod, I at the crossing; at the stroke of Gravemouth each fixed the light in which each sun stood. The true sun stood for him two lights north of the eye, for me in the border of the southern petal — as any near lattice parts a far light between two walkers; no fault there. The Tenant stood in one and the same lozenge for us both. Repeated from the triforium, the west door, the pavement before the quire: one lozenge, the same, for every eye in the building at one instant, as though the thing were in the glass itself — yet its beams sweep the floor with the hour like any traveller of the sky. So to your sentence: near splays, far lays parallel. At the strong hour I hung two lines ten feet apart and took the bearings of their cold shadows with the care I would give a dying man's pulse, and I find them —

*(the foot of the leaf is burned away)*

## The fifth letter — Magister Sault to Doctor Ferrant

*Ostrelle, written in the dust of my return, late summer F.437*

Your fourth letter reached me entire, and I have read its last leaf nine times. You write that the cold shadows of lines ten feet apart lie parallel to a hair — the shadows of a light at the world's edge. Far, then, by shadows. Near, by panes — in the glass, by panes. Both, by measure; and I taught thirty years that everything that shines is one or the other. Optics is not wounded, Aubin. It is unconsulted.

I have seen it now. You stood me at the crossing before dawn, and I had prepared to be disappointed, as a man prepares for a saint's knuckle in a jar. Then the sky doubled behind the rose, and the disc stood low and huge with dawn at my back, and I looked straight into it, and it permitted me. I understand your shame. And on the third day came a noon storm, and when lightning fired the rose every pane went white for a blink — every pane but where it stood: a steady cold coin in a sheet of white. I said "pardon." Aloud. To a window. Set that against my thirty years, and laugh; I have.

I withdraw the crowd; the chamber has no crowd in it. I withdraw reflection. I have nothing to put in their place, and I am less troubled by our failures than by our successes. Every trial we design, it sits for, patient as a corpse for the painter. In thirty years of inquiry something has always failed — a lens flawed, a bell late, a boy asleep. Nothing fails here. We have set down everything and explained nothing.

One more matter, set down because I could not say it in your house. A grey clerk sat at my elbow on my last morning, copying my figures as I made them, courteous as a bridegroom. Your quadrant is new, your lenses the best I have handled outside a princely gift, and a physician's fees did not buy them. Who grinds your lenses, Aubin? I did not ask under your roof. I ask on paper, once, and will not ask again.

## The sixth letter — Doctor Ferrant to Magister Sault

*Ombreval, eleven days before Coswaldstide, F.437*

You ask who grinds my lenses. I have not asked. Let that stand for my answer, and judge me at your leisure; you will not be harsher than I am.

I still teach the doubled ray on market days, because a man must say something. It is dead; I keep the corpse on my desk and go on measuring, which is either philosophy or mourning. The instruments are the same.

Now attend. Forty days after Coswaldstide falls the Concurrence, when the crossing of the two comes dead centre in the eye and, for a handful of minutes, every doubled shadow in the nave fuses into one. One light, or two lights lying? The fused minute is the only assay we have not made. I propose three trials, and I need what I have never needed before: help.

First, the two-eyed chamber. Mistress Sparr renews a lozenge near the eye this autumn, if the Fabric consents; for one morning there is a gap in the rose. I would set the dark chamber so that two pinholes feed one whited board — one eye taking the sky through the gap, one through the glass beside it: two skies side by side on one cloth at the fused minute, drawn by the light itself with no man's hand in it.

Second, the mirror at the crossing, to throw the fused beam down the nave and out at the open west door. If it passes the sill, then for that minute there is one light in the world's eye as well as the Church's, and the fusing is a marriage. If it dies at the threshold as the green beam died, the fusing is a meeting only — and we shall have measured the difference between those two words, which no divine has done.

Third, the small thing: two pellets of one candle in the fused pool, weighed before and after. Warmth is the honestest witness I know. It cannot be taught what to say.

What it needs: Sparr's consent and her silence — I have written, and wait. The keys before dawn, which can be had, as you saw, for silver. A clear sky, for under cloud the Concurrence simply does not happen; the assay is hostage to weather, like the feast. And the doors: if the Chapter bars them again as last year, there is no mirror trial — and after last year I do not know whether the city's temper would suffer a man at the crossing with mirrors and sailcloth at all. So it may come to nothing, and the letter you hold may be the whole of it.

If you do not hear from me by the feast's end, the letters are yours to burn or keep. You were always the better judge of what should survive.

Your neighbour in the dark chamber,
A. Ferrant
