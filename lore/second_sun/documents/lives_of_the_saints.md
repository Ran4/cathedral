# The Lesser Legendary: Lives and Relics of the Eight Saints

> Use: diegetic document — a worn parish legendary of the Candor — the abridged "lesser" lives wick-priests read aloud at each saint's feast — with a closing relics catalog; injectable for wick-priests, vergers, sextons, pilgrims, and Dame Aldith; the lives are quotable verbatim by TTS (bracketed and italic matter is scribal apparatus, never spoken).

*The Lesser Legendary of the parish of Saint Maren of the Reeds: the short lives of the eight saints of Ombreval, read at each saint's feast, after the office and before the blessing. The great legendary is at Ostrelle; a parish keeps the lesser, and its rubric is one sentence: read what is written, and add nothing. Set fair by Ewart Skell, wick-priest, in the year 402 of the Fabric, from an elder copy much worn; the elder hands are marked where they stand. The lives follow the year, which turns at Vhairestide.*

---

## Saint Vhairé of the Ford

*Read at Vhairestide, when the year turns; the life is read at the old ford-stone before the first boats are blessed at the wharves beyond the south wall.*

Before the first stone there was a ford, and at the ford a ferrywoman, and at her prow a lamp. Vhairé kept the crossing when the Serle was kind and when it was not.

In the year of the great flood the river rose over the ford and no boat would go, and on the far bank souls lay dying unshriven. Nine times in one night Vhairé crossed. The spray came down around the flame and not upon it; on the ninth return the lamp was dry, and her hair ran with river. Dry I crossed, she said, and no more; her word rings yet in the bell Vhairé Farcall.

Armed men came afterward by night, to fall on the ford-market sleeping. She would not pole. They held her under her own keel; and the lamp burned on the open water until dawn, and by its light the ford-market stood to arms. So she is a martyr, and patroness of travelers, ferry-folk, and every honest crossing; the first stone of the great church was laid on her ground. Her petal in the Great Rose is the second: the water in torment, the lamp's reflection unbroken.

The Church suffers travelers who touch the ford-stone going out, and the oil left there for her lamp. It does not suffer lit lamps set loose on the night water, that the drowned may follow them home: the wick-priests put such lamps out, and say why.

## Saint Perrin Halfbell

*Read at Perrinstide, when the bells are washed and greased, and alms are given to the deaf.*

Perrin was a founder's boy, deafened young at the breaking of a cracked bell, which spoke once into his ear and never after. He stayed at the craft, and learned to tune by the flat of his palm and by his jawbone laid against the bronze, and the bells he passed rang truer than hearing men's.

At a feast ringing in his age he felt through the tower floor a wrongness no ear had caught, and stopped the ring. The bell was lowered and found flawed through the shoulder; it would have burst above the procession. His miracle is one of nothing happening, which the ringers say is the best kind.

He died in the tower he had saved, at a full ring, his palm on the frame-oak, listening. He is patron of ringers, of founders, and of the deaf. His petal in the Great Rose is the fourth, the only figure that breaks its border, the bell's rim riding over the lead.

The Church suffers the deaf who lay their palms to the tower door while the bells ring, hearing with Perrin's hand. It does not suffer the scraping of filings from a christened bell to be dropped into a deaf ear; the sextons watch for knives, and find them.

## Saint Ambrelle the Glass-Carrier

*Read at the Feast of Saint Ambrelle, when glass is carried from Cinder Row to the Gradine.*

Ambrelle was a glazier's apprentice at the ford-market before the first stone. Fire took the workshop in the night with the year's one melt standing in the crucible. The master counted heads in the yard; Ambrelle went in.

They threw her the wetted gauntlet-cloth, to bear the gather out. She let it fall upon the sill: the cloth would smoke, she said after; her hands were surer. She took the molten gather in her bare hands, came out barefoot over the embers, and laid the glass down upon the fallen cloth, unspoiled. Her hands were scarred to the wrist, and whole. Her petal stands at the crown of the Great Rose, her halo no ring but a furnace-mouth.

She died in great age, teaching. At her washing for burial the scars were found gone smooth, and the old women said the fire had called home its marks.

She is patroness of glaziers and of all who go into fire for another's good, and the great church bears her name. The Church suffers the toasting of a glazier's first burn-scar, called Ambrelle's kisses, and the relighting of the Row's furnaces from the one procession lantern. It does not suffer the laying of sore eyes against her cloth, to see what the window sees; the treasury turns such askers away, and does not ask who taught them the phrase.

## Saint Maren of the Reeds

*Read at Marenstide, at the eel fair, when the drowned are remembered.*

Maren was a poor man's daughter of the reed-shore, and gleaned reeds for paupers' thatch.

A late-summer flood took her at her gathering. Three days she was lost; the poor dragged the reed-beds and the boatmen the pools, and found nothing. On the third morning she came home. She came upstream, against the Serle, through the weir and the foam, her eyes open, and about her hair a crown of reeds that no hand had plaited. Every reed bends downstream; she went against. She came ashore at the mud-stair, and on that landing her church was raised, in the ninety-seventh year of the Fabric.

Her death was her martyrdom and her coming home was her miracle, and the Church has never had to choose between them. She is patroness of the drowned, of the poor, and of lost things. Her petal in the Great Rose is the sixth, green in a window thrifty with green.

The Church suffers the casting of a reed on the water to seek a lost thing, and the boatmen's salute to her panel on Bellday. It does not suffer the giving of the dead to the river for judgment, that Maren might carry the innocent home and keep the guilty: all dead are buried.

## Saint Ilvane the Twice-Shadowed

*Formerly read at Ilvanestide. The day is now nameless and the life is not appointed; it stands here because the book is older than the ruling.*

Ilvane was a hermit, and kept vigil for such dead as had no one else to watch them. She lived against the wall of the city and died against it, alone, in the elder days. Mourners came with candles, as is right. Certain witnesses swore that the body on the bier cast two shadows. It was the candles. *[Here a line is struck through, and under the strike may be read only: "...the second stood toward the candles, and no candle..."]* *[The elder copy differs here, and is not followed.]* It was the candles; the Chapter has so ruled; the ruling is enough, and is repeated.

Her patronage was of those who watch by the dead. It is not now assigned. Of her light in the great window this copy says nothing.

The Church suffers alms left at the anchorhold squint on the nameless day. It does not suffer the vigil of two candles, one at the head of a corpse and one at the foot; the wick-priest snuffs the second, whichever it is.

## Saint Coswald the Wall-Wright

*Read at Coswaldstide, at the blessing of the scaffolds, every pole and every knot.*

Coswald was a mason when the city's wall was young. In the old siege the wall was breached by engines, and the breach could not be closed under the enemy's stones, for the mortar of one night is green, and a green wall falls.

Coswald went into the breach and bade them lay the courses to him. Three assaults he held the new work with his back and spread arms. At dawn the enemy drew off. Then the lodge would have brought him out, and could not: the mortar had set about him as about a stone the wall had wanted, and he was dead, and the wall was whole. They left him within it — at his own word, said the layers, though none heard him say it.

He is patron of masons and of all who build, and the scaffolds are blessed at his feast. His petal in the Great Rose is the third, where the breach-stones carry true masons' marks.

The Church suffers Coswald's wage, the coin a mason sets in the first course, and the burial of masons with their tools. It does not suffer shadow-walling: the measuring of a stranger's shadow with a cord, and the cord mortared into the footing, that the wall may take a shadow and not, in its own time, a man. The wall is not the stronger for it.

## Saint Belwyn the Unlit

*Read at Belwyn's Dark, at the one office of the year sung unlit.*

Belwyn was a Taper of this great church. In the Fabric's hundred and forty-sixth year the sun was eaten at midday, and every soul covered their eyes or turned away. Belwyn watched it whole, to see, he said, what the Light would do; and he was blind after.

He preached until his death that darkness too is of the Light: the Light, he said, had shut its one eye over the world, and the world had stood. Thirty years blind he walked the works — scaffold, triforium, the night stairs — without lamp and without falling; and he carried lamps to others, lit, that no one else should learn the dark before their hour.

He died at his place before the Kindling, the office before light, waiting on a dawn he had no need of. He is patron of the blind and of the Custody of the Eye, whose emblem is an eye shut. His petal in the Great Rose is the fifth, at the root, his staff pointing not heavenward but down, to the sill.

The Church suffers Belwyn's ward, and the one lamp left dark each night in every square. It does not suffer the staring: boys dare one another to look at the true sun on his feast, to borrow his eyes, and some have borrowed more than they meant.

## Saint Gaudry the Ledgerer

*Read at Gaudry's Audit, called Weighday, when the ledgers are closed and sworn.*

Gaudry was a clerk of the tolls under a farmer of the Serle customs, and was bid keep two books, a true and a false. He kept both fair, for a clerk's hand is not where his honesty lives; and when the false book was full he carried it at High Wick to the tax-farmer's own step and burned it there before the square.

The court was the farmer's. Gaudry swore by the light that does not lie, and would not unswear, and was hanged. At the rope he asked the hangman whether the knot was well tied, and said: a just weight. The court bell of the Lanthorn, cast long after, carries his last words in its waist, PONDUS IUSTUM.

His miracle came out of the fire: when the ashes were raked, one leaf lay unburned — a leaf of the true book, its sums balancing to the spark. They balance still.

He is patron of clerks and of honest weights. His petal in the Great Rose is the seventh, where his scales tip against the coin. The Church suffers the closing of the year's ledgers at his Audit, and Gaudry's leaf, the blank first page of a new ledger, on which nothing is written and therefore nothing false. It does not suffer the burning of bonds and tally-sticks at his feast; the court fines it as theft dressed in devotion.

## Of the relics of the eight, and where they are kept

**Of Saint Ambrelle**, the gauntlet-cloth: the cloth thrown to her and let fall, scorched with the print of ten fingers and two palms. In the Lanthorn treasury; shown at her feast behind glass of the Row's own firing.

**Of Saint Vhairé**, the lamp: iron and river-brass, in an iron cage upon the ford-stone, in all weathers; filled and lit at Vhairestide by the eldest of the Alder boats. It has been stolen twice and come back twice, and the manner of its coming back is not enquired into.

**Of Saint Perrin**, the tuning bone: his jawbone, in a bronze case in the ringing chamber of the Lanthorn tower. The ringers lay a thumb to the case before a full ring. It leaves the tower only at Perrinstide, as far as the door and no farther.

**Of Saint Maren**, the reed-crown: at her church upon the old Cut, in a dry shrine above the mark left by the last great flood. The crypt has stood dry since the river was turned beyond the walls; the crown has never taken water, which the parish still calls the relic's own quiet argument.

**Of Saint Ilvane** the book keeps the elder entry struck. What was hers was within her chapel when the chapel was shut, and was not brought out. The chapel keeps it. There is the anchorhold, and the squint, and alms, and that is what there is.

**Of Saint Coswald**, the trowel: kept not at the Lanthorn but by the lodge at Coswald's Yard, shown at Coswaldstide to whom they please: the wall keeps the saint, the lodge keeps the trowel. His body is in the wall, and needs no shrine, being one.

**Of Saint Gaudry**, the unburned leaf: in the Lanthorn treasury, carried to the Tallage at his Audit, where the sealed brass Gaudry weights are proved against the Tallage stone — relics of use, handled and true.

**Of Saint Belwyn**, the staff: with the Custody in the chapter house, beneath the closed eye. It is shown to no one, which is thought fitting. The Custody, asked what it is like, answers that it is like a staff.

*Here end the lesser lives. Read what is written, and add nothing.*
