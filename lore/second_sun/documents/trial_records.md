# The Glaziers' Trial of F.288 — Surviving Leaves of the Sealed Record

> Use: diegetic document — contraband excerpts of the sealed trial record, loose in the city since the Ledger Theft of F.433; findable or purchasable in-game, read aloud at Unwalled meetings, and injectable as context for Custody, guild, mason, or archivist NPCs.
> Canon: 00_canon.md

*A fair copy in a chancery hand, the sewing broken, several leaves wanting; the press-mark of the Grey Press scraped from the first page, but not well.*

## The Bill

[fol. ii] Be it known that the warden and lodge of the masons of Ombreval, by Bartrem Stott their warden, do accuse the guild of the glaziers of Cinder Row, and namely the journeymen Tomelin Vasse, Gilles Loder, and Herry Fane, who in the two years past re-set panes of the Great Rose: that they, by an art that is no honest craft, did fix or renew in the said window a false light, to the deceiving of pilgrims, the fattening of the guild's purse, and the dishonour of the Sole Light, contrary to the creed *Lux una et indivisa*. And this the lodge will prove.

The bill was laid before the Chapter in the last of winter; the three named were put to the Question in the spring. Gilles Loder was not brought to the hearing, having died in the keeping of the court; the manner of it the record does not give.

## The Court

[fol. iii] Proceedings taken in the chapter house of the Great Church of Saint Ambrelle, the sixth day after Ambrelle's feast, in the two hundred eighty-eighth year of the Fabric, before the Praelucent Amaury Vair, lamp-named Vigil, assisted by the Provosts of the Fabric and of the Rite; Fulk Ottey, notary of the Chapter, writing.

First the Edict of two hundred and forty-four was read aloud, and every witness charged that the thing seen in the west window be named the Emblem and nothing else, on pain of fine; and every witness was sworn by the light that does not lie, the hand spread above the taper. *(The bill itself could not be read as written; the forbidden word stands in it four times. The court fined its own accusers before the first witness was sworn. This is recorded without comment.)*

## The First Witness

[fol. ix] **Bartrem Stott, warden of the masons' lodge, of Coswald's Yard, sworn.**

He says that from the north triforium, where the lodge kept scaffold two summers past, he saw the false light stand in the glass at dawn, high in the eye, green as pond-ice. That his mason's level, stood on the pavement, cast two shadows, and the false shadow leaned toward Cinder Row. That at the re-setting he heard the accused whispering over the panes, words he could not make out.

He says further: "Saint Coswald mortared the breach with his own body and got a side altar. The glaziers made a window and got the whole church. Now the window makes pilgrim coin, and the second s—" *(here the witness was fined a dozen sparks, and paid them angrily)* "—the Emblem, then. The Emblem makes them rich. Ask who profits by it. Ask the guild. I have asked it in the Yard these two years, and I will ask it here, fine or no fine."

## The Second Witness

[fol. xvii] **Wystan Crake, corn-measurer at the Tallage, aged three score and eight, sometime choirboy of the Lanthorn, sworn.**

He says he sang in the quire as a boy, before the Edict, when men still said the green sun out loud in the nave and no one blinked. *(He was warned as to the word. He said he was old, and the word was older.)*

He says that in his boyhood, a generation before the accused were born, the light stood in the rose exactly as it stands today. That at dawn it does not stand high, saving the warden's word, but low and great in the lights, and climbs through the day against the sun's own going, the way a bargeman poles against the stream. That late in the day the two share the window and once pass each other, which the boys called the Kiss, the crossed beams laying an X down the nave where the boys would knot their two shadows for a game.

Asked its colour, he says: the colour of dawn under river ice. *(The court asked him to say it again, and he said it the same.)*

Asked whether its beam carries warmth, he says the choir once stood a candle-stub in the green beam a whole afternoon, and it never wept a drop.

Asked whether it hurt the eye, he says no; you may look as long as you like, and the eye takes no hurt of it. He looked long, as a boy. He said men leave off looking all the same, and could not tell the court why.

Asked whether the light is changed in fifty years, he was silent a space, and then said: "No. And I have thought on that more than on my sins."

## The Third Witness

[fol. xxx] **Maud Alder, widow of a boatman, of the Cut, sworn.** Her statement being taken, she was questioned upon it.

Asked whether she holds that she came into the nave by the north door, at the thirty-day office for her husband, she says yes, by the north door; she has used no other door her life long.

Asked whether she holds that the office stood at noon, and that two beams lay crossed upon the bier like sword-blades, she says yes, at noon, she is sure of the hour.

Asked whether she holds that the green beam was warm upon her hands, she says yes, sirs, it warmed me, whatever any candle-stub may say; it was the only warm thing in that church.

Asked its colour, she says: white. White as a dead man's lip. Then she says: green, or white and green both. Then she says: "It was the colour of being cold. I know no better word."

Then the proctor for the lodge pressed her, saying: say plainly, widow — was it a second sun that you saw? And the Provost of the Rite warned her that to affirm a second sun is citable under the Edict, whatever the proctor promised.

The witness stood a long moment between them, and said: "I unsay it. All of it. There was no light. There were candles at the bier, a great many candles, and I was tired, and I saw candles."

*(The witness here wept. The court remitted her earlier fine and excused her. She was led out by the south door, and at the door she asked which door she had come in by. No answer is recorded.)*

[The leaf that bore her first statement is wanting. See the note at the end of this record.]

## The Fourth Witness

[fol. xxxv] **Aymon Harl, clerk of the Fabric, sworn upon the ledgers he keeps.**

He says the Great Rose was glazed by Master Idonea Sparr, finished in the two hundred and third year, and that none of the accused was born within fifty years of the setting. That the re-leading named in the bill stood on the south side of the rose, not the north; the ledger is before the court. That the north porch stood shut with scaffold from Bellday to Bellday that half-year, and whoever came into the nave came by the south or the west, whatever they remember.

Asked what the viewing of the Emblem brings the Fabric in a year, he gave the sums; the court struck them to the private schedule. The lodge's grievance as to money he conceded true, and said that truth of grievance is not proof of sorcery.

Asked whether the Emblem is changed in his time, or in the ledgers before his time, he says the records describe it in the same words for sixty years; that he has checked; and that he wishes he had not.

*(In one thing only were all the witnesses of one mind: that the light does not hurt the eye that looks at it. In colour, hour, and door, no two agreed, and each swore honestly. The court let it stand.)*

[fol. xxxix] *(Here Averil Sparr, master of the glaziers' guild, of Idonea's line, prayed that the deposition taken of Master Idonea Sparr in the two hundred and fourth year be unsealed and read, that the court might hear what the maker said of her window. The court answered: what was sealed in wisdom stays sealed in wisdom. Denied, and minuted.)*

## The Fifth Witness

[fol. xl] **Tomelin Vasse, journeyman glazier of Cinder Row, one of the accused, produced.** Being unable to spread his right hand above the taper, he was sworn with it laid open in his left.

Asked whether he stands by his confession made at the Question, that the journeymen sang over the glass and called the light into it, he says: "I said what the Question asked me to say. It asks kindly at first, and then it does not ask. I unsay the calling. The singing I never denied. Every shop on Cinder Row sings when the lead goes in, so the hands keep time and the strips sit true. It is a work-song. It is about a girl in Ostrelle. I could sing it for the court, if the court wishes." *(The court did not wish.)*

Asked its colour, he says he does not know the mix. He named over the greens a shop can make — copper-green, iron-green, bottle-green — and said it was none of them. Asked to say more, he looked at his hand a while and said nothing more.

Not asked, he said further: "We keep the glass. The light was in the window before my grandsire was born. It was not us. We are only the ones who wash it."

## The Probation by Pane

[fol. xlii] That the matter be proved, not merely sworn, the court removed to the nave at the strong hour. Item, the pane chosen by the accusers themselves was taken whole out of the rose and the gap left open to the sky. Item, a pane of new white glass, made that same day under the accusers' own eyes, of sand and ash of their own choosing, no hand of the guild touching it, was carried to the window and set in lead by a glass-wright brought from Ostrelle. And the court stood at the crossing, and looked.

[Here five leaves are wanting: fols. xliii to xlvii.]

## The Verdict

[fol. xlviii] IT IS FOUND, this court having seen what it has seen, that the light in the Great Rose is **no art of man**; and that the charge of sorcerous glass is not proven, nor could be proven, nor shall be brought again.

And the court says further, for the quiet of the city, holding to the Edict as it is written in its seventh article — *nothing binds the eye; to see is no sin, to affirm is* — that to have seen this light is no sin in any soul of this city, and to affirm it is another matter, and so it shall be held until better comes.

## The Sentence

[fol. xlix] The accused are released without stain. The guild of the glaziers shall pay a fine of peace, in the sum the Chapter shall appoint — not for guilt, of which the court finds none, but for quiet, of which there is need — and shall make good the disturbed pane at its own cost. The lodge of the masons is admonished, and bears its own costs. All present are sworn to silence upon the whole matter, by the light that does not lie. This record is sealed; the verdict shall not be cried at the Bellstand, nor posted, nor read.

Fulk Ottey wrote this.

## Archivist's Note

*In a later hand, unsigned, after the custom of this office.*

Two wants in this record, that the reader be not deceived by its neatness.

First: fols. xliii to xlvii, being the whole probation by pane, were lifted from the binding in the first year of the Custody, by minute of the first Provost-Custodian, and lodged in the inner press, where they remain. The minute is signed; nothing else in this business is. Every copy since — this one too, by whatever road it came to you — was made from the record thus shortened. The thief of four hundred thirty-three did not carry off those five leaves; they were never there to steal.

Second: the leaf that bore the widow Alder's first statement was cut out at the sewing. The stub shows a knife, not age. No minute records the hand or the year. Her unsaying is kept; her saying is gone. Consider what manner of keeping that is. This office does not know whose knife.

Why it matters. Without the probation, the verdict stands like a keystone with no arch under it. *No art of man* — but shown by what? The lodge holds to this day that the arch was never built and the guild was bought clear; the guild, that it was built and hidden; and there are those in the covered passages who read the words as the court confessing something older than craft. All three readings live because five leaves are elsewhere. A verdict without its ground is not a truth; it is a seed.

And mark the witnesses. Five were sworn honestly by the light that does not lie, and no two of them had stood before the same window. Green and white, noon and the strong hour, the north door and the south, a warm beam and a cold one. Do not conclude that any lied. Conclude that memory is glass of another sort: it lets the light through and colours it in passing, and no one can hold their own pane up against another's sky. This record disagrees with itself in colour, hour, and door — which is the ordinary condition of truth in Ombreval, and the keeping of it is this office's whole trade.

Whoever holds this copy holds everything the trial said, and nothing the trial saw.
