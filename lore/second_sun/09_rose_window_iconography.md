# The Lights of the Great Rose

> Use: diegetic document - the verger's book of the Lanthorn, a panel-by-panel guide to the Great Rose; inject into NPC context for vergers, canons, glaziers, and pilgrim-facing actors; authoritative geometry and numbering for building the rose-window diagram.
> Canon: 00_canon.md

*The verger's book of the west window, renewed by Verger Dunstan Pike, keeper of the nave keys, from the book of Verger Amiel Fenn, in the winter of F.436. Whoso holds the keys after me: read this before you answer a pilgrim, and answer less than you read.*

First, the one rule of the mouth. The window holds the Emblem; the Edict of F.244 gives it no other name, and neither does this book. One who calls it a sun is warned once, kindly; twice, and you send for a grey clerk. To see is no sin; to affirm is. Affirm nothing, not even in the margins of this book.

## I. The reckoning of the lights

Positions are read as on a clock face seen from within the nave, facing the window. You face west; the III side is therefore the north side of the window, the IX side the south; the crown is XII, the root VI. Numbering begins at the crown and runs sunwise.

From the centre outward:

- **O, the eye** - the central round light.
- **Ring I, the eight petals**, I-1 to I-8. I-1 at XII; each next petal one eighth of the wheel sunwise: I-2 at half past I, I-3 at III (north), I-4 at half past IV, I-5 at VI (root), I-6 at half past VII, I-7 at IX (south), I-8 at half past X.
- **Ring II, the sixteen histories**, II-1 to II-16. II-1 at the crown, directly without I-1; each next panel one sixteenth of the wheel sunwise. Odd panels stand on petal rays, even on the gaps. Thus II-5 at III, II-9 at VI, II-13 at IX.
- **Ring III, the two-and-thirty small lights**, III-1 to III-32. III-1 on the crown ray; each next light one thirty-second of the wheel sunwise. Odd small lights stand on Ring II rays, even between. Anchors: III-9 at III, III-17 at VI, III-25 at IX.
- **Ring IV, the eight buds** - trefoils in the rim stonework, IV-1 to IV-8, each on the same ray as the petal of its number.

Sixty-five lights in all.

## II. The eye (O)

No figure: the Sole Light itself, a white heart rayed in gold, ringed with the creed lettered black on white - LVX VNA ET INDIVISA. Note: one lead-strip crosses the final A of INDIVISA, so the word undivided is divided. Asked to mend it, Master Idonea Sparr answered: *the lead must go somewhere.* Unmended these two hundred and thirty-three years. Do not point it out.

## III. Ring I - the eight petals

**I-1, crown. Saint Ambrelle the Glass-Carrier**, bearing the molten gather bare-handed from the burning workshop. Ruby, gold, white. Note: her halo is no ring but a furnace-mouth.

**I-2, half past I. Saint Vhairé of the Ford**, mid-river, lamp held dry. Deep blue, white, grey-green. Note: the water is painted in torment, yet the lamp's reflection lies unbroken.

**I-3, at III, the north. Saint Coswald the Wall-Wright**, in the breach, the wall closing on his body. Ochre, iron-grey, one deep red. Note: the breach-stones carry true masons' marks; the lodge disputes two.

**I-4, half past IV. Saint Perrin Halfbell**, palm and jaw laid to a great bell. Bronze, amber, brown. Note: the only figure that breaks its border - the bell's rim rides over the edging lead.

**I-5, root. Saint Belwyn the Unlit**, white-eyed, face lifted to a black sky rayed with white. Black, violet, white. Patron of the Custody of the Eye. Note: his staff points not heavenward but down, to the sill.

**I-6, half past VII. Saint Maren of the Reeds**, borne on the water. Green, blue, silver - the window is thrifty with green everywhere but its waters. Note: every reed bends downstream; she goes against.

**I-7, at IX, the south. Saint Gaudry the Ledgerer**, the false ledgers burning at his feet. Red, parchment-yellow, black. Note: his scales tip against the coin.

**I-8, half past X. Saint Ilvane the Twice-Shadowed**, the hermit on her bier. Grey, white, candle-gold - and the other thing; see chapter V. Old vergers call this petal the Fault.

**Of II-6, the Casting:** pilgrims name the painted bell Gravemouth, and some vergers before Amiel let them. But this glass was fired by F.203, and the bourdon was not poured in Coswald's Yard until F.309, a hundred years and six after; the bell of the pour is an elder bell, and nameless, which is why the motto-band is blank. Amiel wrote that the letters wore; fired paint does not wear, and a bell before its baptism has no letters to lose. Correct a pilgrim once, kindly; past that, let the bell be what they will.

**Of II-9, the Setting of the Rose:** the lodge raising the last panel of this very window, shown small and whole within itself; at the eye of the little painted rose sits one fleck of green-white glass found nowhere else in the window - the vergers call it the Tear. The chronicle of F.203 is public: the day the last panel was set, Master Idonea looked back through her finished window from the crossing and wept, and never fired glass again. This book's tradition holds that II-9 was that panel. Of why she wept, this book says nothing.

**Of II-10, the Light Kept Faith:** the one panel younger by design, set F.365 in a light left as unfinished grisaille - the Great Rains, the city black under cloud, one beam standing in the nave. Every holy ray in this window is gold; this beam alone is silver-white. Reflect once, silently, on why the Chapter passed the cartoon so.

## V. The Fault: the petal of the Twice-Shadowed (I-8)

The cartoon of I-8 survives with the lodge. Drawn to the Chapter's order, it shows the bier, the mourners, the candles, and one shadow - doctrine made picture, for the Church teaches that the second shadow Ilvane's witnesses swore to was thrown by the mourners' candles.

The fired glass does not match it. The bier casts two shadows: one warm, candle-ochre, falling away from the candles, obedient; the other cold, of a thin green cast, falling toward the candles, which no shadow thrown by them could do. No one saw it painted; the daybook for those weeks records pot-metal, firings, nothing else.

Formally remarked F.221, on a canon's complaint. The lodge produced the cartoon; the ruling runs: *a doubling of the paint in the firing; no fault of doctrine; let it stand.* From the word "fault" the vergers named the petal - deniable, for a fault is a flaw, and flaws are nobody's heresy.

Note: no painted mourner looks at the bier - they look at the candles, their hands, away. Only a small dog in the corner regards the cold shadow directly. Pilgrims ask what the dog means. The dog means that Master Sparr painted a dog.

In the Re-leading of F.419 to F.421 this petal - glass, lead, and all, the only light so treated - went to the tracing-house two winters, for a cracked tracery rib, the Fabric said. The city watched it come back; there was no change. I carried keys on that scaffold; the Fabric's word is repair, and so is mine.

Under the Edict you will not say that the cold shadow and the Emblem are one matter. It has been a fault of the firing for two hundred and fifteen years, unchanged, in a window where every other fired fault has bloomed or faded with weather. Affirm nothing.

**Ring IV, the eight buds** - donors' trefoils, each a lamp and the giver's device: IV-1 the glaziers (lozenge and grozing-iron); IV-2 the boat-families (an oar); IV-3 the masons (square and plumb, the one bud with no lamp; the lodge has never said why); IV-4 the bellfounders (a bell, its motto too small to read); IV-5 the chandlers (a taper); IV-6 the fishers (an eel circled); IV-7 the scriveners (quill and honest weight); IV-8 a lamp before a shut cell door, a widow's gift, unnamed in the roll.

## VII. The glaziers' marks, and the repairs

Every pane of the first making bears, low in a corner, small as a fly, its maker's mark; a glass from the triforium reads them.

- **The diamond-and-dot** - the Sparr house mark, Master Idonea's own; the vergers call the dot "the eye." On most of the lights.
- **The bare diamond** - the same lozenge, no dot, on nine panes only, all fired in the last season of the work, F.203. Cinder Row's answer, unchanged for generations: haste. Nine panes' worth of one haste.
- **Wray's wave** - three nested curves; Tilde Wray, journeyman, maker of the water-lights.
- **The bird-foot** - Josse Bequin, journeyman, maker of the Months.
- **The cross-stroked diamond** - the present Sparr lodge's mark for the Re-leading of F.419 to F.421. There are many; the Fabric has counted.

The chalk diamond seen on city walls is no glazier's mark; fired marks do not walk. A verger reports nothing he cannot read.

The repairs:

- **F.203.** The window finished; the master's weeping, cited at II-9.
- **F.240.** Viewing hours; the badges licensed.
- **F.244.** The seventh-bay rail; the Edict the same year.
- **F.288.** The glazier's small-light, III-17, drawn out and returned. Amiel's book keeps the old entry whole: *pane drawn; pane returned; oath taken.* The lead about III-17 is double court-lead to this day; the vergers call it the Sworn Pane. The paint now in it is younger in manner than its neighbours by near a century, which you are not required to notice. Of the oath: above our office. Ask nothing.
- **F.365.** II-10 glazed and set.
- **F.419 to F.421, the Re-leading.** Two years of scaffold; a third of the panes through the lodge; the petal I-8 away two winters entire. The Fabric's published account is repair, and it is the account this book gives.
- **The standing order, older than any name in this book:** a broken or fallen light is boarded before the next dawn - boarded, not glazed, and the Custody told. It is the only order with no reason written beside it. Through the hole, the sky is as any street shows it. Men who look long at the hole are the quieter afterward. Board it.

## VIII. The verger's day: a guide to the viewing

**Where to stand.** The wheel is read whole from the crossing floor; the chronicle says the master wept there. From the seventh-bay rail the root lights foreshorten. From the triforium west walk the root lights hold the Gradine roofline, not sky - and mark the plainest lesson in the Lanthorn: where a light holds roof and not sky, the Emblem does not stand in it. No pane of the sixty-five refuses it, if sky stands behind and you stand within; none grants it to a man on the Gradine.

**The dawn duty.** Unbar at first light; the dawn-showing is the crowded one. The Emblem stands then at the root, low and very great - more mornings than not within Belwyn's petal, I-5, so the blind saint has it at his back; in deep winter lower, among III-16 to III-18, and oftenest in the Sworn Pane itself.

**The forenoon.** It climbs the south side against the true sun's course, taking the Trades of Ring III in their order - the fuller before the draper, the fisher before the boatman. By Maren, I-6, at midmorning. Late morning by Gaudry, I-7; toward the sixth hour, the full south, and the first of the Waters - vergers say of its noon that it comes in by the Ford.

**The teaching of the red pane.** One lawful sentence satisfies a decent pilgrim, at whatever light it then stands in: watch the clouds take the colour of the pane, and watch the Emblem keep its own. Spare your legs: it stands in one pane for every eye in the building at once, so there is no better side of it. It may be looked at straight; it is the true sun beside it that punishes.

**The afternoon and the strong hour.** Past the Ford it climbs on; it does not stoop again all day - by the Fault, I-8, in early afternoon, thence up along the Waters toward the high water, III-32; its evening it keeps at the crown, by Ambrelle, and there it goes out with the last light. In late afternoon the true sun comes round into the west and enters by the south lights, II-13 and I-7, higher in summer, and comes down through the lights as the Emblem goes up; then both stand in the window - the strong hour - and once they pass, the one rising and the one falling: the Passing. The crossed beams lay an X of light down the nave floor. Rope the crossing before it forms. What the crowd then does - the twinning games, the newborns, the fever-beds, the names spoken into the cold beam - the Chapter suffers, and so will you.

**The road of the Passing.** No two seasons give one road. In high summer the two pass wide, up by the Fault and the sluice-light III-30; through autumn the road bends inward week by week, until on the Concurrence, the fortieth day after Coswaldstide, the Passing falls dead centre in the eye as seen from the crossing floor, and every doubled shadow in the nave draws home into one. If the day is clouded there is no feast: the pale one burns through cloud alone, and alone there is nothing to fuse with. Of last year's doors I write nothing; the Chapter will rule again this year.

**Foul weather.** Under full overcast the Emblem stands alone in the glass, a pale coin; watch purses. In daytime storm, when lightning whitens every pane of the wheel at one stroke, it alone does not brighten; storm-watchers pay for the triforium.

**Last.** Now and again one at the rail sees nothing, and says so, and is frightened - by the seeing nothing, in this city, more than others are by the seeing. Be gentle. Fetch a wick-priest, cite no one, and write no name in any book, this one least of all.

*Here ends the reckoning of the lights. D.P., verger, F.436.*
