# Wells and Water in Ombreval

> Author-facing canon for the city's wells, cisterns, water labour, access
> customs, shortages, contamination fears, and fire supply in F.437. This file
> develops the practical water network already implied by Ford Well, the eight
> tax wards, and the Common Bench. It does not place an open river or canal
> inside the walls, and it establishes no new supernatural phenomenon.

## Canon in brief

- The Serle runs outside the south wall. The Cut has been dry since F.369. No
  public fountain, open channel, hidden river, or citywide pressure system
  supplies Ombreval.
- Citizens drink water. Ale, broth, and other prepared drinks matter, but the
  city does not survive by pretending that nobody drinks from a well.
- Most daily water comes from public ward wells and roof-fed cisterns, backed
  by court wells, private shafts, rain butts, work tanks, and carried barrels.
- **Ford Well** is the best-known public source: central, deep, reliable, and
  crowded by residents, Fabric workers, petitioners, sellers, and feast-day
  traffic. It is not holy water and does not tap the old river channel.
- Eight other named ward sources give the city a legible working network:
  **Slate Cistern, Tenter Cistern, Lodge Well, Three-Curb, Chain Well, Reed
  Cistern, Step Cistern,** and **Bitter Well**. The Shambles and Seven Lofts
  keep separate work and fire supplies.
- Water differs by depth, lining, roof, season, storage, and contamination.
  People describe it as cold, chalky, flat, smoky, bitter, soft, iron-tasting,
  or stale. These are material differences, not magical properties.
- A common source belongs to the city through the Common Bench. Its keeper
  tends gear, records bulk turns, controls the queue, and can close a suspect
  source. The keeper does not own the water.
- A household vessel takes precedence over a trade vessel in an ordinary
  queue. Bulk users draw by a turn-list. During the Ruin, all private turns and
  ordinary precedence end.
- Water is not charged for at a common curb. Carriers charge for the labour,
  waiting, distance, stairs, vessels, and cart. Trade assessments and repair
  dues pay for exceptional wear.
- Keepers and carriers hear much because they meet many households. They are
  useful witnesses and prolific gossips, not omniscient information brokers.
- Ombreval has practical knowledge of foul water without germ theory. Sudden
  changes of taste, smell, clarity, level, or sickness are taken seriously,
  though explanations mix observation, inherited rule, blame, and rumor.
- No named well heals, answers questions, reflects the Emblem, remembers the
  dead, or reveals hidden guilt. Water stories may claim such things; authorial
  fact does not.

## One city, several waters

The river made Ombreval, but in F.437 a person inside the walls cannot walk to
it with a household pail. The working river and its freight landings lie beyond
the River Gate and Reed Postern. The old Cut is a dry trade street. Its filled
bed, blocked stairs, bridge arches, cellar marks, and old working names
preserve the history of water without supplying any.

Daily supply is therefore vertical and carried. Water comes up a shaft, down
a roof, out through a draw hatch, across a shoulder, along a handbarrow route,
or from a cask at a door. Every stage has a limit. A well may be deep but slow
to draw. A cistern may hold much but receive nothing in a dry week. A full
cask may reach a square but not the fourth floor of a jettied house. A source
may be sound while the carrier's pail is foul. A keeper may have water and no
sound rope with which to raise it.

This makes “water” several overlapping economies:

- water for drinking, pottage, bread, brewing, and sickrooms;
- soft rainwater for linen, wool, and work that takes soap poorly in hard
  water;
- large rough draws for animals, stonework, cooling, street cleaning, and
  slaughter work;
- covered reserves that must remain available for fire;
- the labour of lifting, carrying, heating, storing, and disposing of it; and
- rights to a curb, roof, gutter, hatch, drain, trough, vessel, route, or place
  in a turn-list.

The richest household does not necessarily drink the city's best-regarded
water. It may drink whatever its servant or contracted carrier can obtain at
the promised hour. A poor household beside Ford Well may carry excellent
water for itself and still lack fuel to heat a tub. A cloth master may command
hundreds of bucketfuls at Tenter Cistern for work while buying a separate pair
of covered pails for the table. Access and use, not a simple scale from clean
to dirty, determine the city's water life.

## What lies under the streets

Ombreval stands on old river gravel, patches of clay, older masonry, filled
ground, and four centuries of foundations and drains. The exact underground
map is not known even to the Line-keeper. Two wells a short lane apart can
reach water of different depth and taste because one shaft ends in gravel and
the other passes a clay layer, or because one lining admits shallow seepage
that the other excludes.

The named deep wells reach groundwater below ordinary cellar level. The named
cisterns do not: they store rain deliberately led from selected roofs through
gutters and settling bays. Shallow court wells also exist, but they are the
first to turn low or suspect after a drain failure. Some abandoned houses hide
covered or half-filled shafts. The Common Bench treats those as collapse and
contamination hazards, not romantic entrances to an underground city.

The Great Rains and the diversion of the Serle changed local levels. Southern
shallow wells that once rose readily became less dependable after the Cut was
filled; others remained wet but acquired stronger mineral or cellar tastes.
High northern ground has always favoured deep shafts and stored rain. A rise
at the outer Serle can affect low groundwater after a delay, but it does not
put flowing river water beneath the Cut or refill Saint Maren's crypt.

Ford Well is especially misunderstood. Its site remembers the old ford-market,
but its shaft reaches a deep groundwater source north of the former river
crossing. It is not a surviving hole into the Serle's old bed. Water raised
there has passed through earth and stone like the water of other wells; it has
not waited intact since Saint Vhairé crossed.

Water level changes through the year. Keepers sound a well with a knotted line
and compare the wet mark with old notches inside the curb. They judge a cistern
by marked steps or sounding poles. These measures are local. There is no city
instrument that announces one universal water level, and keepers can disagree
honestly about whether a source is merely low or dangerously low.

## The city's water vocabulary

Ombreval speech distinguishes structures that an outsider may lazily call a
well:

- A **well** is a lined shaft into groundwater. Its permanent public bucket is
  raised by rope or chain. Personal vessels are filled at the curb and are
  never meant to be lowered into the shaft.
- A **cistern** is a roof-fed storage chamber, normally vaulted, covered, and
  reached through a small draw hatch. Better examples have a leaf grate, a
  settling bay, and a way to send the first dirty roof water to the drain.
- A **trough** is an open working supply for animals, fire, cooling, or a
  named trade. Nobody should claim that water remains fit for a cooking pot
  after mouths, tools, leather, or lime buckets have entered it.
- A **butt** or **tun** is a household or workshop rain vessel. It may be
  soundly coopered and lidded or may contain a season's roof dirt and drowned
  insects. Ownership of the vessel does not create a right to divert a
  neighbour's gutter.
- A **conduit** in a civic account can mean a short covered run, stone channel,
  gutter, or pipe. It does not imply an aqueduct or a citywide water main.
- A **pipe** is normally a short wooden, ceramic, or lead run serving one roof,
  tank, workshop, or downhill property. Most pipe disputes concern stolen rain
  or leaking walls, not water delivered under pressure.
- A **drain** carries used water and rain away. A drain is not a supply. The
  distinction is obvious until someone is short of water or wants to avoid
  paying a carrier.

The practical names of sources survive repairs and changes of keeper. A well
does not acquire each keeper's byname, and a cistern is not renamed for a
generous bencher. This belongs to the same civic instinct expressed in
“nobody renames water”: a person may repair the approach to water without
claiming authorship of the water itself.

### Ford Well

Ford Well stands near `(88, 35)`, north-west of the old Lanthorn crossing
ground and outside the preserved footprint of that crossing. A steep tiled
roof shelters a heavy windlass, pale stone curb, fixed public bucket, drinking
lip, animal trough, paved drain apron, queue space, and the keeper's niche.
The spill runs into a covered street drain. There is no ornamental basin and
no water left standing merely to mirror the sky.

Its water is cold through most of the year, faintly chalky, and usually clear
after the first bucket. It keeps a steadier level than the smaller court wells.
Nearby households favour it for drinking and cooking. Bakers with premises on
the Fabric routes value its predictable taste. Fabric labourers wet stone,
wash dust from hands, and fill covered work vessels there, though large draws
are sent to later turns. Petitioners waiting on church or civic business,
licensed street sellers, servants from Chapter houses, pilgrims, and children
sent from Skinners' Court make the crowd less local than at any other source.

The keeper opens before the Kindling, draws once into a white-glazed bowl,
looks and smells, then sends that first water down the apron unless the source
has been in constant use overnight. The act is practical: it clears water that
stood in the bucket and shows grit, oil, insects, or a changed smell. Some
pilgrims ask to buy the “first water.” The keeper refuses. Water discarded as
a check is not a relic made scarce for sale.

On ordinary days there are two loose lines. Hand vessels and household yokes
stand on the lane side; trade vessels and cask barrows wait against the outer
wall. Animals drink only at the trough. A resident carrying one pot can pass
a brewer's six tubs, but cannot claim six household turns by setting out six
children with six cups. The keeper's authority consists largely of knowing
when a rule is being used honestly.

On major feast days the vergers rope the cathedral approach and redirect the
queue so water traffic does not block service routes. They may order where
people stand; they may not close a common well for Chapter use. Every few years
a zealous verger rediscovers this distinction and the Fabric ward hand has to
state it at the curb before witnesses.

A worn relief of Saint Vhairé appears beneath the roof because the ground is
associated with her crossing. Travellers touch its lower edge with a wet
thumb. Some leave a wick on the dry ledge. These are ordinary devotions. The
water does not remain miraculously pure, does not cure travellers' feet, and
does not show the Emblem at the Concurrence. A keeper who has heard that last
claim too often answers, “The Rose is indoors and the bucket is here.”

Ford Well is famous because it is central, reliable, public, and crowded—not
because it is the purest water in the world. A citizen can praise it, dislike
its chalk, or prefer rainwater without taking a position on Saint Vhairé or
the Great Rose.

### Slate Cistern

Slate Cistern occupies a back working court uphill of the Wickmarket. Its name
comes from the long slate catch roofs of three adjoining storage and meeting
houses, not from a slate-lined tank. Gutters feed a grated stone settling box;
a hinged board sends the first dirty run of a new rain to the street drain.
Once the roofs have washed, the keeper shifts the board and clean flow enters
the vaulted cistern.

Fresh Slate water is soft and prized by laundresses. After weeks underground
it becomes flat. In the first autumn rains it can carry a trace of soot if the
roof sweepers were late. Chandlers use overflow and permitted work turns for
cooling vessels and scrubbing away grease, but tallow buckets are marked and
may not approach the draw hatch. The distinction between a clean water bucket
and a grease bucket is enforced more fiercely here than many formal market
rules.

Part of the cistern is a fire reserve. A low internal mark shows the volume
that ordinary drawing is not meant to pass after Lamplight. The reserve hatch
is sealed with cord and the ward hand's impressed clay. Breaking that seal is
lawful during a visible fire or the Ruin and otherwise requires an account to
the Common Bench. This modest seal produces grand suspicions. Chandlers accuse
laundresses of taking fire water in lidded pails; laundresses answer that the
hot-work stalls use more in an afternoon than a lane of linen uses in a week.

The source is busiest after a sound night's rain and nearly deserted during a
long dry spell. A cistern queue therefore begins with roof news: who swept,
which gutter leaked, whether pigeons nested above the west run, and how many
marked steps the tank gained. A person can learn the state of half the Wick
Ward roofs by listening for ten minutes, though not necessarily learn the
truth about any household beneath them.

The F.407 tallow fire burned six stalls. Sand and smothering did more at the
hot vessels than thrown water could have done, but Slate Cistern supplied the
roof and wall bucket line that kept sparks from carrying into the back lanes.
Chandlers cite the fire whenever anyone proposes reducing the sealed reserve.

### Tenter Cistern

Tenter Cistern lies on the Cloth Ward side of Tenterhook Lane. A broad tiled
catch roof and several lawfully shared gutters feed two underground chambers:
a small settling chamber that can be emptied and scrubbed, then the main draw
chamber. An overflow fills covered work troughs. Used fulling, washing, and dye
water leaves by controlled culverts; it never returns to the clean chamber and
does not become a picturesque open stream.

Rainwater's softness makes this source economically important. It takes soap
and rinses cloth differently from the hard water at Lodge or Bitter. Fullers,
cloth washers, dyers, shearers cleaning tables, and laundresses all want it.
Nearby households also retain an older curb right and resent being treated as
an inconvenience inside a trade district.

The compromise is a turn-list. Hand vessels have precedence before Dayspring
and again near Lamplight. Between those times assessed workshops draw agreed
lots, written as marks rather than a promise of unlimited volume. A workshop
may send apprentices, servants, or hired carriers, but it may not multiply its
mark by multiplying its people. When the cistern drops below the low line,
cloth turns are shortened before household access is suspended.

The valuable right here is often not a right to the hatch but a right to a
roof. A gutter may cross three property lines before reaching the settling
box. Repairs to one jetty can alter its fall. A landlord may quietly turn a
lead elbow toward a private washroom and claim that rain falling on his tile
was always his. The Line-keeper sees gradients and old fastenings; neighbours
bring rent books, repair accounts, and memories of where water ran before the
Hammering. This is why a disputed private pipe can occupy the Civic Measure
Court for longer than a disputed bucket.

Tenter Cistern has the most literate queue in the network because cloth marks,
orders, and accounts already surround it. That does not make it the most
honest. It merely ensures that every act of selfishness can be described as a
pre-existing entitlement.

### Lodge Well

Lodge Well is the roofed working well near the masons' lodge in Coswald's
Yard. Its public face opens toward an outer lane; its work face feeds covered
troughs inside the yard. This arrangement lets households draw without
walking between hoists and banker benches while giving builders a route for
large vessels that does not block the household curb.

The water is pale, hard, and dependable. It leaves a light crust on neglected
vessels. Masons call it honest because it behaves the same from week to week.
It supplies drinking water, dust washing, stone wetting, timber work, and
carefully separated lime-slaking troughs. No bucket that has entered lime,
stone slurry, or an animal trough returns to the public windlass.

The lodge keeps the windlass, roof, buckets, and troughs as part of its
customary burden. The Common Chest pays for deep lining and major stonework
because the public curb remains common. A lodge porter or trusted senior
apprentice orders the daylight turns. The Wallwright ward hand holds the seal
for closure and the second night key. This divided keeping is efficient until
something breaks, at which point the lodge calls the shaft civic and the
Bench calls the windlass a lodge tool.

Stone and timber work can require heavy draws. Householders accept that an
urgent shoring crew may fill ahead of ordinary trade, especially when a wall
is moving. They do not accept that every well-connected master's routine job
is urgent. “The wall is falling” has consequently become both a real warning
and the oldest joke in the Lodge queue.

The yard's covered work troughs are valuable during fire because water is
already above ground. A deep well may contain more, but every bucket still has
to travel the shaft. Lodge practice therefore keeps at least one trough free
of slurry at day's end. Apprentices dislike cleaning it; neighbours remember
which master last argued that the rule could wait until morning.

### Three-Curb

Three-Curb stands where three old residential courts meet the Cinder Ward
working lanes. The courts were divided and rebuilt around a broad older shaft
until each obtained its own stone mouth and windlass. All three mouths reach
the same water. The well is neither three springs nor three separate shafts.

The curbs do not match. One is dark old stone worn hollow at the bucket side;
one is a neat post-Rains rebuilding; the third is a blunt Hammering repair
made from whatever sound blocks could be brought through the lane. Three
little roofs meet badly above them. Rain finds the joints, ropes cross when
drawers are careless, and a dropped bucket may hang where all three courts
must help retrieve it.

Water from each mouth tastes the same when drawn into clean vessels. This
plain fact has never ended claims that one curb is sweeter, another deeper,
and the third somehow receives whatever the other courts throw away. The
claims rise whenever one court has sickness or a keeper skims oil from a
bucket. Children can be made to swear to a difference after being told which
cup came from which curb.

Keeping rotates by Bellday. Each court maintains its rope and roof share;
common masonry is assessed among all three. The arrangement creates good
records in years of friendship and magnificent ambiguity in years of repair.
A court may argue that a cracked stone belongs to one curb while a mason says
the whole head is moving. The Common Bench can order emergency work, but the
Civic Measure Court still has to decide who owes what afterward.

Three-Curb is primarily a household well. Cinder apprentices, food sellers,
and small workshops use it in hand vessels. The glass and furnace trades keep
their own controlled work supplies and fire stores; they may not empty a
residential shaft merely because their need is hot and dramatic. During a
Cinder Row alarm, however, all three windlasses are worked and the courts'
ordinary quarrel becomes an unusually efficient bucket line.

### Chain Well

Chain Well lies in the Weigh Ward approaches north of the Tallage. Its deep
shaft uses an iron chain over a broad windlass and two iron-bound public
buckets. The chain was adopted because heavy, nearly continuous drawing wore
rope quickly and because loose buckets had a habit of leaving with freight
crews. Its slow clank, bucket knock, and windlass pawl can be heard around a
corner before the curb is visible.

The water is usually clear and carries an iron or coin-like taste that grows
stronger when the level is low. Porters, carters, stable hands, salt-house
labourers, poorer nearby households, and carriers filling for rough use form
most of the queue. Brewers and fine cooks generally prefer Ford or stored rain.
Animals take Chain water readily, which is less proof of purity than carters
like to claim.

The mechanism is expensive. Each link is inspected for opening, the axle is
greased outside the shaft, and the keeper keeps a clean leather guard between
the bearings and the mouth. A careless repair can put grease on the chain and
then on the water. A cheap repair can lose a bucket at depth. Bellfounders and
smiths argue over replacement work, while the Chest-keeper asks why an object
made of iron must cost almost as much as a small bell.

Hand vessels still precede bulk freight draws, but Chain Well is where the
rule receives its hardest test. A salt porter with a thirsty team can claim
that animals blocking the Tallage are a public obstruction. A household can
answer that a freight owner's bad timing does not empty a cooking pot. The
keeper uses the turn-list, the trough, and a talent for insults short enough
not to delay the chain.

The chain is not made from the lord of Harne's river chain, and no authenticated
link from F.54 is buried in the windlass. The story arose because Ombreval
already displays an alleged Harne link at some Weighdays and because iron near
the Tallage attracts that history. Metallurgy, repair accounts, and the age of
the mechanism all contradict it. This does not prevent a carrier from telling
the tale to a newcomer for a drink.

### Reed Cistern

Reed Cistern stands on a raised, paved apron off Maren's Green, far enough from
fish washing and Tanners' Slip that their drains can be kept visibly separate.
A low tiled catch-shed feeds a sealed tank through grates and a settling box.
The draw hatch sits above known flood marks. Its structure is deliberately
plain: the Reed Ward trusts a tank it can inspect more than a pretty basin on
low ground.

The water is fresh and soft after rain, then flat after storage. The keeper
sweeps the catch tiles, sends their first washing to the drain, skims the
settling box, and admits the clean run. Fish sellers may use the overflow
trough after household turns. Tanners must bring their own work vessels and
may not draw from the clean hatch during hide processing. Funerary washing at
Saint Maren's uses water carried in covered vessels, not a bucket dipped near
the crypt.

Reed Ward once had more shallow wells. The Great Rains fouled them, and the
post-diversion fall and change of groundwater made several unreliable. A few
remain for rough work; none is treated as a secret continuation of the Cut.
The sealed public cistern became the ward's trusted household source precisely
because it can be separated from shallow ground, drains, old cellars, and
burial soil.

In a long dry spell Reed Cistern depends on carried refill. Casks come from a
sound well, are witnessed at filling, sealed with the carrier's mark, and
emptied through the settling side. This is expensive. The ward then argues
over whether the Common Chest should pay because low-ground history is a civic
burden, or whether Reed should bear a ward cost like every high ward that
maintains a deep shaft.

Outsiders call it “grave water” because Saint Maren's churchyard and charnel
door are near the Green. The insult is materially wrong in F.437: the tank is
roof-fed, raised, and sealed from the churchyard. Reed residents who know every
gutter and drain hear the phrase as both ignorance and contempt. A real crack
would still be serious; sound construction is not eternal protection.

### Step Cistern

Step Cistern occupies a narrow side court on the slope behind the Bellstand,
not the centre of the proclamation square. The domestic draw lobby is reached
down three broad steps under a pent roof. Above it, a second opening admits
roof water through a settling bay; a third locked gate gives direct emergency
access to the reserve chamber. The keeper's three keys are therefore mundane
and publicly accounted: clean draw, settling and drain, fire reserve.

The water is cool and slightly stony from the tank lining. Eastern households,
watch rooms, civic clerks, sellers attending proclamations, private tub
attendants, and boiler tenders all use it. It is busiest immediately after the
Scold calls a well-attended proclamation, when people who expected to stand
for a few minutes find themselves waiting half a day.

The stair and vault give the place a recognisable sound. Pail rims ring on the
lowest step, voices return from the draw lobby, and water entering after rain
can be heard behind the wall. None of this proves a tunnel. The cistern has a
small inspection crawl and drain, not a route to the Ilvane Chapel, Old Sluice,
Bellstand tower, or river.

The keeping is poorly paid because the source is neither a famous deep well
nor controlled by a wealthy trade. The keeper's room and small stipend are
supposed to compensate for opening before Kindling, locking after the Snuffing,
scrubbing stone, managing civic bulk draws, and answering every accusation
about the reserve. In practice, Step keepers often take tub-heating work,
doorstep alms, or carrying between turns. The result is not corruption by
definition. It is a city demanding constant labour from an office it funds as
if keys did the work themselves.

### Bitter Well

Bitter Well lies among the eastern housing and small metal trades on the road
side of Bell-and-Sluice Ward. It is a deep, narrow shaft with a rope windlass,
stone curb, small animal trough, and unusually little queue shelter. Its water
is clear but carries a bitter mineral taste and leaves a hard pale scale. The
taste strengthens as the level falls.

Households with a choice do not favour it for bread, delicate cooking, or ale.
It also takes soap poorly. It remains important because it is dependable when
roof cisterns are low. Animals drink it; metal and building workers use it for
rough washing and cooling; eastern households use it for all purposes when
distance, price, or shortage makes a fashionable preference irrelevant.
Carriers can always sell Bitter water honestly if they name the source. Passing
it off as Ford water is difficult once the buyer tastes it.

**Sibbe Quern** keeps Bitter Well in F.437. She keeps its rope, cover, turn-list,
trough, and low-water marks, and she answers strangers in clipped practical
phrases. The outer strands of the raising rope are presently fraying where it
runs across the upper guide. Sibbe has reported it. A replacement is ordinary,
urgent maintenance—not evidence of sabotage, poisoning, or a plot against the
ward.

Bitter water has a medicinal reputation among people who equate a strong taste
with a strong effect. It is sold in small bottles by dishonest resellers as a
purge, a cure for joint pain, a means of restoring appetite, and a punishment
for drunkenness. It sometimes loosens a person's bowels and sometimes does
nothing. No reliable healing property is authorial fact. The keeper permits
people to fill lawful vessels; she is not responsible for the claims they make
three streets away.

Because its flavour is obvious, Bitter Well is the first source named in an
eastern poisoning scare and the easiest one to clear by comparing routes. If
five sick households all received a carrier's cask while neighbours drawing
directly remain well, the common source is a poor first explanation. Queue
rumor rarely waits for that comparison.

## Work supplies outside the main ward list

### The Shambles well

The Shambles has the dedicated well already required by the city's plan. It
stands uphill of the slaughter courts on a raised apron, with its drain led
away from the shaft. Butchers, renderers, pen keepers, hide sorters, and dung
collectors use marked work buckets. Animals drink at separate troughs. Carried
barrels supplement the well on heavy market days.

This is not a preferred household source. That reputation is partly sensible
and partly social disgust: a well can be sound while the work around it is
unpleasant. The Bench inspects the apron, lining, covers, and downhill drains
because one broken channel can make the sensible fear true. Shambles workers
resent householders who demand clean meat and tallow while speaking as if the
people who provide both willingly drink blood from the curb.

### Seven Lofts and other fire stores

Seven Lofts keeps covered work tanks and roof-fed fire casks because grain dust,
timber roofs, dry stores, and hoists make delay dangerous. These are compound
supplies, not a public drinking source. Their covers, seals, turning schedule,
and distance from spoiled grain matter as much as total volume.

Cinder Row keeps covered sand tubs and separated water stores. Bellfounders'
Yard, the glass work, bakehouses, breweries, stables, and larger inns likewise
keep some water above ground. A deep well cannot answer a roof fire quickly if
the first hundred efforts are spent raising buckets. The city's fire supply is
therefore distributed among ordinary vessels that must be kept full before
any bell rings.

### Court, household, and private sources

Dozens of smaller arrangements complete the network:

- a shared court shaft with three or four household keys;
- a sound private well whose owner sells neighbours a draw during dry weeks;
- roof butts reserved for laundry or garden watering;
- a brewer's covered tank filled by hired carriers;
- a landlord's cistern shared by lease, with the right omitted from the newest
  copy of the rent book;
- abandoned wells capped beneath yards after Hammering deaths and the Long
  Departure; and
- shallow work wells officially barred for drinking but still used when the
  nearest queue is long.

Private does not mean unregulated. The Line-keeper and ward hand may inspect a
new shaft, dangerous cover, leaking tank, or pipe that wets a neighbour's wall.
A private owner may charge for access and gear. The owner may not foul a common
source, block a public drain, or turn a neighbour's established gutter by
calling rainfall private property.

Empty property makes old rights difficult. A court may have used a well for a
century through a house whose owner left in the Long Departure. A returning
heir may own the passage but not have the right to close it. A tenant may own
no masonry and still possess a witnessed draw at Kindling by lease and custom.
Water disputes therefore join Hammering claims, party walls, shared roofs, and
blocked stairs among the ordinary work of the Civic Measure Court.

## Who governs common water

The Common Bench governs common wells, public cisterns, street drains, and the
rules that keep one use from spoiling another. The Common Chest pays for deep
lining, major masonry, public roofs, emergency rope and chain, drain work, and
the basic keeping contracts. The arrangement descends from the same civic
claim that supports walls and watches: water needed by every household cannot
depend entirely on whether one neighbour has a generous landlord.

Responsibility is deliberately divided:

- the **Common Bench** makes ordinary water orders, emergency restrictions,
  assessments, and repair warrants;
- the **Line-keeper** views levels, gradients, drains, walls, pipes, catchment
  roofs, and boundaries when construction or a dispute requires survey;
- each **ward hand** inspects nuisances, carries complaints, witnesses closure
  seals, and checks that a keeper has reported a serious defect;
- the **Chest-keeper** pays a warranted repair and demands an account for rope,
  chain, buckets, stone, timber, lead, and hired labour;
- a source's **keeper** performs the daily work and has immediate authority at
  the curb; and
- the **Civic Measure Court** hears fraud, damage, access, pipe, gutter, debt,
  and repair-share cases that ordinary mediation cannot settle.

There is no separate water court, master of all wells, hereditary water lord,
or established water-carriers' guild. A keeper can be contracted as an
individual, a household, a lodge servant, or part of a rotating court duty.
The form follows the source. A busy deep well needs a near-constant keeper; a
small cistern may be kept by the household receiving the adjacent room; Three-
Curb distributes the burden among its courts.

The keeper normally receives a small wage, a room or niche where one exists,
and limited permission to take paid carrying or tub work outside the busiest
turns. The office is not comfortable ownership. A keeper may not sell a common
draw, reserve the best hour for friends, invent a bucket fee, or lease a public
key. A keeper may charge for a privately owned vessel, an agreed delivery, or
labour beyond the keeping bond.

### Common water and private labour

The ordinary rule is simple: **no fee at a common curb for a reasonable
household draw**. Wards pay through common assessment, and heavy trades may owe
specific repair money or labour because they wear gear and drains faster.
During shortage, quantity is limited by turns rather than by auctioning the
remaining water to the richest queue.

This does not make delivered water free. A carrier sells time, strength,
vessels, route knowledge, and the risk of spilled or stolen loads. A private
well owner may sell access. A cooper may rent a sound cask. A boiler tender may
charge separately for fuel and heating. Ombreval speech compresses the
distinction into **“the curb is common; the stairs are not.”**

The distinction is politically important. Wealthy masters sometimes argue
that their assessment buys priority; poor residents answer that it buys a
sound common well. Carriers are accused of selling water the city already
paid for; carriers answer that anyone making the accusation is welcome to
carry the pails home without charge.

## Queue law and curb custom

No single order can settle every source, but the Common Bench recognises a
shared rule called **hand before trade**. One household pot or pair of pails
draws before a vat, work cask, freight team, or repeated commercial load. The
rule protects ordinary need without pretending that bakers, brewers, animals,
and workshops can wait forever. Keepers therefore alternate hand turns with
scheduled bulk turns instead of allowing either class to consume the whole
day.

The normal order is:

1. **Visible emergency.** Fire, collapse rescue, urgent sickroom need, and
   childbirth can be admitted at once. The claim must be concrete enough for
   the keeper and queue to judge; “my master says it is urgent” is not enough.
2. **Hand vessels.** A person carrying one pot, bucket, or household pair has
   the common first claim.
3. **Contracted carriers and larger household loads.** Yokes and barrows take
   longer and are interleaved so a professional cannot hold the curb all day.
4. **Animals and routine work.** Trough flow and written trade turns proceed
   without letting animals mouth the draw vessel.
5. **Exceptional bulk.** A brewer's casks, major building work, cistern refill,
   or institutional demand receives an agreed hour and may owe extra wear or
   carrying labour.

The order is custom rather than a mechanical ladder. An animal team blocking
a gate may be moved ahead; a laundress with a fevered child may be treated as
an urgent household; a brewer whose mash will spoil may bargain for an
exchange of turns. The keeper's job is to preserve the principle while
recognising the actual street.

Empty vessels do not hold a place indefinitely. A child may stand for a
household, but six cups do not make six household turns. No one lowers a
personal vessel into a public shaft. No dirty bucket sits on the inner curb.
No hide, fish, mop, tool, or animal goes into the draw bucket. If a person
drops a vessel, the keeper decides whether retrieval is safe, who pays for a
hook, and whether the source must close while it is recovered.

At the Snuffing, a well cover and windlass are locked and a cistern hatch is
barred. Night access remains possible for fire, birth, severe illness, or
civic need. The keeper has a key; the ward hand or another named officer keeps
a witnessed second means of opening the source. A person who wakes a keeper
for an invented emergency may be fined. A keeper who refuses a real one can
lose the bond.

### The daily rhythm

The Kindling queue is quiet and purposeful: cooks, bakers, domestic servants,
householders who must climb stairs, and carriers beginning contracted rounds.
Around Dayspring come animals, market sellers, apprentices, and larger work
vessels. High Wick favours trade turns while many household users are at work.
The Waning brings a second press of cooks, returning servants, laundresses,
and people who postponed carrying until the day cooled. Near Lamplight,
keepers try to fill fire troughs, finish household draws, settle disputed
marks, and close before curfew.

Market days distort the pattern. Highmarket crowds Ford, Lodge, Slate, Tenter,
and Three-Curb. Lowmarket presses Chain and Reed. A proclamation crowds Step.
A feast crowds Ford even when no ritual uses the water. Rain reverses ordinary
preferences: everyone who can use a household butt stays home, while Slate and
Tenter draw people eager for fresh soft water.

## Well keepers

A good keeper's work is repetitive, skilled, and easiest to notice when it is
not done. The daily tasks include:

- opening and closing at the proper offices;
- checking cover, roof, curb, apron, steps, drains, trough, rope or chain,
  bucket hoops, axle, pawl, and lock;
- drawing and examining the first water after a quiet period;
- sounding the level and remembering how quickly it changed;
- keeping trade turns and marking missed or exchanged draws;
- stopping dirty vessels at the outer apron;
- cleaning leaves, soot, silt, ice, dung, grease, and spilled work material;
- separating drinking, animal, work, overflow, and fire vessels;
- reporting cracks, bad smells, dead animals, lost buckets, seepage, sickness
  reports, and unusual falls in level; and
- closing first and arguing afterward when the source may be foul.

Keepers learn water with their senses and through comparison. They know the
ordinary sound of a full bucket entering a shaft, how a rope feels when wet,
which cistern wall sweats in cold weather, how long a tank usually gains after
rain, and which taste belongs to low summer rather than a new contaminant.
Their knowledge is local, not universal. The Ford keeper need not know why
Reed's outlet leaks, and a skilled cistern keeper may know little about a deep
chain windlass.

They also learn households. A keeper sees who carries personally, who sends a
servant, who has changed from two pails to one, whose child can now lift a
bucket, whose door has stopped ordering water, and which trade works before
dawn. That knowledge makes a keeper a useful witness and an easy target. When
sickness follows a source, the keeper is blamed for failing to see it. When a
source closes in time, the keeper is blamed for causing scarcity. When a
bucket breaks, both owner and carrier remember handing it over sound.

A keepership can support a modest household at a major source. A lesser one
may be intermittent work supplemented by carrying, heating tubs, cleaning
drains, mending rope, begging, or relatives' earnings. Keys at a belt can look
like authority while the person carrying them lacks money for supper.

## Water carriers and the price of distance

Professional carriers are part of the **water and bathing worker** occupation
family. Some carry water every day; others combine it with tub attendance,
boiler tending, laundry hauling, market porterage, or irregular labour. Women,
men, adolescents, servants, and older workers all carry. The work is divided
more by strength, route, vessel, household need, and access to a cart than by
one rigid social rule.

The ordinary tools are:

- a shoulder yoke with two covered pails;
- a single pail and balancing hand for stairs too narrow for a yoke;
- a handbarrow holding a small lidded cask;
- a two-wheeled cask cart for inns, brewers, institutions, cistern refill, and
  fire stores; and
- a dipper and clean transfer vessel so the delivery cask does not enter the
  household pot.

Good carriers keep drinking vessels lidded and separate from rough-work casks.
They name the source when taste matters and mark contracted casks at filling.
Mixing two sound sources for rough use is not an offence if the buyer did not
contract for one. Selling Bitter as Ford, topping fresh rainwater with trough
water, or filling from a closed shaft is fraud.

A nearby pair of pails may cost a penny when the queue is short. Distance,
steps, waiting, market crowds, night work, winter ice, and the carrier's own
vessels increase the price. Regular households often settle by the week or
exchange food, lodging, laundry, or other work. There is no universal tariff:
the value lies less in a fixed volume than in getting a known vessel to a
known door at a useful hour.

Carriers know routes differently from cart drivers and pilgrims. They know
which passage accepts a yoke, where a barrow must be turned, which external
gallery has a missing board, which landlord bars a short stair, and which
house has a sound cistern but no one strong enough to draw. They also know
where a delivery can be watered down unseen. Reputation is therefore capital.
A carrier publicly proved to have used a foul cask may lose a street's custom
even after the source itself is cleared.

Their gossip travels across household boundaries because a morning round can
touch a baker, sickroom, wealthy lodging, tavern kitchen, and poor upper room.
It still follows a route. A Bell-and-Sluice carrier does not automatically
know a Wick Ward court, and a cask-cart worker knows ground-floor institutions
better than families reached only by stairs. Good prompting should give a
carrier a network, not the whole city's secrets.

During the Ruin, keepers can take over the order of water already at the curb,
and ward officers can direct carts and able carriers into a bucket line. Who
pays for a split cask, injured shoulder, missed private contract, or horse
worked through the night is settled afterward and often badly.

## Household water

A household stores drinking water in its cleanest covered pot, jar, pail, or
cask. Wealth changes vessel size and decoration more reliably than it changes
the need to carry. A prosperous house may have leaded roof gutters, a private
tank, servants, and a copper for heating; a poor room may have one sound pot
and no fuel. Both must keep soot, vermin, hands, ladles, and old food out of
what they intend to drink.

Water is used in descending orders of cleanliness. Fresh water goes to drink,
dough, pottage, brewing, and a sick person. Water that has rinsed vegetables
may scrub a table. Warm wash water may take a floor or a chamber pot before it
reaches the drain. This economy is practical but never perfectly observed. A
crowded household can confuse the clean dipper with the work dipper, and
reusing water does not make every final use safe.

Citizens wash hands, faces, cloths, utensils, and parts of the body more often
than they fill a whole tub. Full bathing exists in private houses and hired
rooms. Tub attendants carry, heat, pour, scrub, and remove the water; boiler
tenders supply heat. Ombreval still has no established public bathhouse. A
story may include private tub work without inventing a grand civic bathing
institution.

Boiling every drinking vessel would consume time and fuel the city does not
have. Water is heated for food, drink, washing, sickness, and bathing, but
routine boiling is not universal. Cloth straining, settling, skimming, and
charcoal or sand vessels can improve appearance and taste without guaranteeing
safety. People can follow a sensible inherited rule for the wrong explanation
and ignore a dangerous source because it looks clear.

## Water by trade

### Food, baking, and brewing

Bakers need predictable water before most streets are fully awake. A changed
taste or measure changes dough, but a missed draw can stop the day's bread
entirely. Bakeries near a sound source often maintain a contracted early turn;
others store a day ahead in covered vessels. In scarcity, the Bench treats
common bread as a strong claim without granting every baker unlimited priority.

Brewers are among the heaviest users. They distinguish source by taste and by
how it behaves in the mash, even when they cannot explain the cause. A brewery
can hire cask carts, maintain tanks, and schedule repeated turns. Its waste,
fuel, malt traffic, and cooling water are as important as its draw. “People
drink ale” is not permission to ignore the greater volume of water used to
make it.

Cooks, taverns, food sellers, and institutions prefer clear, familiar water.
Fish sellers and butchers require much rough wash water but may not return
their marked buckets to a clean curb. In a sickness scare, food businesses
have an interest in blaming a distant source rather than their storage pot,
knife table, cask, or servant's hands.

### Cloth, laundry, and dye

Fulling, washing, rinsing, and some dye work value soft rainwater. This makes
Slate and especially Tenter economically contested. Laundresses know which
water takes soap, which leaves linen stiff, and which soot falls from a given
roof. Their expertise is practical and often dismissed by people whose only
water work is paying them.

Used cloth water can contain grease, fulling matter, dye, dirt, and soap. It
goes to controlled culverts or permitted rough reuse, not back to the supply.
A blocked culvert can foul a court, undermine a wall, or lead people to accuse
the clean cistern above it. The source and drain must be investigated as two
connected but distinct structures.

### Stone, glass, metal, and hot work

Masons, plasterers, carpenters, glaziers, bellfounders, smiths, chandlers,
bakers, and brewers all need water beside fuel, tools, and storage. Different
uses demand different care. Drinking and eye washing remain clean. Lime,
slurry, quenching, cooling, grease, wax, ash, and furnace tools remain in work
vessels.

Water is not a universal answer to fire. Sand, lids, shutters, cutting off
fuel, pulling down burning material, and preventing spread may matter more.
Throwing a small bucket into hot tallow can spread burning fat and scald the
thrower. The Wickmarket keeps sand beside hot-work islands for that reason.
Water protects adjacent roofs and walls once someone has judged where it can
help.

### Animals, streets, and waste

Horses, oxen, donkeys, dogs, poultry, and penned animals consume water and
foul it if traffic is not separated. Public troughs stand below the draw lip
or receive a controlled pour. An animal does not drink from a household
bucket because its owner is in a hurry. Overflow and trough drainage must
leave the queue space before it turns the apron to mud.

Street washing is selective, not a daily cleansing of the entire city. Water
is spent on blood, grease, dangerous lime, market filth, sickroom waste, and
specific nuisances where dry sweeping is insufficient. Dung and night soil
are collected as material; they are not simply washed into every drain.

### Sickness, birth, and death

Midwives, household carers, physicians, barber-surgeons, and funerary workers
need carried water, clean cloths, heat, and privacy. Their urgency can move a
vessel ahead in a queue, but the well keeper does not demand a diagnosis in
public. A repeated false claim damages the claimant's standing because the
custom depends on neighbours recognising real need.

Funerary washing uses covered household or church vessels. Water that has
washed a body is disposed of under local funerary direction and never returned
to a public trough. No authorial fact gives it magical danger. Respect,
contamination, and belief are sufficient reasons to handle it separately.

## Knowing when water is foul

Ombreval has no germ theory, laboratory, or reliable universal test. It does
have generations of observation. People know to fear a dead animal in a
shaft, privy seepage, tannery runoff, slaughter drainage, dye and grease,
cracked cistern plaster, roof filth, a drain running backward, sudden clouding,
an unfamiliar film, a strong new smell, or several households becoming ill
after sharing one delivery.

A keeper's first examination uses ordinary means:

- compare colour and grit in a pale glazed bowl;
- smell the first and later draws separately;
- taste only if there is no obvious sign of danger;
- sound the level and compare the rate of fall or rise;
- inspect curb, lining visible from above, roof, gutters, settling box, drains,
  apron, trough, rope or chain, and nearby works;
- ask who drew, what vessel was used, what fell, what was washed, and when the
  change began; and
- compare direct users with deliveries from a carrier's cask.

The last step is socially difficult and extremely useful. If direct users of
Ford remain well while households on one carrier's round fall sick, the cask,
transfer vessel, food, or route deserves attention. If unrelated carriers and
direct users around one curb share the illness, the source becomes more
suspect. If a whole lane is sick but its neighbours use three sources, a
shared meal, drain, or household contact may matter more. Citizens do not need
modern language to notice such patterns; they can also notice them and still
prefer an accusation that fits an old grievance.

### Closure and reopening

A keeper may stop drawing immediately. The windlass or hatch is bound with
cord, the ward hand witnesses a **crossed-bucket mark** chalked on the cover,
and a crier or posted notice names the closure and nearest alternatives. The
mark means “do not draw,” not “this well is poisoned.” Prematurely announcing
poison can provoke assault, panic, ruined trade, and destruction of evidence.

Inspection may require lifting a dropped object, bailing and cleaning a
cistern, diverting a gutter, repairing plaster, opening a drain, replacing a
bucket, or viewing nearby privies and work pits. A deep well cannot simply be
scrubbed from top to bottom in an afternoon. After a repair it is drawn down
repeatedly as practical, allowed to settle, and compared over time. Reopening
is witnessed and entered in the Common Clerk's record.

Blessing may accompany reopening if a parish and neighbourhood want it. The
blessing restores confidence and gives thanks; it is not a substitute for
removing a carcass or mending a crack. The Candor itself does not teach that a
prayer makes foul water physically safe.

### Blame and poisoning rumors

Keepers, carriers, newcomers, tanners, dyers, fish sellers, gravediggers, and
rival wards are common targets. Deliberate poisoning is possible in the same
sense that any person can put filth in a vessel, but it is rarer than neglect,
leakage, dirty containers, spoiled food, and rumor. A poisoning claim should
therefore create an investigation, not establish its own truth.

The Civic Measure Court can punish deliberate fouling, fraudulent delivery,
breaking a closure seal, concealment of a known defect, or a malicious public
accusation supported by no witness. Honest error is harder. The law expects a
keeper to close on reasonable suspicion, not to know invisible causes.

## The water year

### Spring and Vhairestide

Spring raises many wells and fills roof storage, but the first strong rain
also washes soot, nesting matter, leaves, and winter dirt from roofs. Keepers
send that washing to drains before admitting water to a cistern. Vhairestide
turns the year and blesses the first boats beyond the walls; inside, it is a
natural season for sounding records, gutter work, bucket repair, and arguments
about who neglected winter damage.

Ford receives travellers touching Vhairé's worn relief. Reed residents watch
outer river reports without treating a high Serle as water already inside the
city. Spring cellar damp and a rising shallow well may share a cause without
being the river's return.

### Early and high summer

Cistern water is abundant after regular rain and heavily used by cloth and
laundry work. Animals, brewing, markets, building, and pilgrims increase
demand. Warm stored water turns flat faster; uncovered vessels grow foul.
Keepers clean troughs and watch children seeking cool play at aprons.

A dark, abruptly cold sky still sends Hammering survivors toward cover. Water
workers have a particular memory of F.415: smashed covers, broken conduits,
blocked streets, fire, and then meltwater where useful water could not be
reached. Hammer-weather can shorten a queue in moments.

### Late summer and low water

Deep wells fall, mineral tastes strengthen, and rain cisterns approach their
low marks. Market, field, animal, and building demand may all remain high.
This is the season of turn-lists, shorter cloth draws, sealed fire reserves,
carrier profit, and accusations that another ward wastes what it has.

The Hammering anniversary falls in late summer. Some keepers leave the curb
silent during the Waning mourning toll; others keep drawing because kitchens,
sickrooms, and animals do not pause. Neither practice is a citywide religious
rule.

### Autumn and Coswaldstide

Autumn rain begins to restore tanks but brings leaf-filled gutters and roof
repairs. Coswaldstide's blessing of scaffolds coincides with practical work on
catch roofs, hoists, well shelters, and stone heads. Builders claim the season
as proof that water masonry belongs in their repair priority; lower wards
answer that a beautiful roof does nothing for a leaking tank.

### Winter, frost, and Wet Alms

Roofs yield less useful water in frost. Aprons ice, ropes stiffen, bucket hoops
loosen, and a spilled load can close a narrow passage. Deep water remains
liquid, but reaching it becomes more dangerous. Keepers spread grit and ash
where it will not enter the shaft. Carriers charge for risk and arrive more
slowly.

Fire does not cease in winter. More hearths burn, while some cistern reserves
are low or partly iced at their exposed fittings. The F.229 freeze, when the
Cut itself stopped, remains proof that visible abundance can become
inaccessible. Wet Alms remembers the Great Rains with bread at the Lanthorn
door; it does not confer a special right to Ford Well, though the two queues
can obstruct one another.

## Historical spine of the network

### Before and during the Cut

The old ford-market required wells, rain vessels, and carried water even when
the Serle ran nearby. River water served animals, washing, fire, and trades,
but proximity never made every bucket fit to drink. The precise age of Ford
Well's first shaft is unknown. Its present curb, roof, and lining belong to
several repair campaigns, and the name proves continuity of place rather than
one untouched structure from F.1.

After the Cut was opened in F.83, bank households and trades could take rough
water directly. Wells remained essential above the channel, during dirty
water, flood, freeze, and for people who preferred a known shaft. The F.157
tannery fire and F.171 glaziers' fire both strengthened habits of separate
work water, sand, covered reserves, and drains, though later generations
repeated old economies whenever repairs cost money.

The great freeze of F.229 stopped the Cut and cost boatmen their work. It also
crowded wells that households had neglected while open water was convenient.
Later keepers cite the freeze whenever someone argues that one abundant source
makes reserve vessels unnecessary.

### The Great Rains and diversion, F.362–369

The Great Rains filled cellars, broke banks, reversed drains, overflowed
cisterns, and fouled shallow wells across lower ground. A roof-fed tank was not
automatically safe when its drain backed up or its cover lay beneath flood
water. Deep wells survived unevenly. The disaster was an accumulation of wet
foundations, rotten timber, spoiled stores, failed work, and suspect water—not
a single dramatic poisoning.

Closing and filling the Cut removed the city's easiest source of bulk rough
water. Between F.363 and F.369 the Common Bench, wards, lodges, landlords, and
householders deepened shafts, repaired cisterns, enlarged roof catchments,
formalised turns, and expanded carrying. Some modern water rights are therefore
older uses rewritten after the river moved. Others are post-Rains bargains
that claim impossible antiquity because old custom sounds stronger in court.

Reed Cistern's authority, Chain Well's heavy use, and the value of cask routes
all grow from this transition. So does a continuing ward grievance: the city
made the Serle a cart journey away, then treated the cost of carrying as a
private inconvenience.

### The Wickmarket fire, F.407

When tallow fire burned six Wickmarket stalls, sand, covers, hooks, and
separation controlled the hottest work while water defended roofs and
neighbouring walls. Slate Cistern's reserve entered every later argument over
how low a cistern may be drawn. Accounts disagree about whether the reserve
was admirably full or dangerously low. The surviving result—only six stalls
lost—lets both sides claim vindication.

### The Hammering, F.415

The Great Hail broke well roofs, buckets, gutters, conduits, cistern covers,
and drain gratings. Falling masonry cracked curbs. Ice blocked approaches.
Meltwater flooded stores and shafts with surface filth at the same time that
chimney and loft fires demanded clean, accessible reserves. Some people died
beside water they could not raise because a windlass was smashed or a lane was
blocked.

Ford's heavy roof and main windlass survived, though lesser fittings and the
trough were damaged. Lodge's above-ground work troughs supplied early bucket
lines. One Three-Curb mouth cracked and was rebuilt in unmatched stone. Slate
and Tenter lost gutters and admitted roof debris. Reed's predecessor tank was
opened by falling tile and later rebuilt on its raised plan. These are material
survivals, not miracle tales.

In the weeks afterward, water work became relief work. Keepers, carriers,
coopers, rope makers, masons, roofers, drain cleaners, carters, and household
children moved water through streets still full of wreckage. Sound containers
were scarce. Shared casks spread help and, sometimes, sickness. Repair favoured
sources serving institutions and recorded property before obscure court wells.
That inequality remains in ward memory.

The Long Departure left more shafts than keepers. Some were capped; some were
claimed privately; some remained open beside half-empty houses until the Bench
ordered covers. Present F.437 infrastructure is therefore a repaired network
serving fewer people, not a perfectly planned network built for five thousand.

## Shortage, closure, and the Ruin

### Ordinary shortage

A falling source first loses convenience, then work turns, before it loses
basic household access. The usual measures are:

1. publish the low mark and stop unlisted bulk draws;
2. shorten cloth, brewing, construction, washing, and animal turns according
   to available alternatives;
3. protect the fire reserve;
4. increase cask carriage from sound sources and inspect delivery vessels;
5. reopen a capped or private source only after viewing its structure and
   drains; and
6. assess emergency repair or carriage openly through the Common Bench.

Scarcity decisions cannot be neutral. Shortening brewing threatens food and
wages as well as drink. Denying fullers can spoil cloth and leave labourers
unpaid. Moving animals can block freight. Paying carriers helps households
that can afford them first unless the ward arranges common delivery. The
political question is not whether every use matters, but whose loss arrives
soonest and who is asked to bear it.

### A suspected source

Closure creates an immediate human map. Households move to the next curb;
carriers change routes; bakers seek stored water; animals crowd a farther
trough; an infirm resident loses an accessible source; an inn raises prices;
the accused keeper stands beside a bound windlass hearing the story change.
The source itself can remain static while the whole ward behaves differently.

A good investigation traces vessels and people as carefully as masonry. Who
drank directly? Who received a delivery? Was the cask previously used for
fish, dye, tallow, or waste? Did sickness begin before the source changed?
Which households share food, work, or a drain? A false poisoning rumor can be
recognised through these relationships without a glowing clue or infallible
test.

### Fire and the Ruin

Coswald Ironthroat calls help to a local breach or fire. The full backward
ring calls the Ruin for a burning quarter or flood emergency. At either alarm,
a well keeper suspends ordinary turns and fills the vessels directed to the
fire. Covered reserves are opened, seals witnessed if time allows, animals
moved, cask carts turned, and bucket lines formed through the routes that
remain passable.

Fire response uses water with hooks, ladders, sand, shutters, demolition tools,
wet cloth, and judgement. A bucket line is only as fast as its narrowest turn,
its smallest vessel, and the people who can keep passing. A well's recovery
rate and lifting gear matter. A cistern can deliver quickly but end suddenly.
A trough is fast but may already contain slurry. An empty decorative basin
would help nobody, which is one reason Ombreval does not spend good square
space pretending water is ornament.

Emergency access crosses ordinary property. A private roof path, court gate,
carrier's cask, workshop tank, or household bucket may be taken into use.
This does not erase ownership. Damage and compensation claims follow, with
all the usual problems of missing witnesses and people who value their lost
bucket more highly after the fire than before it.

## The curb as a social place

A well queue mixes people, but never the whole city at once. Time and source
sort the crowd:

- before Kindling: servants, bakers, cooks, carriers, and households with long
  stairs;
- at Dayspring: market sellers, apprentices, animals, and school-bound
  children carrying a last small pot;
- at High Wick: professional carriers and trade vessels;
- at the Waning: laundresses, returning workers, cooks, and neighbours with
  time to quarrel; and
- near Lamplight: people who misjudged storage, tavern workers, keepers filling
  reserves, and anyone hoping to be served before the bar goes down.

Class appears indirectly. A wealthy person is represented by a servant with
several sound vessels. A poor resident may have time but one cracked pail. A
master's apprentice can wait in a trade turn while the master's household
servant uses hand precedence. An older resident may know every queue rule but
depend on a neighbour to lift. Water makes inequality visible through bodies,
objects, and time rather than through a separate speech about wealth.

The curb is good ground for witnesses. A promise made there is not a special
water oath, but several people from unrelated households may have heard it.
“Said at the curb” means difficult to deny, not legally sacred. Keepers are
often summoned because they remember the order of people and vessels; their
memory of conversation is no more perfect than anyone else's.

News changes as it travels. A Ford seller hears that Bitter has closed; a
carrier says the rope broke; a Step queue says someone cut it; by Lowmarket a
Tallage porter says Harne agents poisoned every eastern well. The network
explains how rumor moves without requiring instant citywide knowledge.

## Current matters in F.437

There is no citywide water disaster in the present year. The Serle remains in
its outer bed, the rye stands well, and the public network functions. The
following smaller matters are enough to occupy wards, workers, and the Bench:

### Bitter's fraying rope — true and urgent

Sibbe Quern has reported visible wear at the upper guide. The well remains
open under shorter, slower draws while a replacement is obtained. The rope
was worn by work and a rough guide, not cut. If it parts, the public bucket may
be lost and a person at the windlass injured. A rumor that Sibbe invented the
defect to reduce her queue is false.

### The Three-Curb head — true defect, disputed shares

The Hammering-repaired curb has opened a finger-wide joint after seasonal
settlement. The water presently shows no new taste or grit. Masons advise
lifting and resetting more than one court's stone. Two courts agree that all
three should pay; the third says the crack lies wholly in another share. The
engineering question is easier than the debt.

### The Tenter gutter — real diversion, unsettled entitlement

A lead elbow on one catch run was recently turned toward a private upper tank.
Tool marks and clean fastenings prove alteration. It is not yet settled who
ordered it or whether an older lease reserved part of that roof water. The
cistern has lost inflow, but no pipe is secretly drawing water uphill from the
tank. The current case is theft or restoration of a right, depending on which
document and witness the court believes.

### The Reed damp seam — real damp, false grave-water story

A damp patch has appeared beside the outer outlet collar. The draw water
remains clear, and inspection places the moisture outside the sealed tank wall:
rain and overflow are entering cracked apron mortar. Repair is still needed
because persistent damp can become a structural path. The present claim that
churchyard water is entering the cistern is false.

### The Chain link — real wear, unproved favour

One chain link is pitted enough to require replacement before winter. Metal
workers have offered different repairs at different prices. The claim that a
bencher promised the work to a Bellfounders' Yard cousin is unproved. The claim
that the entire chain must be replaced with silver from the Common Chest comes
from the most expensive bidder.

### The Ford feast queue — jurisdiction, not shortage

Vergers want a wider roped lane for the next major service. Fabric residents
and sellers say the proposed lane leaves too little standing room at the well.
Both sides agree the source must remain open. The dispute concerns whose crowd
must bend around whose route, which is exactly the kind of small overlap from
which civic and Church resentment grows.

## Sayings, calls, and small customs

### Ordinary sayings

- **“Hand before trade.”** Household necessity before commercial volume; also
  used outside a queue when profit is being put before immediate need.
- **“The curb is common; the stairs are not.”** Access may be free while labour
  is not.
- **“He owns the bucket and calls it the well.”** Said of someone mistaking a
  useful office or tool for ownership of the thing served.
- **“Three curbs, four quarrels.”** Too many shares produce more disputes than
  owners.
- **“Bitter tells on the carrier.”** A substitution that cannot remain hidden;
  more broadly, an obvious lie.
- **“Chain-deep.”** Slow, laborious, and certain to cost someone iron or effort.
- **“Keep your dirt below the apron.”** Mind the boundary between private mess
  and common use.
- **“A dry rope has a long memory.”** Prepare before need. Keepers say this
  about reserves; everyone else uses it about old grudges.
- **“Said at the curb.”** Spoken before too many ordinary witnesses to deny
  comfortably.
- **“First rain to the street.”** Let the roof wash before keeping what falls;
  also, do not seize the first offer merely because it is early.

### Curb customs

- A newcomer is shown the clean and dirty side of an apron before being told
  where to stand. Correcting the vessel matters more than correcting the
  person's accent.
- A person borrowing the public dipper returns it handle-out. Handle-in means
  the user has touched the drinking end and earns a keeper's rebuke.
- Travellers wet a thumb at Ford and touch the worn edge of Vhairé's relief.
  The keeper prevents them from dipping objects into the shaft.
- At Three-Curb, each court claims to begin the Bellday rotation. In practice,
  the person holding the sound key opens and the argument follows.
- A carrier arriving with both pails full calls **“wet behind”** in a narrow
  passage. It means make room and do not turn suddenly, not that water is
  sacred.
- The first hard autumn rain is listened to from inside Slate or Tenter. The
  keeper does not shift the wash-board until the runoff loses its black edge.
- Throwing a coin into a public shaft is not a recognised devotion. It fouls
  the mechanism, risks the bucket, and makes the keeper retrieve someone
  else's performance.

### Children's source rhyme

Children use a practical rhyme whose order changes by ward:

> Ford for pot and Slate for sheet,
>
> Chain for horse and Bitter for feet,
>
> Tenter for wool and Lodge for lime,
>
> three curbs quarrel all the time.

Reed children replace the second line with “Reed for pot and Chain for beast”
and insist theirs is older. Keepers object that Bitter water is perfectly fit
for a pot and that no child in the rhyme has offered to carry from Ford. The
rhyme persists because it teaches reputations, not because it is a water order.

## Rumor pool with truth values

The tags below are author-facing. Ordinary speakers do not announce them.

- **[FALSE]** Ford Well is the old Serle trapped underground. Its shaft reaches
  groundwater north of the former crossing.
- **[FALSE]** Ford water shows the Emblem at the Passing or Concurrence. No
  exterior water reflects the impossible disc.
- **[TRUE]** The three mouths of Three-Curb reach one shaft and the same water.
- **[FALSE]** One of the Three-Curb courts receives cleaner water because its
  curb faces the Lanthorn.
- **[FALSE]** Chain Well's lifting chain contains the lost link from Harne.
- **[MIXED]** Chain water tastes of iron because of the chain. The source itself
  has a mineral taste; poor mechanism care can add grease or rust, but the
  chain does not create the ordinary flavour by itself.
- **[TRUE]** Tenter Cistern has recently lost catchment to a turned private
  pipe. Who had the right to that rain remains unsettled.
- **[FALSE IN F.437]** Reed Cistern contains seepage from Saint Maren's
  churchyard. The current damp lies outside the sealed tank.
- **[FALSE]** Bitter Well was poisoned by an agent of Harne. No such poisoning
  has occurred.
- **[MIXED]** Bitter water is a purge. A large unfamiliar drink may disturb
  some stomachs; no stable medicinal effect supports the remedies sold under
  its name.
- **[TRUE OF SOME, FALSE AS A CLASS]** Water carriers sometimes mix sources or
  use foul casks. Carriers as a body do not conspire to dilute the city.
- **[FALSE]** A well keeper can tell who will die by how a bucket strikes the
  water. Skilled keepers can hear level, rope, and bucket problems; prediction
  is a story attached to skill.
- **[UNRESOLVED]** One private court well closed after the Hammering still has
  a lawful shared access beneath an absent owner's house. Several courts make
  this claim, and surviving records do not identify one certain example.

## Small diegetic documents

### A Common Bench water order, copied for a ward hand

> Let no hide, fish basket, lime pail, grease pot, swab, or beast come within
> the clean curb. Let the well bucket fill the hand vessel and the hand vessel
> fill the trough. Hand before trade, save fire, birth, hurt, or falling wall.
> Let every bulk mark take its written turn. He who breaks a bound windlass or
> crossed bucket shall answer at Measure. The keeper shall close on doubt and
> shall not be amerced for an honest closing.

The last sentence exists because keepers once delayed closure for fear of
paying lost trade. Masters regularly quote the first half and omit the last.

### Bitter Well keeper's marks, present year

> Kindling: clear, bitter as common, line lower by one knot.
>
> Dayspring: rope hair at upper guide. Turn slowed. Ward hand told.
>
> High Wick: two beast tubs, one carrier pair, four hand pots. No grease.
>
> Waning: rope worse. Asked again for hemp. Told iron first at Chain.

The marks are a fair clerk's expansion of a keeper's shorter notches and oral
account, not proof that every keeper writes fluent prose.

### Petition of the three courts

> We agree that the head is common, the water one, the danger near, and the
> mason to be admitted. We do not agree that one third means three equal parts,
> for our curb is the old curb and stood before their houses. We ask Measure to
> order the work now and reckon the shares after, lest agreement be required
> before safety.

All three courts can sign this wording while believing it condemns the other
two.

### A carrier's promise

> One covered pair from Ford before Dayspring, six days in seven; step money
> above the second stair; no charge when the well is bound, but the round may
> be made from Lodge if the house accepts the taste.

This is a service promise, not the sale of Ford water. Its hardest words are
“before Dayspring,” because a queue can make an honest carrier break them.

## Knowledge layers for NPC use

### What nearly everyone in Ombreval knows

- The Cut is dry and the Serle lies beyond the south wall.
- Ford is the central famous well; Bitter tastes bitter; Chain uses a chain;
  Three-Curb produces quarrels.
- Common household water is not sold at the curb, but carrying costs money.
- Hand vessels normally precede trade vessels.
- A crossed-bucket mark means do not draw.
- The first roof water after dirt and drought goes to the drain.
- The Ruin ends ordinary turns and opens fire reserves.
- No sensible adult expects a public well to show the Green Sun.

### What a ward resident commonly knows

- the nearest two or three sources and the shortest route carrying a load;
- the keeper's temperament and ordinary opening habits;
- which taste is normal at low water;
- the bulk turns that create the longest queue;
- which court, trade, roof, or landlord owes a repair share; and
- the ward's preferred explanation for its latest shortage or defect.

Ward knowledge is partisan. A Reed resident knows the raised tank and rejects
“grave water”; a distant Wick seller may repeat it. A Cloth household knows
that hand rights predate a master's mark; the master remembers paying for the
catch roof.

### What keepers and experienced carriers know

- signs of wear, lost level, changed taste, blocked drainage, dirty containers,
  and false urgency;
- how source, vessel, route, and household connect;
- which work uses can safely share rough water and which must remain separate;
- who has keys, marks, casks, carts, stairs, and physical ability to help;
- that closing early produces anger and closing late produces graves; and
- that a convincing rumor often contains one true bucket inside a false well.

They do not automatically know the medical cause of sickness, private motives,
the content of closed civic records, or events outside their routes.

### Author-only fixed truths

- The named sources have ordinary physical causes and limitations.
- Ford does not tap the old Cut and no well reflects the Emblem.
- Three-Curb is one shaft.
- Reed's present damp is external apron seepage, not burial contamination.
- Bitter's rope is worn rather than cut.
- The Tenter diversion is real, but culpability and historic entitlement are
  genuinely unsettled.
- No current F.437 dispute is a disguised supernatural water crisis.

## Story and encounter uses

The network supports problems whose evidence is social and material:

- trace sickness by comparing direct drawers, carrier rounds, household food,
  casks, and drains;
- decide how to supply a street while a suspect source is bound;
- recover a dropped bucket without contaminating or injuring the shaft;
- prove that a pipe was newly turned while leaving the ownership of rainfall
  legally arguable;
- organise casks, keys, roof routes, and bucket lines when Ironthroat or the
  Ruin sounds;
- choose which bulk turns to shorten during low water and face the employment,
  food, animal, and fire consequences;
- distinguish Bitter's ordinary taste from a real new taint;
- find the absent witness who remembers a court's pre-Hammering draw right;
- correct a poisoning rumor before it becomes violence against a keeper,
  carrier, trade, or ward; and
- learn a household's condition from changed water habits without treating a
  carrier as an all-seeing quest giver.

The best water encounters remain legible without an explicit task. A new cord
around a windlass, a crossed-bucket mark, animals at the wrong trough, cask
carts turning into a residential lane, a silent chain, an argument over empty
pots, or a keeper sounding twice tells the player that the city's routine has
changed.

Sound and activity should distinguish sources. Ford has the wooden windlass,
trough splash, mixed central queue, and vergers' feast ropes. Chain clanks and
draws deep among carts. Three-Curb overlaps three ropes and three sets of
voices. Slate and Tenter sound with gutters after rain. Step echoes. Bitter's
rope complains at the guide. Reed has a settling board, cask refill, and the
flat slap of water in a covered tank. None needs enchanted particles or a
glowing surface to be memorable.

## Authoring guardrails

- Keep the Serle outside the south wall and the Cut dry. Do not reveal a
  navigable underground river, surviving in-city canal, or secret waterfront.
- Keep Ford Well at its established site and form: roof, windlass, curb,
  trough, drain apron, queue, keeper's niche, and deep groundwater source.
- Do not give any well magical purity, healing, prophecy, memory, voice,
  bottomless volume, or a reflection of the impossible light.
- Do not make every old belief foolish. A rule can be materially wise even
  when its explanation is incomplete. Do not make every superstition true
  merely because it is atmospheric.
- Do not give residents modern germ theory or laboratory certainty. Do let
  them observe clusters, vessels, taste, smell, drains, roof dirt, and the
  consequences of past mistakes.
- Do not repeat the myth that medieval people never drank water. Do not make
  their storage, reuse, and waste handling implausibly clean either.
- Preserve the difference between common access and paid labour. A public
  draw can be free while a delivered, heated, stored, or privately controlled
  supply is costly.
- Keep queues time-specific and local. One well is an encounter hub, not a
  magical switchboard through which every secret in Ombreval passes.
- Give a shortage downstream effects: bread, brewing, cloth, wages, animals,
  sickrooms, fire reserve, carrying price, and ward politics.
- Give contamination an actual route through shaft, roof, gutter, drain,
  vessel, food, or person. “Poisoned well” is a claim to investigate, not a
  default plot answer.
- Keep public squares as working spaces. A trough, cistern hatch, or off-centre
  curb may occupy useful ground; a grand decorative fountain should not.
- Keep fire response mixed: water, sand, hooks, ladders, shutters, demolition,
  stored vessels, bells, and human access. Water alone does not extinguish
  every material safely.
- Preserve the Common Bench's jurisdiction, ward hands, Line-keeper, Common
  Chest, and Civic Measure Court. Do not invent a water prince, modern utility,
  or separate bureaucracy that supersedes them.
- Preserve Ombreval's small present population inside a larger inherited city.
  Disused shafts, excessive old capacity, absent owners, and long routes are
  consequences of the Rains, Hammering, and Long Departure.
- None of the named network or encounter material in this lore dump asserts
  that a corresponding simulation, prop, schedule, sound, or quest is already
  implemented.
