The cathedral reckons public dates After First Glass (AFG), beginning with the dedication of the western rose. Older civic records use reigns, floods, or guildmaster tenures. This chronology makes AFG convenient without implying the glass caused the phenomenon.

Most citizens know only the bold events. Archivists, clergy, glaziers, and the Unwalled Day contest the details between them.

Before First Glass

The hill before the cathedral (c. −190 to −18 AFG)

The cathedral hill was first a defended settlement above two branches of the river. Excavations beneath the forecourt have found three street surfaces, foundation posts burned in place, and a round stone basin whose bottom remains dry even when the canal floods.

The oldest surviving local prayer asks the dawn to “enter by the two doors of heaven.” Church scholars read this as poetic parallelism. Occlusionists call it proof. The phrase appears nowhere else in the region.

An early chapel on the hill faced west, contrary to local custom. Its apse was incorporated into the undercroft. The civic-wound school notes that remote light-falls often land along the chapel's lost axis.

Masterplan of the impossible church (−17 AFG)

Bishop Calvus announced a cathedral larger than the existing hilltop could reasonably carry. The first master mason, Odren Vale, solved the foundations by building outward over terraces, streets, and retaining vaults. Later generations added towers, chapels, bridges, and whole inhabited quarters against its flanks.

Odren's surviving plan shows a blank circle at the west end labelled only “leave this measure unowned.” No contemporary explanation survives.

The making of the rose (−9 to 0 AFG)

The Guild of Saint Lume assembled the rose under Master Glazier Ysabet Vey. Accounts list sand from seven riverbanks, beech ash, copper, cobalt, “manganese the colour of a closed eye,” and twelve pounds of material described as debt glass. No known recipe uses that term.

Ysabet rejected the bishop's proposed Last Judgment. The completed window shows a creation cycle arranged around an empty central oculus: waters, plants, beasts, stars, humanity, angels, and finally twelve figures shielding their faces from something outside the design. The central medallion is not empty in all conditions, although no two inventories agree on its image.

At dedication, witnesses described ordinary late-summer light. The dating era begins here.

The quiet generations (1–82 AFG)

Repair accounts mention oddities without alarm: lead that rang at sunset, glass warm after midnight, two shadows behind scaffolds. A novice's notebook from 31 AFG records, “Sister Mera asked which sun I meant,” then changes subject.

In 56 AFG, an earthquake cracked three lancets around the rose but spared the central masonry. A crew replacing one blue pane suffered identical dreams of a street descending west through the air. The pane was set back in place.

These scattered notes matter because later institutions claimed nothing unusual had preceded the first mass sighting.

The Year of Two Vespers (83 AFG)

On the afternoon of the autumn eclipse, the city crowded into the cathedral to pray. The ordinary sun vanished. While the nave should have darkened, the rose filled with pale violet-white radiance.

Accounts agree on the following:

  • a second disc appeared below and to the left of the eclipsed sun;
  • all bells in the west towers began sounding without visible movement;
  • the congregation cast shadows both east and north;
  • vespers was sung once by the canons and, according to hundreds of witnesses, heard a second time from somewhere above them;
  • seventeen people remembered a bridge across the forecourt that had never been built, including three who drew nearly identical plans;
  • a mason named Perrin Dault climbed the west gallery, touched the central medallion, and disappeared from public record.

The eclipse ended normally. The second light remained for eighty-three minutes. The date became Two Vespers, simultaneously vigil, embarrassment, and feast.

The first response (83–87 AFG)

Bishop Henric declared a “manifestation of superabundant grace” before a council could investigate. Pilgrims arrived within weeks. The chapter sold stamped lead tokens granting a place in the nave at sunset. The city taxed the token booths.

When the phenomenon failed to appear for several large pilgrim gatherings, mockery followed. Henric ordered the rose veiled. Wrong light then appeared beneath the veil, clearer than before, and threw the bishop's shadow onto the ceiling.

The veil was removed. The phrase “superabundant grace” quietly vanished from official sermons.

The age of measures (88–141 AFG)

Canon Orra's tables (91–106 AFG)

Canon Orra Mel compiled the first disciplined record: hour, weather, liturgical calendar, crowd size, panel temperature, shadow direction, and witness reports. Her tables established that the phenomenon was neither simple fraud nor regular astronomy. They also showed that the strongest appearances followed no public prediction.

Orra concluded only: “The light resists becoming an appointment.” Her original tables are sealed in the chapter archive. Copies omit thirteen pages.

The brass noon (113 AFG)

The Guild of Clockmakers erected a brass sighting frame in the nave. During its first test, every plumb line leaned west though the tower remained vertical. Seven clock faces around the city lost the same eleven minutes. The instrument cast no wrong shadow and was dismantled.

The missing eleven minutes remain a common phrase for time wasted in argument.

The first glass riots (126 AFG)

A rumour that powdered rose glass cured river fever caused crowds to attack the glaziers' lodge. Three counterfeit panes were smashed; two apprentices died; no original glass was taken. The guild thereafter stopped publishing complete repair inventories.

The Council of Sufficient Light (141 AFG)

After decades of unstable doctrine, the cathedral adopted a compromise: the one sun was sufficient for nature; the apparent second light was spiritually useful but not a second created body. Questions of mechanism were permitted to licensed scholars. Public preaching that another physical sun existed became an offence.

The phrase Sufficient Light still names official moderation and, in taverns, someone insisting that obvious trouble is under control.

The Unwalling (142–206 AFG)

Mara Venn and the covered passages (149 AFG)

Mara Venn, a widowed plasterer, claimed the Second Sun was visible through a crack in a covered passage three streets west of the cathedral. Neighbours who looked saw only sky, but several observed double shadows on the opposite wall.

Mara began hosting whispered meetings in passages, under bridges, and inside party walls — places neither wholly indoors nor outdoors. Her teaching was practical: “Every wall rehearses the first wall. Learn where walls are unsure.”

Her followers called themselves the Fellowship of the Unwalled Day. Officials called them Mara's Breach.

The Book of Openings (157 AFG)

The Fellowship collected maps of doors, cracks, drains, squints, arrow loops, and broken rooflines where wrong light had appeared. Rather than a scripture, the Book was a changing route through the city, memorized and revised orally. To know a chapter was to be able to walk it.

This is the origin of rotating whispered passphrases: the phrase identified the route revision and proved that the speaker had visited its newest opening.

The Night of White Mortar (171 AFG)

Unknown hands painted hundreds of false windows on blank walls. At dawn, wrong light emerged from seven paintings. The city plasterers were accused; then the heretics; then the chapter itself. Guards whitewashed every painted opening. For months, children drew windows in soot faster than they could be erased.

One preserved false window remains beneath later plaster in Candlewick Court.

The western extraction (188 AFG)

Radical Unwallers attempted to remove the rose's central medallion, believing the tracery imprisoned the hidden day. They reached the gallery with help from a canon and two guild apprentices. Before they touched the glass, the nave filled with absolute ordinary sunlight at midnight. Their ladder shadows pointed up.

All five conspirators surrendered. During trial, each claimed a sixth member had planned the attempt. Their descriptions of the sixth were mutually consistent. No such person could be found.

The covered-passage executions (206 AFG)

Following an assassination blamed on the Fellowship, nine alleged Unwallers were hanged beneath bridges so they would “die between enclosure and sky.” Evidence against four was later shown to have been purchased. Mara Venn's original, mostly nonviolent movement fractured permanently.

Two Vespers became a day of mourning among Occlusionists.

Smoke, fracture, and revision (207–319 AFG)

The west-quarter fire (233 AFG)

Fire crossed projecting upper floors toward the cathedral. Smoke hid the western facade, yet inside the rose remained perfectly visible in an unfamiliar blue sky. Its light illuminated escape routes through the nave. Hundreds survived.

Official teaching calls this the Mercy in Smoke. Unwallers say the false local sky burned thin. Skeptics note that panic testimony is unreliable while quietly admitting no source explains the interior brightness.

After the fire the city widened three lanes. Counter-memories still preserve the old, narrower routes, and wrong-light falls often stop where demolished walls once stood.

The replacement of the Lamb (247 AFG)

A damaged medallion depicting a lamb was replaced with near-perfect glass. For eleven years, the wrong light would not pass through the new pane. When the glazier's daughter scratched a tiny flaw into it, the effect returned.

The guild teaches this as proof that “a window must admit its wound.” Counterfeiters sell intentionally flawed sun-charms on that principle.

Plague of Pale Hands (274–277 AFG)

During plague, families placed the sick in the nave hoping for healing. Some fevers broke; many did not. Prolonged exposure left pale shadow-marks on hands and an epidemic of counter-memory among survivors. People emerged remembering uninfected families and streets without corpse carts.

Riots began when the bereaved tried to enter houses belonging to their alternate kin. The Church ended the vigils. The resulting proclamation is still cited whenever access to the rose is restricted.

The Great Revision (301 AFG)

Provost Salvi ordered civic maps standardized after competing property records caused tax chaos. Forty-seven obsolete street names disappeared. Curiously, ten had appeared only in counter-memory testimony.

Unwallers claim the Revision was an attempt to erase the second city. Archivists counter that it preserved more old names than it destroyed. Both are correct enough to keep arguing.

The silent rose (319 AFG)

For one full year, no documented appearance occurred. Pilgrimage collapsed. Church officials presented the silence as proof that disciplined worship had ended excess. The Unwalled Day declared the hidden sun had moved behind the cathedral in its own sky.

On the anniversary of the silence, every west-facing window in the city produced a second shadow for the length of one bell stroke. The rose itself remained dark.

The era of custody (320–present)

Founding of the Office of Lucent Custody (327 AFG)

The chapter created a permanent bureaucracy to guard the rose, license repairs, record phenomena, manage pilgrims, investigate fraud, and keep public doctrine within tolerable bounds. Its members are the Custodians of Sufficient Light, or “Dousers” in street speech.

The Office's existence turned uncertainty into a profession. It has prevented real vandalism, suppressed useful research, exposed lucrative frauds, preserved irreplaceable glass, and destroyed records. No simple moral summary fits it.

The canal manifestation (348 AFG)

Boatmen beneath Saint Orra's Bridge saw a violet disc in black water at noon. Those who looked up saw only the ordinary sky. A Custodian drained the canal section and found a circular foundation basin directly below the reflection. The report ends mid-sentence.

Boatmen still avoid saying “sun” beneath bridges.

The pilgrimage economy (361–392 AFG)

Licensed smoked lenses, commemorative lead roses, measured viewing cords, guide services, indulgences, and “authentic dust of Two Vespers” transformed the five squares. The Church alternately condemned and licensed the trade. Merchant families funded both doctrinal scholarship and Unwaller safe rooms.

This era fixed the current social hypocrisy: the city officially discourages sensational talk while depending economically on visitors who came for it.

The little conjunction (403 AFG)

For nine seconds, witnesses said the two suns overlapped. Every shadow in the nave vanished. Elsewhere, the city's bells sounded their own pitches backward — an acoustic description no scholar has satisfactorily defined. Nine children born that week later developed identical counter-memories of a garden atop the western towers.

Calculators now seek a greater conjunction. Proposed dates range from tomorrow to three centuries hence.

The present tension

The Second Sun has recently grown brighter, or people have grown less willing to pretend not to see it. Wrong-light falls occur farther from the cathedral. Passage meetings multiply. The Office of Lucent Custody has reopened sealed archives while publicly denying any change. Someone is stealing tiny pieces of lead, not glass, from the rose's outer rings.

For the first time in a generation, three separate calculations predict a conjunction within the same season.

The recurring year

These observances give NPCs shared temporal references.

  • First Glass (late summer): guild procession, window cleaning, crafts fair, and official sermons about works made in humility.
  • Two Vespers (autumn eclipse season): cathedral vigil; Unwaller mourning; merchants sell double-wicked candles; children try to stand on both shadows.
  • White Mortar Night (first winter new moon): householders repair plaster; forbidden soot windows appear by morning.
  • Sufficiency Sunday (midwinter): clergy preach that ordinary light is enough. Taverns extinguish every second lamp in mock observance.
  • Mercy in Smoke (spring): fire brigades bless ladders and buckets; the west quarter sets lanterns inside blue glass.
  • The Pale Hands (variable plague memorial): names of the dead are washed from temporary woad prints in running water.

Dates people use in speech

  • “Before the Mortar” means impossibly long ago or before a secret was covered.
  • “Eleven brass minutes” means time lost to official process.
  • “Since the Silent Year” means longer than expected without resolution.
  • “At second vespers” can mean never, unexpectedly, or when the official event is over and the real one begins.
  • “When the suns agree” means when incompatible demands become reconcilable.
