# Core Lore of Ombreval

> A reusable world foundation extracted from the `second_sun` corpus. This is the
> minimum shared setting that other lore dumps should inherit unless they are
> explicitly alternate continuities. It is intentionally broader and less
> plot-specific than `second_sun/00_canon.md`.

## Preamble: the kind of world this is

The core setting is a **medieval city-state in a realistic-ish world**. Its
centre is **Ombreval**, a free fortified city whose survival depends on walls,
river traffic, stored grain, skilled trades, tolls, markets, and the labour of
ordinary people. Weather delays journeys. Floods move graves. Fire remakes
streets. Disease, hunger, bad harvests, debt, guild rivalry, and distance are
material facts rather than scenery.

The setting's default inhabitants are human and its default causes are natural,
social, or political. Do not assume routine spellcasting, magical species,
healing miracles, convenient prophecy, or modern institutions. A saint's life
may report a miracle and a grandmother may swear to an omen, but belief is not
the same thing as authorial confirmation. Extraordinary claims should usually
remain testimony, doctrine, rumor, coincidence, fraud, or unresolved mystery.

The one fixed rupture in this ordinary world is the **impossible light in the
Great Rose of the Lanthorn**. It is objective and repeatable, but narrowly
bounded and unexplained. It does not turn the setting into a high-magic world.
Other lore dumps may barely mention it. A cloth merchant, boatman, midwife,
thief, mason, magistrate, cook, or foreign carrier has a whole life that is not
about the cathedral.

Religion is culturally and institutionally powerful, but Ombreval is not only
a church with houses around it. The **Candor** shapes oaths, calendars,
education, burial, public language, and parts of the law; it does not exhaust
trade, family, ward loyalties, guild politics, crime, entertainment, or civic
government. The exact secular constitution of the free city is not yet fixed
in core canon. Writers may develop it, but should not casually make the
Praelucent a temporal monarch.

## How to use this core

- Facts stated here are the shared baseline for future lore dumps.
- The present default date is **F.437**, the 437th year of the Fabric era.
- New lore may fill open ground without turning every new subject back toward
  the Candor or the impossible light.
- The detailed Unwalled conspiracy, passwords, named informants, personal
  secrets, and quest tensions belong to the `second_sun` dump unless a later
  work deliberately imports them.
- Omission from this file does not erase the `second_sun` corpus inside its own
  continuity. This file identifies what other writers most need to share.
- Where a nature, miracle, or supernatural cause is described as unresolved,
  no later text should settle it by accident.

This is an **author-facing** reference, not a block every NPC should know in
full. An ordinary citizen knows the city, markets, common calendar, public
Candor teaching, and visible behaviour of the impossible light. They do not
automatically know suppressed experiments, the contents of the Grey Press, or
the reason an institution acted behind closed doors. Sections explicitly
marked “writer-level truth” must be distributed by role and access.

## Ombreval at a glance

**Ombreval** is a free, walled city-state built on the canalized river
**Serle**. It measures roughly 1.2 kilometres west to east and 1.0 kilometre
north to south, also described locally as twelve hundred by a thousand paces.
Its name has long invited jokes about a city named for shadow becoming famous
for light.

The city's oldest named civic ground is an old ford-market. The **Lanthorn**
stands a little west of its centre, with its quire to the east and its
monumental west front facing the **Gradine**. The Serle enters beneath the
western water-wall, crosses the southern third of the city in a straightened
channel called **the Cut**, and leaves through the sluice under the eastern
wall.

Ombreval is dense and irregular. Streets narrow and widen between independently
set facades, dogleg through blocks, pass under houses, and open unexpectedly
into courts and five major squares. Upper storeys are jettied over the street;
eaves nearly meet; covered passages take the names of the buildings above them;
timber bridges join lofts across lanes. Stone, plaster, timber, lead, tile,
smoke, river damp, animal traffic, bells, and hand labour should remain visible
in everyday descriptions.

The city has no established population figure. Do not invent one merely to
make the world sound complete. Its scale should feel walkable, crowded, and
socially legible: a person can cross the walls in a day, while still spending a
lifetime ignorant of another trade's back rooms.

## The Serle and the wider world

The river is older than the city and keeps one name from its source to the sea.
The local rule is simple: **nobody renames water**.

- **The Combs** are the sheep-hills west of Ombreval, where the Serle rises.
- **Brede**, three days' pole upstream, is the head of navigation and the wool-
  stack town. Wool comes downriver to Ombreval's cloth halls; finished cloth
  travels both upriver and down.
- **Harne**, four leagues below Ombreval, is a lord's town with a castle above
  the river. Its lord once chained the Serle and seized the salt boats. “To buy
  peace of Harne” still means paying an extortionate price one cannot refuse.
- **Salorge** lies at the river mouth among salt pans: about six days down with
  the current and twelve back against it. Pan-salt comes upriver in barges.
- **Ostrelle** is the distant primatial city, seat of the Luminary, a great
  school, and the mint that strikes gold lanterns. It is about six weeks away
  by the **Lantern Road**, through two toll-gates. To most Ombreval citizens it
  is a coin, a rank, a letterhead, and a nearly legendary distance.

News and authority travel at the speed of feet, horses, boats, and carried
paper. The blessing of the completed Lanthorn took three years to arrive from
Ostrelle along a road a carrier can cover in six weeks. Ombreval jokes about
the difference between the speed of freight and the speed of grace.

Nothing like the impossible light is established anywhere beyond Ombreval.
The wider world's skies and waters show one sun. Rumors become less accurate
with distance: Harne speaks of “a light in their window and a fine if you name
it”; farther away, people tell grander and falser tales.

## The five squares

The five squares are fixed city anchors. The **Gradine** is important but is
always counted separately from them.

| Square | Position and function | Character and associations |
|---|---|---|
| **The Wickmarket** | West; chandlers' market | Wax, tallow, wicks, honey, hot fat, candle stalls, and the lamplighters' trade. |
| **Coswald's Yard** | North | Stone, timber, lime, scaffolding, and the masons' lodge. Guild pride and old grievances are aired loudly here. |
| **The Tallage** | On the Cut | Customs square: toll-house, weigh-beams, chained standards, pawnshops, and the Tally Bridge. It is a place of clerks, freight, valuation, and careful faces. |
| **Maren's Green** | Downstream, south-east | Fish market, eel smoke, boatmen, the Alder moorings, and the church of Saint Maren at the water's edge. |
| **The Bellstand** | East, behind the Lanthorn's east end | Proclamation square beneath the old watch-bell tower. Edicts are cried and posted here; crowds are summoned by the Scold. |

### The Gradine

The **Gradine** is the Lanthorn's shallow, stepped ceremonial forecourt. It is
the ground of processions, public recantations, licensed pilgrim sellers,
petitions, feast-day crowds, and civic spectacle. It is too small for the whole
city. From outside, the Great Rose appears to be only dark glass and tracery;
the impossible light has never been visible from the steps.

## Established places inside the walls

These names are already attached to places and should be reused consistently.

### Cathedral and river landmarks

- **The Lanthorn**: the common name of the Great Church of Saint Ambrelle.
- **The Great Rose**: the monumental rose window in the Lanthorn's west front.
- **The eye**: the Rose's central round light.
- **The Cut**: the straightened city reach of the Serle.
- **The Tally Bridge**: a stone bridge of two arches at the Tallage, with the
  toll-chain below it.
- **The Eel Bridge**: a low timber footbridge at Maren's Green. Pedestrians must
  wait when a laden barge passes beneath.
- **The sluice**: where the Cut leaves beneath the east water-wall.
- **The Alder moorings**: the boat-family's moorings at Maren's Green.

### Streets, passages, courts, and halls

- **Cinder Row**: the glaziers' street. It burned in F.171 and was rebuilt in
  stone below and timber above. Before the fire it was Glassmakers' Row.
- **The Needle**: Ombreval's narrowest pinch, shoulder-wide, running from the
  Wickmarket back lanes toward the Tallage. “Past the Needle” means beyond
  saving.
- **The Draper's Reach**: the roofed gallery of the cloth halls between
  Coswald's Yard and the Wickmarket. The standard ell of Ombreval is an iron
  rod fixed there.
- **Gaunt Passage**: a blind covered passage under the salt-cellars of the old
  Gaunt house near the Tallage. The Gaunt salt-factor line is extinct; the name
  survived the family.
- **Bellfoot Passage**: the covered way beneath the Bellstand watch-tower stair.
- **Tanners' Slip**: a brine-damp lane behind Maren's Green.
- **Skinners' Court**: a court north of the Gradine, associated with a persistent
  but unproven tale about light from the Rose.
- **The Hungry Ox**: a boatmen's tavern by Maren's Green, home of the song “The
  Water Knows One.”

### Approved names not yet required to occupy a fixed site

The naming handbook supplies the following as in-register examples. A future
writer may establish them as real locations if useful, provided their stated
kind is preserved:

- **Tenterhook Lane** — a trade street by fullers' frames.
- **Burnt Court** — a place renamed after fire.
- **Maren's Slip** — a saint-name attached to a way down to water.
- **Crookneck Lane** and **Eelback Alley** — names from street shape.
- **Malt Passage** — a passage beneath a malt-house at Coswald's Yard.
- **The Chain Bridge** — a working bridge name associated with a harbour chain.
- **The Dry Arch** — an old crossing over a ditch that no longer carries water.

## Work, trade, and ordinary life

Ombreval's economy is not sustained by pilgrimage alone. Its main visible
networks are:

- wool from Brede and the Combs; fulling, tenting, measuring, and cloth trade in
  Ombreval;
- salt from Salorge, brought upriver and stored near the Tallage;
- fish, eels, barges, ferries, and river labour at Maren's Green;
- stone, lime, timber, scaffolding, and generations of unfinished Fabric work
  around Coswald's Yard;
- glassmaking and leading on Cinder Row;
- wax, tallow, wicking, domestic candles, and public lamps at the Wickmarket;
- customs, tolls, weighing, lending, pawning, copying, carrying, food, lodging,
  and small manufacture throughout the city.

Trade bodies matter. The **masons' lodge** at Coswald's Yard and the
**glaziers' guild** of Cinder Row are the best-established examples. A trade can
control apprenticeship, customs, tools, reputation, feast observance, burial,
and collective memory without becoming a modern union or corporation.

The river makes the city prosperous and vulnerable. Flooding reaches streets
and crypts; frost stops boats; a downstream chain can threaten the salt supply.
A realistic plot should remember storage, spoilage, transport time, labour,
season, price, and who owns access to a quay, bridge, oven, warehouse, or
standard weight.

### Money

Ombreval's usual coinage is:

- **spark**: copper; the street normally calls it a **penny**;
- **bell** or **bell-coin**: silver; twelve sparks to one bell;
- **lantern**: gold, struck at Ostrelle; sixty bells to one lantern and mostly a
  money of account in ordinary Ombreval trade.

Useful price anchors in F.437 are two sparks for a loaf, roughly three bells
for a journeyman's day, and eight bells for a pauper's funeral with a knell.
Prices can vary with harvest, flood, scarcity, feast, guild pressure, and the
buyer, but new lore should not treat a lantern as tavern change.

### Measures

- Cloth is measured by the **ell of Ombreval**.
- Canal cargo is measured by the **Serle tun**.
- Salt is measured by the **salt-weigh**.
- Measures are proved against the **Tallage stone** and sealed brass **Gaudry
  weights**, kept chained in the customs square.
- The chandlers' pound runs heavy by charter, “for the wick's sake.”

### Week and daily rhythm

The week has seven days. **Bellday** is first and is the weekly holy day.
**Highmarket** is the third day, chiefly at the Wickmarket and Coswald's Yard.
**Lowmarket** is the sixth, chiefly at the Tallage and Maren's Green. Other days
are commonly counted relative to Bellday rather than given elaborate names.

The Candor's seven offices also tell civic time:

1. **the Watch** — deep night;
2. **the Kindling** — before light;
3. **Dayspring** — sunrise;
4. **High Wick** — noon;
5. **the Waning** — mid-afternoon;
6. **Lamplight** — sunset;
7. **the Snuffing** — curfew.

## The Candor

The formal institution is the **Church of the Sole Light**. In Ombreval almost
everyone calls it **the Candor**, a name that can be respectful, familiar, or
dryly ironic but is not normally a slur.

The Candor teaches that light was the first creature and the only one that
never disobeyed. Its creed is ***Lux una et indivisa***: “one light,
undivided.” Contracts, testimony, verdicts, marriages, and deathbed bequests
are sworn **“by the light that does not lie.”** This makes the Candor part of
the city's legal and moral infrastructure even for citizens whose piety is
lukewarm.

Latin belongs mainly to creeds, inscriptions, and bell mottos. Ordinary clergy
and citizens speak plainly. Do not make every sermon, office, or street name
ornately Latinate.

### Hierarchy

From highest to local:

- The **Luminary**, supreme head of the Candor, sits in primatial Ostrelle. No
  Luminary has visited Ombreval in living memory.
- The **Praelucent of Ombreval** governs the Lanthorn and the city's spiritual
  life. The office is open to women. At elevation a Praelucent takes a liturgical
  lamp-name, though the street continues to use the family name. In F.437 the
  incumbent is **Havise Dorn**, lamp-named **Serene**.
- The **Chapter** consists of three Provosts over the **Canons of the Rose**:
  the **Provost of the Fabric** oversees building works; the **Provost of the
  Rite** oversees liturgy, choir, and calendar; and the
  **Provost-Custodian** heads the Custody of the Eye. In F.437 the
  Provost-Custodian is **Segwin Rasp**.
- **Wick-priests** serve parishes: “each parish a wick of the one flame.”
- Lay and junior offices include **vergers**, **sextons**, and **Tapers**
  (novices). The Custody's clerks wear grey.

The city has parish life beyond the named sacred sites below. Those additional
parishes are open ground. New churches may be added sparingly, with a clear
district, congregation, economy, and patron; they should not all concern the
Great Rose.

### The Custody of the Eye

The **Custody of the Eye** is a grey-clad office seated in the Lanthorn's
chapter house. It was founded in F.289. Publicly it cares for the Great Rose,
orders pilgrim traffic and viewing hours, licenses glass work, keeps records,
and stamps pilgrim badges. Its patron is Saint Belwyn the Unlit, its emblem is
a closed eye in grey wax, and its archive is called the **Grey Press**.

Its public maxim is **“to see is no sin; to affirm is.”** Under the Edict of
F.244, calling the appearance in the Rose a sun, second sun, or green sun is a
citable offense. The mandated word is **the Emblem**. Enforcement normally
means correction, fines, paperwork, or public recantation rather than
spectacular punishment.

Writer-level truth: the Custody possesses centuries of tests proving the
appearance is objective and is no glazier's trick, but it does not know what
the thing is. It is not concealing a solved mystery. It is concealing its
failure to solve one.

## Established churches and sacred sites

These are the sacred buildings with names and sites already fixed inside
Ombreval. No other Ombreval church currently has a canonical name and site in
this core.

### The Great Church of Saint Ambrelle — “the Lanthorn”

The cathedral is formally the **Great Church of Saint Ambrelle** and almost
universally called **the Lanthorn**. The first stone was laid at the old ford-
market in F.1; the Fabric was declared complete in F.398, though scaffolding
still stands on the unfinished crown of the north tower in F.437.

The west front holds the Great Rose. Within are a nine-bay aisled nave, the
crossing, transepts, quire, triforium, clerestory walks, chapels, towers, chapter
house, and the institutional rooms expected of the Candor's chief church in
Ombreval. The west doors open onto the Gradine.

The Lanthorn is simultaneously a place of worship, a building project, a major
employer, a pilgrim destination, a court and archive complex, a civic clock, a
guild grievance, and the largest indoor public space in the city. Scenes there
need not be mystical: they may concern keys, cleaning, choir practice,
scaffolding, funerals, fees, petty theft, copying, contracts, crowd control, or
repairs.

### The Church of Saint Maren of the Reeds

**Saint Maren of the Reeds** stands at the water's edge by Maren's Green. It was
consecrated in F.97 for boatmen and the poor. Its crypt lies low and floods at
high water.

Its riverside **charnel door** has a lintel on which the sexton chalks the given
name of each newly buried soul, leaving it until the next burial. This custom
began after a flood disordered the dead in F.323. The passing-bell **Maren
Smallvoice** rings the **name-knell**, one slow stroke for every year of the
dead person's life. The church is a parish, burial place, riverside refuge,
boatmen's landmark, and institution of poor relief—not merely a satellite of
the cathedral plot.

### The Ilvane Chapel and anchorhold

The **Ilvane Chapel**, dedicated to Saint Ilvane the Twice-Shadowed, was
deconsecrated and mortared shut in F.290. It was officially declared
structurally unsound; at writer level, the Custody also wished to stop the kind
of pilgrimage it attracted.

An **anchorhold** in its north wall has been occupied continuously since F.211.
Canon law forbids sealing a living anchoress into a wall without access, so the
Custody enclosed the chapel around the cell but left its squint open to the
lane. In F.437 the anchoress is **Dame Aldith**, who speaks through the squint,
accepts alms, and trades verses for honest news. The nameless day in early
autumn was once Ilvanestide; the public calendar leaves it blank, while the
anchoress keeps it alone.

## The eight saints of Ombreval

These are saints of the Candor and a shared source for personal names, bells,
feasts, guild customs, shrines, oaths, and local art. Their lives are religious
traditions, not a license to confirm routine supernatural intervention.

1. **Saint Vhairé of the Ford** — ferrywoman and martyr said to have carried a
   lamp dry across the flooded Serle; patron of travellers, ferrymen, the old
   ford, and honest crossings.
2. **Saint Perrin Halfbell** — deaf bellfounder said to have tuned bells by palm
   and jawbone; patron of ringers and founders.
3. **Saint Ambrelle the Glass-Carrier** — a glazier's apprentice said to have
   carried molten glass bare-handed from a burning workshop; patron of
   glaziers and of the Lanthorn.
4. **Saint Maren of the Reeds** — drowned girl whose body was said to have
   floated upstream against the Serle; patron of the drowned, the poor, and
   lost things.
5. **Saint Ilvane the Twice-Shadowed** — hermit whose corpse witnesses said cast
   two shadows; the Candor attributes the second to mourners' candles.
6. **Saint Coswald the Wall-Wright** — mason said to have mortared a siege
   breach with his own body; patron of masons.
7. **Saint Belwyn the Unlit** — blinded after staring at an eclipse and preached
   that darkness too is of the Light; patron of the Custody of the Eye.
8. **Saint Gaudry the Ledgerer** — tax clerk who burned false ledgers on the
   tax-farmer's step and was hanged; patron of clerks, honest weights, and fair
   accounting.

The order above follows the ritual year rather than rank. Saintly patronage
does not erase trade rivalry: the masons have never entirely forgiven the
Lanthorn for giving the whole church to a glazier saint while Coswald received
a side altar.

## The impossible light

The light is part of shared world canon even when a lore dump is about
something else. Its **observable rules** are fixed:

1. A second sunlike disc is visible only in sky seen **through the glass of the
   Great Rose, from inside the Lanthorn**. Other windows show nothing. Outside
   the building there is one sun.
2. A missing pane's open gap shows an ordinary sky. A pane removed from the
   Rose and carried elsewhere shows nothing. New plain glass leaded into the
   Rose shows the disc perfectly. The effect belongs to the Rose in place, not
   to a special piece of old glass.
3. The disc is cold green-white, often compared to dawn seen through river ice.
   It keeps its own colour through every coloured pane. It can be looked at
   directly without pain.
4. It casts visible green-white beams but no heat. Wax does not soften in them.
   It heals nothing, speaks no words, and leaves no physical trace.
5. Within its light, people and objects cast a faint second **grave-shadow** at
   an angle inconsistent with the true sun. The two kinds of light overlap
   without visually mixing.
6. It occupies the same pane for every viewer in the building at a given
   moment, despite their different positions, yet its rays move across the
   floor with the hour. This lack of parallax has no accepted explanation.
7. It moves against the true sun's course: the **countergait**. It is low and
   large in the western Rose at the **dawn-showing**, shares the window with
   the true sun during the late-afternoon **strong hour**, and crosses it once
   daily in **the Passing**, called **the Kiss** in street speech.
8. Once a year, on **the Concurrence**, the Passing centres in the eye from the
   crossing floor and doubled shadows temporarily fuse. If cloud hides the
   true sun, the pale disc remains visible but there is no fusion; the feast
   simply fails.
9. It remains visible through daytime overcast. During lightning every pane may
   flash white except the place where the disc stands, which does not brighten.
10. It never appears in the Serle, rain barrels, or mirrors carried outdoors.
    No moon, star, flame, or other heavenly body doubles.

The Candor calls it **the Emblem**, a granted image of the one Light:
***Una lux, gemina species***, “one light, twofold appearance.” The street calls
it **the Green Sun** despite the Edict. Folk say the cold light belongs to the
dead, speak dead names into its beam, hold newborns in it for a “spare shadow,”
and avoid a grave-shadow on a bride. These practices are sincere; their claimed
effects are never confirmed or mocked by authorial voice.

No document, character, system, or future lore dump should reveal the light's
origin, intention, or ultimate nature. It does not choose, answer, escalate, or
perform. Its force comes from being identical every day in a world otherwise
governed by ordinary causes.

## Bells of Ombreval

A consecrated church bell is named at casting like a baptism. Its normal form
is a saint's name plus a voice-word; its first-person motto is cast into its
waist. The mould is broken, and a recast bell keeps its name. Secular bells are
hired rather than christened.

The Lanthorn's established ring is:

- **Great Ambrelle, called Gravemouth**, the bourdon; motto *SOLI LUCI CANO*,
  “I sing to the sole light”;
- **Coswald Ironthroat**, the alarm;
- **Vhairé Farcall**, for fog and flood; motto *SICCA TRANSIVI*, “dry I
  crossed”;
- **Perrin Evenblow**, which marks the hours;
- **Gaudry Truetongue**, the court bell; motto *PONDUS IUSTUM*, “just weight”;
- **the Orphan**, whose baptismal name was lost in a sacristy fire. Folk distrust
  an unnamed bell and avoid weddings under its voice.

Outside that ring, **Maren Smallvoice** is Saint Maren's passing-bell. **The
Scold** is the unhallowed secular watch-bell at the Bellstand; it rings the
Snuffing curfew and calls people to proclamations. **The Ruin** is not one bell
but the alarm pattern of the ring sounded backward, greatest bell first, for
fire or flood. Everyone who hears it is expected to drop ordinary work.

Approved bell-forming elements include *-mouth, -voice, -throat, -tongue,
-call, -blow, -cry,* and *-tone*. **Ambrelle Clearcall** and **Belwyn Stillcry**
are in-register examples not yet required as fixed bells.

## Calendar

Years are counted in the **Fabric era**: F.1 is the laying of the Lanthorn's
first stone. The year-number turns at Vhairestide. Dates are normally located
by feast rather than numbered month, for example, “the third day after
Coswaldstide, F.436.”

The ritual and civic year, from spring:

- **Colm's Night** — unofficial, early spring; green-dipped candles are burned
  at home for the drowned labourer Colm Attergate.
- **Vhairestide** — spring; first quarter-feast, blessing of the first boats,
  and turn of the year.
- **Perrinstide** — late spring; bells washed and greased, with alms for the
  deaf.
- **Feast of Saint Ambrelle / Ambrellestide** — early summer; second quarter-
  feast, with glass carried from Cinder Row to the Gradine.
- **Marenstide** — late summer; eel fair and remembrance of the drowned.
- **The nameless day** — early autumn; formerly Ilvanestide.
- **Coswaldstide** — autumn; third quarter-feast and blessing of scaffolds.
- **The Concurrence / Feast of the Sole Light** — the fortieth day after
  Coswaldstide.
- **Wet Alms** — winter; fourth quarter-feast, with bread at the Lanthorn door.
- **Belwyn's Dark** — midwinter; the year's one office sung without lights.
- **Gaudry's Audit / Weighday** — late winter; standards are proved and ledgers
  closed.

Do not turn the calendar into a list of magical effects. Feasts regulate work,
contracts, fairs, eating, processions, maintenance, alms, travel, and memory as
much as doctrine.

## Historical spine

This is the minimum chronology useful across lore dumps. More detailed annals
remain in `second_sun/04_chronicle_of_the_city.md`.

- **F.1** — First stone of the Lanthorn laid at the old ford-market on Saint
  Vhairé's ground.
- **F.22** — The sweating death strikes Ombreval; the ford-quarter dead are
  buried together among the reeds.
- **F.38** — The Serle freezes; wolves cross the ice into the shambles.
- **F.54** — The lord of Harne chains the river and seizes salt boats; Ombreval
  buys peace for four hundred marks.
- **F.83–90** — The Serle is straightened through the city to form the Cut. The
  Tallage is established and the first Tally Bridge built.
- **F.97** — Saint Maren's church is consecrated for boatmen and the poor.
- **F.132** — A scaffold collapse kills seven masons and a boy; the blessing of
  scaffolds at Coswaldstide begins.
- **F.157** — Fire burns the tanneries and riverside reeds.
- **F.171** — The glaziers' street burns and is rebuilt as Cinder Row.
- **F.183** — Two rye failures bring severe hunger; the Chapter opens its
  granary late enough to be remembered.
- **F.198–203** — Idonea Sparr makes the Great Rose. On seeing it complete from
  the crossing, she weeps and says she has made two of something there was one
  of. She never fires glass again.
- **F.219** — First surviving public trace of the impossible light: “the sky
  doubled behind the rose.”
- **F.229** — A great freeze stops the Cut; an ox is roasted on the ice at the
  Tallage while boatmen lose their work.
- **F.240–244** — Pilgrim traffic surges; the Concurrence feast is established;
  the Edict fixes **Emblem** as lawful vocabulary.
- **F.247** — The Tally Bridge is rebuilt in stone.
- **F.251–252** — Child-pox is followed by the casting of Maren Smallvoice and
  the beginning of the name-knell.
- **F.263** — The cloth-hall gallery is roofed and named the Draper's Reach.
- **F.288–290** — The Glaziers' Trial fails to prove sorcery; its verdict is
  sealed, the Custody is founded, and the Ilvane Chapel is shut.
- **F.301** — Colm Attergate preaches that the Church is walling a sun out of
  the sky. Banished, he drowns returning through the Serle sluice.
- **F.309–312** — Gravemouth and the lesser bells are cast; the glass procession
  from Cinder Row becomes an annual custom.
- **F.323** — A flood disorders Saint Maren's crypt; the charnel-door chalking
  custom begins.
- **F.341** — A sacristy fire destroys one bell's recorded name; it becomes the
  Orphan.
- **F.362** — The Great Rains bring three weeks of overcast and flooding. Wet
  Alms begins in their aftermath.
- **F.371** — Encroaching rebuilt houses narrow a weavers' lane into the Needle.
- **F.390–398** — The north tower crown is scaffolded; the Fabric is declared
  complete, but the scaffold and “little that remained” stay.
- **F.401** — Ostrelle's delayed blessing of the completed Fabric arrives.
- **F.407** — A tallow fire burns six Wickmarket stalls.
- **F.415** — Hail damages the cloth halls; the Draper's Reach keeps the wool
  dry.
- **F.436** — During the Concurrence the Chapter bars the Lanthorn doors; a
  crowd crush on the Gradine causes injuries. The event is called the Shut
  Door.
- **F.437** — Present year. The rye stands well, the Serle remains in its bed,
  ravens nest in the north-tower scaffold, and the city waits to see what the
  next Concurrence brings.

## Naming language

Names should sound as if they grew from use, work, damage, geography, devotion,
or an old joke. The setting's church vocabulary uses light and fabric
metaphors; its secular vocabulary is usually practical. Do not spread
*lantern*, *wick*, *light*, and *candor* into every unrelated tavern and street
name merely because they are prominent in the cathedral material.

### People

A citizen normally has a given name and a byname. Bynames arise from trade,
street, origin, physical feature, or an event. They are earned or inherited,
not self-selected. Families of standing have fixed surnames. Women keep their
own bynames at marriage. The poor may carry only a short given name.

Established or approved given-name bank:

- men: **Aubin, Colm, Corin, Dunstan, Grigor, Hamel, Jos, Noll, Segwin, Ansel,
  Bertran, Ewart, Gile, Renn, Tobin**;
- women: **Aldith, Betriss, Ede, Havise, Idonea, Jonet, Lise, Osanne, Averil,
  Clemence, Petronel, Rohese, Sibbe**;
- short names: **Pin, Ib, Cobb, Sef, Dob, Mote, Nan, Tib**.

Established family names include **Sparr, Copp, Stott, Dorn, Alder, Ashe,
Marle, Fitch, Pike, Brant, Vell, Rasp,** and **Ferrant**. New fixed family names
should usually be short and consonant-heavy; approved examples are **Crake,
Hobbe, Skell,** and **Tarn**.

In-register constructions:

- **Gile Salter**, **Petronel Roper** — trade;
- **Ede of the Needle**, **Renn of the Bellstand** — place;
- **Clemence of Ostrelle**, **Tobin Upstream** — origin;
- **Cobb Threefinger**, **Sef the Long** — feature;
- **Averil Wetalms**, **Bertran Scaffold** — event;
- **Wyn Alder**, **Sibbe Crake** — fixed family.

Origin bynames invite questions. A person called “of Ostrelle” should have a
reason to be six weeks from home.

### Streets and structures

- Streets take names from trades, damage, saints, or shape—not living people.
- A street is normally renamed only after a calamity or physical change strong
  enough to defeat its old name.
- A covered passage takes the name of what it passes beneath.
- Bridges take working names from toll, cargo, or keeping, never saint names. A
  bridge belongs half to the water, and nobody renames water.
- Formal institutional names can be elevated; spoken names contract toward the
  practical: Great Church of Saint Ambrelle becomes the Lanthorn, Feast of
  Saint Ambrelle becomes Ambrellestide.

### Tone guardrails

- Prefer compact medieval vernacular over ornate generic-fantasy names.
- Use Latin rarely and purposefully.
- Let names carry old labour and local memory: a burnt street, a low bridge, a
  toll, a crooked lane, a family whose building outlasted it.
- Saints belong naturally to churches, feast days, altars, and old lanes; they
  should not be the source of every shop sign.
- Preserve the accents and spellings of established names, especially
  **Vhairé**, **Ombreval**, **Serle**, and **Ostrelle**.

## Shared sayings and customs

These small pieces make unrelated lore dumps sound as if they happen in the
same city:

- **“Past the Needle.”** Beyond saving.
- **“Sold at the Tallage.”** Betrayed, at a fair price.
- **“To buy peace of Harne.”** To pay an extortionate price one cannot refuse.
- **“By the light that does not lie.”** The lawful oath.
- **“One shadow to your road.”** A blessing at doors and moorings. Answer:
  **“And one light over it.”** It is not said inside the Lanthorn.
- **“Peace with the impossible, and dinner at noon.”** Stop arguing and get on
  with the day.
- **Belwyn's ward:** close one eye and lay two fingers on the lid. Clergy call it
  remembrance that darkness belongs to the Light; ordinary people use it
  against ill luck.
- Lamplighters whistle a three-note wick-call so people know the figure on the
  ladder is lawful. One public lamp is left dark each night as Belwyn's lamp.
- A Cinder Row apprentice swears articles on the lead, not the glass: “glass
  shows; lead holds.” Burn scars are called Ambrelle's kisses.
- Boatmen insist that the water knows one sun and state it with the same calm as
  a depth sounding.

## Open ground for future lore dumps

The following subjects are deliberately available for development:

- the secular charter, councils, courts, watch, taxation, and balance between
  civic and ecclesiastical authority;
- unnamed gates, towers, wards, quays, residential courts, alleys, bridges,
  wells, granaries, bathhouses, hospitals, schools, taverns, and workshops;
- parish churches beyond Saint Maren's, provided their sites and congregations
  do not overwrite the three established sacred places;
- guilds and trades beyond the masons and glaziers;
- nearby farms, mills, villages, common lands, and the food supply that a dense
  walled city requires;
- family histories, neighborhood feuds, crime, romance, seasonal labour,
  medicine, entertainment, cooking, childhood, migration, and river life;
- regions beyond Brede, Harne, Salorge, the Combs, and Ostrelle, with travel
  times and information limits respected.

Good additions connect to material life. A new feast should close shops or
change food prices. A new bridge needs keepers, repair, and a reason for its
name. A guild needs inputs, customers, rivals, and waste. A district needs
water, work, noise, and a route to market. A church needs a congregation and a
means of support. This is how new lore remains medieval, local, and coherent
without becoming another variation on the same cathedral plot.

## Compact reusable setting block

Ombreval is a free fortified medieval city-state, roughly 1.2 by 1.0 km, on the
river Serle. The canalized city reach is the Cut. Wool comes down from Brede;
salt comes up from Salorge; Harne lies four leagues downstream; distant
Ostrelle is the seat of the Candor's Luminary. Ombreval's five squares are the
Wickmarket, Coswald's Yard, the Tallage, Maren's Green, and the Bellstand. The
Gradine, ceremonial forecourt of the cathedral, is counted apart.

The cathedral is formally the Great Church of Saint Ambrelle and universally
called the Lanthorn. Its western Great Rose contains an impossible visual fact:
from inside the church, and only through its glass, a cold green-white second
sunlike disc is visible. It casts a second shadow but no heat, disappears at
the threshold, and has no known cause. The Church of the Sole Light—the
Candor—calls it the Emblem and teaches *Lux una et indivisa*, one light
undivided. No story should finally explain it.

Ombreval is otherwise realistic and materially medieval. Most people are
occupied by freight, food, weather, tools, wages, kin, debt, guilds, markets,
burial, and neighborhood reputation. Religion shapes civic life but is not the
only subject worth writing. Names come from trade, damage, shape, origin,
saints, and old use; waters keep their names; news travels only as fast as a
person or boat can carry it. The present year is F.437.
