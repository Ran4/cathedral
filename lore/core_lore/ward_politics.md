# Ward Politics

> Author-facing canon for the everyday political life of the eight tax wards:
> what each ward believes about itself, what its neighbours say instead, what
> it presently wants, and who sits for it in F.437. The constitution — seats,
> quorum, officers, courts — is in `secular_government.md` and is not repeated
> here. The Reed Ward's politics are worked out in full in
> `../the_dry_boatmen.md`; the water disputes referenced below are law in
> `../wells_and_water.md`.

## Ward before opinion

Ombreval has no parties and no ideologies with names. It has wards. Ask a
householder what they think of a cart fee and they will answer with where they
live, what they carry for a living, whom they owe, and whom they married — and
the answer will be the one their neighbours give, because that is what a ward
is: an economy with a voice. An NPC's politics should be derivable from ward,
trade, household, and creditors, and should never need a label.

Every ward keeps a reputation it calls a virtue. Its neighbours repeat the
same reputation as an insult, often word for word, and both are describing the
same behaviour. Nobody in the city finds this strange. A householder says
their own ward's line first and laughs; the same line from a stranger's mouth
is a different thing entirely.

## The reckonings

Each tax ward reckons four times a year, in the open, in its own customary
place, in the days after Vhairestide, Ambrellestide, Coswaldstide, and
Gaudry's Audit. The Chest's expenditure is read aloud, the ward's complaints
are stated, and the standing quarrels are rehearsed — the same quarrels,
mostly, at every reckoning, which is not a failure of the institution but the
institution itself.

The reckoning after Gaudry's Audit is the **head reckoning**: with the year's
ledgers freshly proved against the Tallage weights, the resident householders
choose the ward's two benchers for the coming year. The chosen take their
seats when the year turns at Vhairestide.

The customary places:

| Ward | Reckons at |
|---|---|
| Fabric | Skinners' Court — the Gradine is the Church's ground, which is the point |
| Wick | the Wickmarket |
| Cloth | under the Draper's Reach — the one roofed reckoning in the city, and resented for it |
| Wallwright | Coswald's Yard |
| Cinder | Burnt Court |
| Weigh | the Tallage |
| Reed | Maren's Green |
| Bell-and-Sluice | the Bellstand |

A reckoning is the city's secular assembly and belongs to the Common Bench,
not the Church. No blessing opens it, no bell but the ordinary offices times
it, and a wick-priest attends his own ward's reckoning as a householder or not
at all. The Candor has its processions; the Bench has its reckonings; the city
is generally glad they are not the same thing.

## Standing for the Bench

In Ombreval one *sits* for the Bench. At the head reckoning each ward sets out
its **reckoning bench** — plain common wood, backless, unpainted, repaired but
never replaced — and whoever would be bencher sits on it while the
householders stand and state their complaints to the sitter's face: the well
rope, the fouled drain, the licence that took a season, the ward hand who was
somewhere else. Custom allows the sitter to ask *what would mend it?* and
nothing more. Answering a complaint from the bench is fatal — the answer is
the year of service, and everyone knows a man who defends himself on the
bench will spend his seat defending himself at the Bench. The custom gives
"standing for the Bench" its appropriately literal inconvenience, and the
city's word for seeking office is simply **to sit the bench**.

Two of the benches are famous. Reed's is a thwart sawn out of a broken-up
hull, and boat-family candidates touch it like a relic. Wallwright's is stone,
and the other seven wards consider this the most Wallwright thing that has
ever happened.

Support is sought the way everything in Ombreval is sought: through meals,
favours, kin, credit, guild pressure, and visible service. A candidate does
not orate — the Sitting is the only public performance, and it is a
performance of listening. What actually gathers votes is a wall shored before
it was ordered, a debt carried one more quarter, muster drills attended in
person, a well assessment paid early and seen to be paid, a marriage that
joins two streets, and the quiet arithmetic of who holds whose paper. The
guilds organise votes and lend expertise conspicuously, but hold no seats by
right, and a lodge that pushes too openly loses more householders than it
delivers.

The lawful limit of a candidate's table is **bench-fare**: bread, herring,
and small ale. Nobody starves at a reckoning and nobody is bought at one —
that is the theory, and the herring is its unit of measure. *He fed the ward
past herring* is the standing accusation of vote-buying, and an actual bribed
reckoning is an offence even when the payment is disguised as a guild gift
(`secular_government.md`).

Two customs of the title. First, it outlives the year: once returned, the
street says *bencher* to your face until you are buried, whatever the rolls
say — so the city is full of benchers, most of whom hold no seat, some of
whom the Bench still sends out as **viewers** to look at a nuisance, a party
wall, or a boundary before the Measure Court hears it. Second, wards like
their arithmetic visible: it is common, though nowhere required, for a ward to
return one name of standing and one byname — the ledger and the barrow,
elected together. Reed does it deliberately; most wards do it without
noticing.

## The eight wards

Each ward below: the virtue it claims, the insult its neighbours make of it,
its present grievances, and its benchers of F.437. The Reed pair and the First
Seat have full character sheets (`../characters/civic_officer/`); the twelve
benchers named for the other wards are established here for the first time and
are open to be developed.

### Fabric — the cathedral quarter

**Claims:** *the city's porch.* Fabric keeps the Lanthorn's peace, lodges the
pilgrims, feeds the works, and hosts the one industry that never stops hiring.
**Neighbours say:** Fabric sweeps the Church's steps and calls it a trade —
and reckons light while spending loud, since half its ground is consecrated
and consecrated ground pays no ward tax.

**Grievances.** The tax gap is the sore: pilgrim traffic wears civic streets,
wells, and drains, and the ground that draws the pilgrims pays nothing toward
any of it. Fabric wants the Chapter to pay a yearly composition for street
wear — a thing the Bench cannot order and can only negotiate, jurisdiction
being what it is. Second, lodging: the Hammering left the quarter full of
empty upper rooms, and unlicensed pilgrim beds in them undercut every
licensed keeper at each feast.

**Benchers, F.437:** **Segwin Hobbe**, a carter to the Fabric works whose
trade eats from the Chapter's hand, so the ward's tax quarrel rides on his own
wagon and everyone watches how he votes; and **Clemence Pike**, who keeps a
licensed pilgrim lodging off the Gradine and wars on the unlicensed beds — the
ward likes that a woman of the cathedral's own servant-family quarrels with
the Chapter anyway.

### Wick — the light quarter

**Claims:** *first lit, last dark.* The city sees by Wick: wax, tallow, wicks,
lamps, and honest light honestly weighed.
**Neighbours say:** the fat ward — Wick keeps its fortune in its rafters and
its neighbours in kindling. The stall fire of F.407 is remembered against it
by people who were not born then.

**Grievances.** Cinder's proposed furnace and render-house rules would fall
hardest on Wick, whose renderers need fire under fat all day; pushed too far,
the rules push rendering outside the walls and the trade with it. And the
Slate Cistern's fire reserve: a third of the ward's own stored rain sits
sealed under the ward hand's clay while households queue — Wick wants the
sealed line lowered, Cinder wants it raised, and the queue at Slate Cistern
has opinions about both (`../wells_and_water.md`).

**Benchers, F.437:** **Jonet Kett**, a market-food householder who has sold at
the Wickmarket since she could count and knows every stall licence by heart;
and **Gile Bram**, an out-of-service soldier with a cookshop, returned because
he drills his muster file in person and because a man who cooks over fire all
day can say so when the furnace rules come up.

### Cloth — the credit quarter

**Claims:** *our word is money.* A Cloth mark is good from Brede to Salorge;
the ward's ledgers are the city's honest paper.
**Neighbours say:** two prices and a promise — a Cloth man will lend you your
own coat and charge you interest on the sleeves.

**Grievances.** The proposed cart fee on the Cut cartway (see the Drain
Question below) would toll every bale twice, coming and going — *to lift it
twice* made into law. And Tenter Cistern: the ward is assessed for the bulk
its fullers and dyers draw, and accused in the same breath over foul return
water in the drains; Cloth's position is that its dye-water is the pretext by
which everyone else means to make Cloth pay for everyone's culverts.

**Benchers, F.437:** **Averil Nett**, a garment-mistress who speaks for the
cloth halls and is exactly as honest as her house's reputation, which is the
ward's proudest exhibit; and **Jos Mere**, a victualler and carrier whose
family's paper is famously tidier than its loads — the ward returns him
because a man who dislikes scrutiny of carts fights cart fees with perfect
sincerity.

### Wallwright — the wall quarter

**Claims:** *the wall stands because we do.* Stone, lime, timber, and the
muster rolls; when the Ruin rings backward, you will all be glad of us.
**Neighbours say:** Wallwright reckons high and dry — built on the one ground
that drains itself, and never yet paid for anything that runs downhill.

**Grievances.** The drain assessment, above all: Wallwright's standing answer
is *water runs downhill; so does money* — let the streets that use the
culverts pay for them. And Lodge Well: the lodge keeps the windlass and calls
the shaft civic; the Bench pays for the deep lining and calls the windlass a
lodge tool; the well-head repair now due sits exactly on the crack between
those two positions (`../wells_and_water.md`).

**Benchers, F.437:** **Ewart Toll**, a lime-burner whose kilns feed the lodge
without belonging to it — the masons wanted a Stott and got the man who sells
them lime instead; and **Bertran Crake**, who went downriver in the Long
Departure and came back up, and carries a Hammering claim of his own on a
half-owned ruin, which makes him the one bencher who argues shoring orders
from experience on both sides.

### Cinder — the burned quarter

**Claims:** *burned once.* Cinder Row burned in F.171 and never twice since:
covered lamps, swept flues, the fire-watch roll, and the discipline the rest
of the city borrows the morning after every scare.
**Neighbours say:** the scolds — a Cinder nose finds smoke in your soup, and
the ward would license your hearth and inspect your candle if you let it.

**Grievances.** Cinder demands stricter furnace and cistern rules citywide —
sealed fire reserves in every ward's cistern on the Slate model, furnace and
render-house licences actually inspected — because fire travels and Cinder is
where it has already been. It presses hardest against Wick, which is where
the tallow is. Second, Three-Curb: the shared well-head is moving, three
courts' records make magnificent ambiguity about who owes what, and Cinder
wants the Bench to order the masonry first and apportion afterward — which
the other wards call jumping the queue with a bucket in each hand.

**Benchers, F.437:** **Petronel of the Row**, a furnace-widow who keeps the
fire-watch roll and has stood every watch she has ever posted; and
**Dunstan Hook**, bynamed for the fire-hook he has carried to every alarm
since the Hammering — his platform *is* the byname, and he has never needed
another.

### Weigh — the weighing quarter

**Claims:** *the weight is the weight.* The city's standards, tolls, and
sealed measures are kept here, and kept exactly; Weigh is hated because it
counts, and the count is right.
**Neighbours say:** *sold at the Tallage* — the small-fraud ward, where the
ale is watered, the coin is clipped, and everything has two prices, one of
them for you.

**Grievances.** The Reed Postern, first and always: every handbarrow that
passes it untolled is revenue the River Gate collected from somebody honest,
and Weigh votes for a barrow toll and shorter hours at every asking. And the
Serle tun: Reed's standing accusation that the tun has crept heavier stains
the whole ward's honour, and Weigh is split between demanding the Bench prove
the tun publicly once and for all, and dreading it — because if the boatmen
are right, sixty years of tolls were wrong, and no one has the arithmetic for
what follows.

**Benchers, F.437:** **Rohese Sedge**, a grocer who has spent two seats being
scrupulously, conspicuously exact in the city's least-trusted trade — the
Sedges' rehabilitation, one true measure at a time; and **Aubin Wren**, the
balance-maker whose beams and weights the Tallage itself buys — the man who
made the weights, defending the weights, who must stand aside whenever the tun
is spoken of, which, Reed says, is exactly why Weigh keeps returning him.

### Reed — the water quarter

**Claims:** *we carry.* Since the diversion the city eats off Reed's backs —
the dry carry, the fish, the funerals, and every load walked up from the
wharves.
**Neighbours say:** everything in Reed *comes by barrow* — the ward with a
thieves' door in the wall and a civic argument for keeping it open.

**Grievances.** The postern: hours long, toll off handcarts, and open before
the Kindling on Lowmarket so the fish reaches Maren's Green by Dayspring — the
*poor man's door*, defended at every reckoning by people who know precisely
what else walks through it. The Serle tun, which the ward has watched proved
at every Gaudry's Audit for sixty years and never once believed. And the
wharf-born: whether a child born in a shed on the outer strip is a resident,
owes ward tax, or may one day sit the bench — a question the Bench has never
ruled on, because any ruling creates either untaxed residents or taxed
outsiders, and which this file, like every other, leaves exactly where it
found it.

**Benchers, F.437:** **Idonea Tarn** and **Hamel of the Reach** — the ledger
and the tally, one name of standing and one byname, the ward's arithmetic,
elected. Both are worked out in `../the_dry_boatmen.md` and carry full
character sheets.

### Bell-and-Sluice — the mixing bowl

**Claims:** *everyone's neighbour.* Every family in the city keeps a branch on
the Bell-and-Sluice streets; the Bellstand is where Ombreval is told what it
is, and the ward hears everything first because everyone lives there second.
**Neighbours say:** nobody's ward — a lodging-house with a bell on it.

**Grievances.** The seats, before anything: one ward with two old names, the
biggest and poorest in the city, holds two benchers like tiny Cinder — and its
standing petition to be reckoned as Bell *and* Sluice again, four seats where
two now sit, is treated by every other ward as a raid on the Bench's
arithmetic and fails at every asking, which does not stop the asking. Second,
Step Cistern: three locked openings and a keeper paid too little to guard any
of them; the ward wants the keeper's pay carried by the Chest, and the Chest
observes that a ward pays its own keeper (`../wells_and_water.md`).

**Benchers, F.437:** **Gude Dask**, a drover's daughter of twenty, who is also
this year's First Seat (below); and a **Marle** — the seat is real, the name
is deliberately not given here, and what it costs the house to keep that seat
scrupulously, visibly clean is told in `../families/family_marle.md`.

## The Drain Question, F.437

The city's live dispute, and the reason this year's Bench is what it is. The
Great Rains broke the culverts of the low streets and the Hammering's repairs
were never finished; cellars in Reed and along the Bell-and-Sluice flood in
every hard rain, and the Common Chest cannot carry the repair alone. Three
ways to pay have been proposed, and each is blocked by the ward it lands on:

1. a **city-wide assessment** — blocked by Wallwright (*water runs downhill;
   so does money*);
2. a **cart fee on the Cut cartway** — blocked by Cloth (*to lift it twice*);
3. a **barrow toll at the Reed Postern** — blocked by Reed (*the poor man's
   door*), and backed loudly by Weigh, which is half the reason Reed can block
   it.

Cinder will vote for anyone's plan that buys sealed fire reserves into the
bargain; Fabric offers sympathy and the Chapter's exemption, which is to say
nothing, which is the point everyone else makes about Fabric. Nine agreeing
votes make a rule, and nobody has nine. Helping any one ward out of this
disadvantages another by construction — that is not a flaw in the Question,
it is the Question.

## The First Seat, F.437

The Drain Question deadlocked the choosing of the First Seat: every bencher of
weight was some ward's enemy on it. The Bench did what benches do and chose
the one member who had made no enemies worth naming — **Gude Dask**, twenty
years old, of a Bell-and-Sluice droving family so plain it is famous for
nothing, who remembers agreements and faces and arranges the docket without
owning a position anyone can find. Half the city calls her a placeholder. The
other half has noticed that the docket is where the Drain Question lives, and
that the placeholder decides what comes up when.

## What this file does not settle

- **The Serle tun is never proved or disproved.** The proving happens every
  Gaudry's Audit; the Reed Ward always leaves unsatisfied; whether the tun is
  honest stays open, per `../the_dry_boatmen.md`.
- **The wharf-born question is never ruled.** Per `secular_government.md` and
  `../the_dry_boatmen.md`.
- **The Drain Question is not resolved here.** It is the standing dispute of
  F.437 and belongs to play, not to this file.
- **Benchers past F.437 are open.** Seats turn over yearly; three consecutive
  years is the limit; nothing here fixes any future reckoning.
- **The new benchers are seeds.** The twelve named here for the first time
  have a ward, a trade, and a position each, and no more; character sheets on
  the Reed pair's model may follow.

## The wards in one breath — safe common talk

Any citizen may say any of these; every citizen has heard all of them.

- **Fabric** sweeps the Church's steps and calls it a trade.
- **Wick** keeps its fortune in its rafters and its neighbours in kindling.
- **Cloth** pays in promises and lends you your own coat.
- **Wallwright** reckons high and dry, and has never paid for anything that
  runs downhill.
- **Cinder** smells smoke in your soup.
- In **Weigh** you are sold at a fair price.
- Everything in **Reed** comes by barrow.
- **Bell-and-Sluice** is nobody's ward — a lodging-house with a bell on it.
