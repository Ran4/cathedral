# The Dry Boatmen

> The Reed Ward, the Alder Moorings, and the boat-families of a river that was
> moved outside the wall. Author-facing. Load with `core_lore/places.md` and
> `wells_and_water.md` when writing anything in Maren's Green.

## The thing to get right first

**The boatmen are not out of work.** This is the mistake every writer makes on
first contact with the phrase *dry boatmen*, and it is wrong.

The Serle still runs. Ewart Alder still poles three days up to Brede against the
current and comes back down in a day and a half with wool to the gunwales. Wyn
Alder is on the water before the Kindling bell. Boats are built, hired, holed,
patched, lost, inherited, and fought over exactly as they were before F.362.

What went dry was not the trade. It was the **neighbourhood**.

Between F.363 and F.369 the Common Bench closed the ruined Cut and turned the
Serle into its present bed beyond the south wall. The river left, and left
everything behind: the warehouses, the hoists, the water stairs, the counting
rooms, the rope racks, the tavern, the church, the churchyard, the guild custom,
the songs, and the families. The boat quarter is still the boat quarter. It is
simply a boat quarter that a man must walk out of, through a gate, and down a
cart road before he can touch a boat.

Everything in this document follows from that one fact: **the water is outside
and the household is inside, and the wall runs between them.** The Reed Ward is
not a ruin. It is a working quarter with its heart on the wrong side of a gate.

## The wound, stated plainly

Before F.362, a boat came up the Cut and stopped at a door. The Alder Moorings
had a water gate; cargo came off the deck, onto the hoist, and into the loft, and
the same family did all three motions. One lift, one household, one profit.

In F.437 the same basket of eels is:

1. landed at the outer wharf by the boat crew;
2. carried up the bank by wharf hands, who are paid;
3. loaded on a cart or handbarrow by porters, who are paid;
4. brought through the River Gate, where it is **tolled**;
5. hauled up the River Cartway to the Cut junction;
6. carted east to Maren's Green;
7. lifted a second time, into a dry warehouse whose hoist was built to take
   things off a boat that will never come.

The boat-families call the whole business **the dry carry**, and they are not
being poetic — it is a line in a ledger. Every load in Ombreval is now lifted
twice, and *the second lift belongs to someone else*. Carters, porters, freight
brokers, gate clerks and toll interests eat the difference. The families who
owned the water end of the work now own only the water end of the work.

The wells doc puts the ward's grievance in its coldest form, and boatmen repeat
it almost word for word: **the city made the Serle a cart journey away, and then
treated the cost of carrying as a private inconvenience.**

## Gate-time

The river has no hours. The gate does.

A boat-family's day is not ruled by tide, current or light. It is ruled by the
**River Gate** and the **Reed Postern**, which open in the morning and shut at
the Snuffing, and by the customs shelter, which keeps its own idea of when a
clerk is available. The old boatman's dawn — out before the Kindling bell — is
now a dawn spent standing at a gate waiting for a man with a key.

Consequences that shape every scene set in the Reed Ward:

- **Gate-caught.** A crew that misses the Snuffing sleeps outside the wall. This
  happens constantly: bad water, a broken pole, a late tally at the wharf, a
  clerk who went to eat. A gate-caught man is not in danger. He is simply *not
  home*, and his household eats without him, again.
- **The outlodge.** Because being gate-caught is normal, boat-families keep beds
  outside: a shed, a half-loft over a wharf shack, a bench in an unlicensed
  drinking hut on the strip. The Bench cannot easily regulate the strip, because
  the strip is outside. The Church dislikes it. Everyone uses it. Old boatmen
  spend more nights out there than in the houses their grandmothers keep.
- **The split household.** In many Reed Ward families the men are half-resident
  outside the wall and the women, children and old are wholly resident inside.
  A wife runs the yard, the ledger, the price, the credit and the funeral; her
  husband knows the river and comes home smelling of it. This is the ordinary
  shape of a boat-family, and it is why the Reed Ward's real power is so often
  a woman at a table with a book.
- **Born inside.** A boat-family child who has never been out to see the water.
  Said as a light insult and heard as a heavy one. There are people of thirty on
  Maren's Green who have seen the Serle once — at Vhairestide, in a crowd.

## The houses

The Reed Ward's boat-families are not one interest. The diversion forced every
household to answer the same question — *do we keep the boat?* — and the answers
have hardened, over three generations, into something close to a class system.
An NPC's family name is a position in that argument.

### The Alders — kept the boat

The last boat-owning house of standing on the Green. They hold the **Alder
Moorings**, a dry warehouse court with a broad cart gate, timber galleries,
hoists, rope racks, canvas repair, a rough ledger table, and no water at all.
They kept the name after the river left because the business and the memory
remained. Handcarts are tied up in a yard whose iron rings were forged for boats.

They are proud, cash-poor, land-rich, and quietly coming apart:

- **Wyn Alder** (34) is the best boatwoman on the Serle and has been since she
  took the fore-pole at twelve, in the season after the Moorings roof came down
  in the Hammering and killed her father.

- **Ewart Alder** (49), her elder half-brother, poles the Brede run on a finished pair of
  shoulders and tells the whole Hungry Ox in drink that the yard should be sold
  to a Tallage broker while it still fetches a price — that the water left it
  seventy years ago and three generations of Alders have been too stubborn to
  notice. By morning he is ashamed and never takes it back.
- **Tobin Alder** (17) takes the fore-pole, never complains, and wants the eel
  smoke-sheds — a trade that stays still under him.
- **Rohese of the Sluice** (61), Wyn's mother, keeps the dry gatehouse at the
  Old Sluice on her dead husband's keys and insists nothing in this city has
  changed in sixty years.

Four Alders, four different answers, none of them said out loud at the same
table. That is the family, and it is the ward in miniature.

### The Skells — let the boat go

The other answer, and numerically the winning one. The Skells got off the water
and took the trades that stayed *inside* the wall: fish, eel, smoke racks, the
fish hall, wharf wages, hide carrying, burial work, a salter, a doctor. A few
Skells still crew — **Ede Skell** (35) is a bargeman, **Gude Skell** (54) is a
wharf hand — but a Skell crews for a *wage*, on someone else's boat, and goes
home at the gate without a care in the world for what the hull cost.

**Averil Skell** (52), fish smoker, is the practical power of Maren's Green: she
smokes, she sells, and she lends at a spark on the dozen. Ewart Alder owes her
forty-eight sparks and crosses the Green the long way to avoid her smoke, which
everybody, including Averil, thinks is funny.

The Green's summary of the whole argument, said with a shrug:

> **An Alder owns a boat and owes for it. A Skell owns nothing and is never
> hungry.**

The Alders hear this as an accusation of cowardice. The Skells hear it as a
compliment. Both are right.

### The Hobbes — went to the cart

Made peace with the enemy. The Hobbes carry: wool, freight, loads up from the
gate. They are, in the strictest ward-politics sense, the people who profit from
the dry carry, and they are boat-family by blood. **Gude Hobbe** (53) is a wool
carrier. **Renn Hobbe** (34) is the wick-priest of Saint Maren of the Reeds — a
boat-family son in the boat-family's pulpit, preaching to men who think his
brothers robbed them. He is very careful about which parable he chooses.

### The Tarns — went to barrel and hide

Coopers and tanners: **Sibbe Tarn** hoop-setter, **Warin Rasp** and **Colm Pell**
at the tubs, **Gile Tarn** (63) on Tanners' Slip. River-adjacent trades that
never touch a river. A tub is the safest thing a boat-family can make. It holds
water and does not have to float.

### The Crakes — went downriver

The Long Departure family. A boatman can *leave* — this is the ugly truth the
Reed Ward has never digested. A mason is tied to a fabric, a glazier to a
furnace, a draper to a hall; a boatman has the road under him and it goes
somewhere. When the Hammering broke the ward in F.415, boat-families left in a
proportion no other trade matched. They poled to Salorge, to Harne, to the sea,
and most did not come back.

**Havise Crake** (31) is a warehouse hand in the Reed Ward and the one who
stayed. Letters come, twice a year, from a Crake at the salt pans who signs
himself well. She reads them aloud at the Ox when she has had a drink and not
otherwise. The ones who stayed feel loyal and stupid in about equal measure, and
the ones who left feel — as far as anyone can tell from a letter — nothing at
all.

### The fallen — the ones with bynames

**This is the most important social fact in the ward. A boat-family that loses
its boat loses its name.**

A fixed family name is a thing you can only afford to keep if you own something.
When the hull is sold, the yard is lost, the heirs scatter and the lease lapses,
what is left is a person with a given name and whatever the street calls them.
So the poor of the Reed Ward carry bynames of place and gear rather than houses:

- **Cobb Hawser** (63), rope-picker on Tanners' Slip. A hawser is a boat's rope.
  He gathers rope for a living now. Nobody on the Green needs this explained.
- **Renn of the Slip** (24), salvage picker.
- **Warin Underbridge** (27), carter on the Cut.
- **Averil Vell** (59), badge attendant — the Vells had a warehouse once, and
  she sells pilgrim badges in the shadow of somebody else's.

Ombreval's naming rule does the cruelty for you: bynames arise from trade,
place, feature or event, and they are *earned or inherited, not self-selected*.
The ward's saying is exact and merciless:

> **There are more Hawsers than Alders.**

### The rest — Reed Ward, not boat-blood

Not every heavy name on the Green came off the water, and the class system above
does not include them. It belongs to the **boat-families proper**: the five
houses — Alder, Skell, Hobbe, Tarn, Crake — and the fallen bynames of gear and
place — Hawser, Underbridge, Vell, "of the Slip." A boat-family is one that had
to answer *do we keep the boat?* Plenty of Reed Ward households never faced the
question, because they were never on the water.

These names are **ward-resident, not boat-blood**: the **Fitches** (Noll Fitch
keeps the keys and the parish register as sexton of Saint Maren of the Reeds),
the **Rasps**, the **Ruds**, the **Pells** and the **Sarks**. They keep shops,
tubs, benches and altars on the Green, marry into the boat-houses, and work
beside them — a Warin Rasp or a Colm Pell sets hoops at a Tarn's tubs — but the
name carries no share in the boat argument, no pole to lay over the door, and no
soundings in the head. On the Green the line is drawn without malice and never
forgotten: a man can live his whole life in the Reed Ward and still not be *of
the water*.

## The soundings

The boat-families keep one asset the diversion could not take, and they keep it
in their heads.

They remember **where the river was**.

The Cut was filled between F.363 and F.369 with rubble, spoil, ruin and whatever
came to hand. What was channel is now made ground; what was bank is old solid
earth. From the street you cannot tell the difference. From a cellar, a
foundation trench, a well shaft or a cracked party wall, you can tell very well
indeed — a house built over the old channel settles, and a house built on the
old bank does not. Doors that will not close, floors that run downhill, a
staircase pulling off a wall: these follow an invisible river that stopped
flowing sixty-eight years ago.

The old boat-families know the line of the deep channel, the soft banks, the
places where a boat used to ground in a dry summer, the sills, the shallows, the
made ground and the rubbish. They call this **the soundings**, and it is worth
money:

- Masons and cellar-diggers on the Cut pay quietly for a walk-over and an
  opinion. The masons' lodge resents paying, disputes the fee, and pays.
- The **Line-keeper** consults old boatmen on disputed boundaries and shoring
  orders and does not put it in the minutes.
- Well-diggers and cistern men want the same knowledge for the opposite reason.
- Landlords on the Cut deny the whole thing exists, because a house **built on
  the bed** is worth less, and every Cut landlord's house was built on the bed
  according to somebody.

The soundings have never been written down. They are taught by walking a
seven-year-old up the Cut and asking him where he would have poled. The Alders
hold the best of it. Ewart, in drink, has threatened more than once to sell the
lot to the masons' lodge in a single afternoon and be done with the family
forever — which would be the end of the Alders' last real capital, and everyone
who heard him knows it.

The smallest and most guarded sounding is the **true arch of the Old Sluice**,
where Colm Attergate drowned in F.301. Wyn Alder will walk any pilgrim there for
a penny and show them an arch that is not the arch, and tell them it is close
enough for a penny. The true one is the family's, and no coin has ever bought it
out of her.

## Money, toll, and the barrow

Three quarrels, all live, all about the same thing.

**The tun.** River cargo is measured by the **Serle tun** and proved at the
Tallage against the stone and the sealed Gaudry weights. Every boatman in
Ombreval will tell you, without evidence and without hesitation, that the tun
has crept heavier since the diversion, because the men who keep the standard are
now the men who profit from the carry. At Gaudry's Audit somebody from the Reed
Ward always comes to watch the proving, and always leaves unsatisfied.

**The toll.** Freight through the **River Gate** is tolled. Handbarrows through
the **Reed Postern** are tolled lightly or not at all, because the postern was
opened as a working door for wharf hands, fish baskets and funerals, not as a
customs house.

**The barrow.** Therefore: a load *come by barrow* is a load that has not been
tolled. Everybody knows what that means. Eels landed at dusk on the strip below
the wall and walked up Maren's Slip at first light in a dozen small trips are, in
the eyes of the Bench, twelve honest handbarrows; in the eyes of the Tallage,
one untolled boat. This is the Reed Ward's steady, unglamorous, universally
practised petty crime, and it is why the ward fights so hard at every reckoning
to keep the postern's hours long and its toll off handcarts. When a Reed Ward
bencher stands up to defend the postern as *a poor man's door*, half the Bench
believes him and the other half knows exactly what he is doing, and it is not
uncommon for both halves to vote for it.

*Say a thing came by barrow* and you have called it stolen without saying so.

In F.437 the ward's two benchers are **Idonea Tarn**, who keeps the Tarn
cooperage's ledgers on Tanners' Slip and whose kinsman Tobin is precisely why
the postern vote is never quite about smuggling out loud, and **Hamel of the
Reach**, a Cut tally-man whose family name went with its hull and who stands
at every Gaudry's Audit to watch the proving of the tun. One name of standing
and one byname — the ward's arithmetic, elected.

## The year

The Reed Ward's calendar is a calendar of a river seen through a wall.

### Colm's Night — early spring

Green-dipped candles for Colm Attergate, the drowned labourer, burned at home
across the city — but the boat-families burn theirs *at the sluice*, because he
drowned there, in the Cut, when the Cut still ran. They set them in the dry grate
of the Old Sluice, where there has been no water for sixty-eight years, and
**Rohese of the Sluice** rakes out the stubs in the morning as her husband did
and his father did.

It is an unofficial feast. Colm preached that the Church was walling a sun out of
the sky and was banished for it, and a grey coat from the Custody is generally
somewhere on the sluice road that night, standing where the light is bad, being
seen. Nothing ever happens. Everyone notices anyway.

### Vhairestide — the Walking Out

Spring. The blessing of the first boats at the wharves beyond the south wall, and
the turn of the year.

The blessing happens at the water, which means it happens *outside the wall*,
which means the Reed Ward has to leave the city to attend its own most important
feast. And so, once a year, the **Reed Postern is opened wide for a crowd** and
the ward walks out: the wives, the ledger-keepers, the children, the old, the
gate-bound, the born-inside. Some of them are carried. Some of them have not seen
the Serle since the last Vhairestide and will not see it again until the next.

The wick-priest blesses the boats. The boat-families answer the blessing at the
moorings with the old words — *one shadow to your road*, and the answer, *and one
light over it* — which are said at doors and at moorings everywhere in Ombreval,
and which are said at a real mooring exactly once a year by people who say them
every morning at a gate with nothing behind it.

Then everybody walks back in through the postern, and Maren's Slip turns, and the
river goes out of sight.

Somebody always weeps and it is never who you expect. The Common Bench grumbles
every year about the cost of the watch for an open postern, and every year finds
that it cannot be the Bench that closed it.

### Marenstide — the eel fair, the drowned, and the Dry Race

Late summer. The eel fair fills Maren's Green with smoke, baskets, drink, money
and noise, and it is the best week of the ward's year.

Two things sit inside it.

**The roll of the drowned.** Saint Maren keeps the drowned tidy, and at
Marenstide her roll is read at the church. It has a problem: **since the
diversion, almost nobody drowns in Ombreval.** There is no water in the city to
drown in. The roll grows now only by boatmen lost outside the walls — a handful a
decade — and so, after F.415, the parish began adding the ward's Hammering dead
to a roll of the drowned, because the Hammering dead were denied their
name-knells and this was a bell that could still be rung for them.

The traditional families object, in the ward's flattest voice:

> **You cannot drown under slate.**

The wick-priest **Renn Hobbe** reads them anyway. He has been told to stop by
people he baptised. He has been told to read more of them by people who bury
their own. He reads the same list every year and takes the argument afterwards in
the porch, where it belongs.

**The Dry Race.** The young men of the Reed Ward put a real boat — an old,
retired, half-rotten hull the ward keeps for the purpose and repairs badly — on a
timber cradle with runners, and pole it down the dry Cut with river poles against
the stone. It is a straight run on the only straight street in a city that bends,
which is to say it is a run down the old bed, which is to say they are poling the
Serle. Other wards enter. Bets are laid at the Hungry Ox and settled at the Ox and
occasionally fought over at the Ox. Carts are shoved aside, the Bench issues a
notice about obstruction every few years, the notice is posted, the race is run.

It is a joke, and it is not a joke, and if you say either of those things out loud
on Maren's Green at Marenstide somebody will disagree with you.

### The Hammering anniversary — late summer

The common mourning toll at the Waning. In the Reed Ward it is heard in a
particular place: the **Moorings roof came down in F.415** and killed, among
others, the father of Wyn and Ewart Alder. The yard's present roof is a patch of
three campaigns and everyone who works under it knows which bays are new.

*One bell is not a name* is heard as often on Maren's Green as anywhere in
Ombreval, and here it is heard from people who also say *you cannot drown under
slate*, without any sense of contradiction, because both sentences mean the same
thing: **give me back the name of my dead.**

### The frost

When the Serle freezes, the Reed Ward eats debt. The boats stop, the wharf hands
stop, the wage stops, the credit tightens, and Averil Skell's book gets longer.
The great freeze of F.229 stopped the Cut and cost the boatmen their work, and
they roasted an ox on the ice at the Tallage — inside the city, on the river, in
the middle of a market square, which is a thing that cannot happen any more and
which every child in the ward can describe.

The Hungry Ox's sign shows an underfed ox. Bertran of the Ox will tell you it is
that ox. Bertran of the Ox will tell you a number of things.

### Funerals — the postern

The Reed Postern is a working gate for wharf hands, fish baskets, handbarrows —
**and funerals**.

The ward's drowned and its dead from the outer wharves come home through the
postern, up Maren's Slip, into Saint Maren's churchyard. Maren's Slip has a final
turn that hides the wall opening from the Green and hides the river from inside
the city. So a boatman's last sight of the Serle is going out, and coming back he
does not see it at all.

A boat-family lays the dead man's pole across the coffin on the way up the slip,
and takes it back at the churchyard gate, and hangs it over the door of the house.
An old Reed Ward doorway with three poles over it is a house that has buried three
boatmen, and a house with no pole over the door and boat-family inside it is a
house that has sold the boat, which is worse.

The newest buried name is chalked on the charnel-door lintel and can be read
obliquely from the Eel Bridge rail without standing at the door.

## How they talk

Boatmen sound like a depth sounding: level, short, weather first, nothing wasted.
They are the calmest people in Ombreval and the least impressed by anything, up to
and including the impossible light — *the water knows one sun*, and they say it the
way they would report a depth.

The language of the ward is a river language spoken on dry land, and it is the
fastest way to mark an NPC as Reed Ward in one line of dialogue.

- **They give directions by the current.** *Two doors downstream of the Ox.*
  *Upstream side of the bridge.* On a street. With no water in it. Everyone else
  in the city says north and south; a boat-family says upstream and downstream,
  and means the direction the Serle ran before their grandparents were born. A
  stranger who asks for "up the street" is instantly a stranger.
- **The Cut's cartway is *the reach*.** The Tally Bridge is *the bridge*, though
  it crosses nothing.
- **"The dry carry."** The doubled handling, and everything wrong with the world.
- **"To lift it twice."** To pay for a thing you have already paid for.
- **"Come by barrow."** Untolled. Stolen, politely.
- **"A dry drowning."** To be ruined by debt: quietly, on land, in full view, with
  everybody watching and nobody putting out a hand.
- **"He poles the Cut."** He works very hard at a thing that goes nowhere. Said of
  a man keeping up a trade, a lawsuit, or a marriage that is finished. The Dry
  Race is the joke and this is the knife inside it.
- **"Ask an Alder where the bed was."** Said when a wall cracks, a floor tilts, a
  cellar weeps, or a scheme fails for reasons nobody can see. It means: *there is
  an old cause under this, and somebody knew about it, and it was not written
  down.*
- **"Born inside."** Never been out to the water.
- **"Gate-caught."** Shut out at the Snuffing. Also, more generally: late, through
  no fault of one's own, in a system that does not care.
- **"There are more Hawsers than Alders."** Most people who had something have
  lost it. Said as consolation, and as a threat.
- **"One shadow to your road." / "And one light over it."** The city's blessing at
  doors and moorings. Boat-families say it at the Moorings gate every morning of
  the year to people walking out through the wall to reach the river.
- **"The Water Knows One"** is sung at the Hungry Ox and spills thirty metres into
  the lane, and it is sung louder when a grey coat passes.

## Quarrels worth playing

Every one of these is live in F.437 and none of them has a right answer.

1. **Sell the Moorings.** Ewart says the yard is a dry warehouse with a proud name
   and should be sold to a Tallage broker at the top of a falling market. Wyn has
   never answered him out loud, which the family has decided means she agrees.
   Tobin wants the smoke-sheds and has told nobody but a fifteen-year-old girl who
   strings eels.
2. **Sell the soundings.** The one Alder asset that cannot be repossessed, and
   Ewart can turn it into cash in an afternoon. The masons' lodge would pay. The
   Line-keeper would rather it stayed exactly where it is, unwritten, and available
   to him for the price of a drink.
3. **The postern.** Reed Ward wants its hours long and its toll off handcarts.
   Every other ward knows why. The vote happens at every reckoning and it is never
   quite about smuggling out loud.
4. **The tun.** Prove the Serle tun at Gaudry's Audit and find out whether sixty
   years of boatmen have been right, or whether a whole ward has been nursing a
   grievance against an honest weight. Either answer is a disaster for somebody.
5. **The roll of the drowned.** Whether the Hammering dead belong on it. This is
   really the argument about the Unknelled, fought on ground the boat-families own,
   and the wick-priest reading it is a boat-family son.
6. **The wharf-born.** Is a child born in a shed on the strip a Reed Ward resident?
   Does his household owe ward tax? May his father stand for bencher? The wall says
   no and the parish register says whatever the sexton wrote down. **Noll Fitch**
   has written down a number of things.
7. **The carters.** The Hobbes are boat-family and the Hobbes eat off the dry
   carry, and at a funeral everybody stands in the same churchyard and nobody says
   any of it.
8. **Cobb Hawser's rope.** Sixty-three, picking rope on Tanners' Slip, and he can
   still walk the Cut and tell you the depth of a river you cannot see. Nobody has
   thought to ask him in twenty years. The soundings are not only the Alders'; they
   are simply the Alders' to *sell*.

## What this document does not settle

Deliberately open, per the canon rules:

- **No secret water.** The Cut is dry, the Serle is outside the south wall, and
  nothing in this document puts flowing water back under the city. The soundings
  are geology and memory, not a hidden channel. `wells_and_water.md` is law here.
- **No river magic.** Saint Maren keeps the drowned tidy in a song. The true arch
  of the Old Sluice is a fact about masonry and a family's stock-in-trade — no
  spirit, no voice, no green reflection, no environmental effect. Colm Attergate
  drowned; that is all that happened.
- **The Serle reflects one ordinary sun.** Every boat-family in this document
  states this as calmly as a depth. It is not evidence for or against anything, and
  boatmen would be embarrassed to have it treated as a theory.
- **Whether the tun is actually heavy.** Never resolve it in the boatmen's favour
  or against them. The grievance is the content.
