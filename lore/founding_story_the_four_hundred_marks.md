# The Four Hundred Marks

> *Peace was bought for four hundred marks, of which the town says the Church
> gave nothing. The town lies, but not by much.*
>
> — the Chapter's chronicle, under F.54

> Shared canon for Ombreval's disputed secular founding story. This is
> author-facing history rather than one authoritative in-world account. The
> seizure, the payment, and the emergency reckoning are real; the surviving
> documents do not identify one true founder, one exact division of the sum,
> or an unbroken constitutional line from F.54 to F.437.

## Canon in brief

In the winter of **F.54**, the lord of **Harne**, four leagues downstream,
closed the Serle with a heavy river chain and detained salt-boats coming up
from Salorge. He claimed unpaid or disputed river tolls. Ombreval called it
theft then and calls it theft now. With winter salt held below the chain, the
lord demanded **four hundred marks** for release of the boats and peace on the
river.

Ombreval possessed no ruler, treasury, or settled civic body able to answer
for the whole town. Authority was divided among the Chapter, market officers,
landholding households, boat families, merchants, craft fellowships, and
people who held old customary rights at the ford. Each could raise some part
of the demand; none could bind all the others. Delegates therefore met in
emergency, reckoned what could be given or pledged, assigned contributions,
and created a custody for what they gathered.

The payment was not four hundred identical coins. A **mark** was an accounting
weight of silver, and the settlement almost certainly combined weighed coin,
foreign money, plate, cloth, credit, future toll income, shares in boats and
freight, and many small household payments. Much of what Harne received at
once had been advanced against assets or revenues that Ombreval spent years
repaying internally.

Later civic tradition named the meeting the **First Common Reckoning** and
called its coffer the first **Common Chest**. The present Common Bench treats
it as the moment Ombreval first acted as one legal and financial body. That is
a defensible inheritance, not a provable claim that sixteen elected benchers,
eight tax wards, a First Seat, or the modern Chest already existed in F.54.
Those institutions took their present forms over centuries.

The founding story must retain four uncertainties:

- No surviving account gives a trustworthy division of the four hundred
  marks.
- No single estate or person can be established as the city's saviour.
- It cannot be proved that the emergency gathering became the Common Bench
  without interruption.
- It remains possible for a document to be genuine about one contribution and
  self-serving about what that contribution meant.

The event has no supernatural cause or consequence. It predates the Great
Rose by nearly a century and a half and the first public record of the
impossible light by even longer. It belongs to salt, winter, iron, credit,
jurisdiction, and memory.

## Before the chain: one town, many purses

Ombreval in F.54 was already more than a village at the ford, but it was not
yet the civic machine of F.437. The Lanthorn had been rising for fifty-three
years; its quire would not be vaulted and sung in until F.61. The Chapter
could speak for consecrated ground, rents, clergy, and the Fabric. Market
officers could enforce market custom. Great households could promise their
own goods and tenants' dues. Boat masters could bind a crew or a vessel.
Merchants could extend credit to people they trusted. Craft fraternities
could collect among their own members. None of these powers amounted to a
generally accepted right to tax Ombreval.

This division had advantages in ordinary years. A bad bargain made by a cloth
merchant did not become a boatman's debt. A Chapter building expense did not
automatically fall upon the shambles. A ford toll did not entitle its keeper
to search every household's cupboard. Old liberties survived precisely
because no one purse was called the town's.

The weakness appeared when the loss was both common and indivisible. Harne
had not taken one merchant's private cargo in a quarrel that could be left to
that merchant. It had stopped the route by which a necessary good reached
everyone. The rich could buy from stores for longer than the poor, but no
quarter could exempt itself from a winter without salt while the rest of the
town settled the river.

Later Bench orators say that Ombreval had a body before F.54 but no hands: it
could suffer as one town and could not yet pay as one. The phrase is late,
but the problem is exact.

## Salt before midwinter

Salt from the pans at Salorge travelled six days upriver in favourable
conditions and longer when current, weather, or towpath failed. It was not a
luxury seasoning. It preserved meat and fish, entered cheese and curing,
served tanners and healers, and kept stores usable when fresh food was scarce.
A detained cargo therefore threatened more than the owners of the boats.

The seizure occurred after the easy carrying season and before the hardest
part of winter. Sources disagree about whether it fell just before Wet Alms
or between Wet Alms and Belwyn's Dark. Both dates serve later storytellers:
the Chapter prefers a crisis answered during almsgiving, while household
accounts prefer salt already running short at midwinter. The safest canon is
simply **early winter**.

Harne's castle stood above a narrower reach of the Serle. A chain could be
raised from a chain-house or winch to bar an approaching hull and lowered for
traffic the lord permitted. It did not need to make the river physically
impassable for miles. Armed men on the banks, the castle overhead, and several
boats already held were argument enough.

No reliable count of the captured boats survives. Ombreval traditions give
numbers from five to twelve, usually choosing a number meaningful to the
speaker. Boat families count hulls; merchants count cargo shares; sermons
count scriptural virtues; Harne records, where they can be trusted at all,
count only vessels formally attached for toll. **Salt-boats in the plural** is
all that should be fixed.

### Harne's claim

The lord of Harne did not describe himself as a robber. His clerks claimed an
old right to toll traffic below Ombreval, arrears owed by carriers, and the
cost of keeping the lower river safe. Different copies add different legal
grounds. One says the toll attached to the castle bank. Another says it
attached to salt alone. A third says Ombreval had promised payment after an
earlier flood and defaulted. None is independent of later litigation.

Ombreval's answer was that no lord could invent ownership of flowing water by
putting iron across it. Boat families still give the shortest version:
**nobody renames water, and nobody owns its throat**.

It is possible that some toll was genuinely overdue. It is certain that Harne
chose the moment and the cargo that gave its claim maximum force. A lawful
claim pressed by starving one's neighbour can remain extortion. The present
lord of Harne takes a known toll under later agreements and does not close the
river; the curse attached to the castle is inherited history, not a statement
that every later toll is unlawful.

## The demand

The message brought upriver asked four hundred marks for the boats, their
cargo, and peace. Later pictures show a Harne messenger dropping a chain link
onto a table. Later plays give him a sack of spoiled salt to empty at the
delegates' feet. Neither detail appears in an early source. What matters is
that the demand arrived in a form no existing Ombreval office could answer.

Who had been asked to pay?

The Chapter said the quarrel concerned secular toll and private freight. The
boat owners said the town would lose the salt. The cloth merchants said they
had no title to levy households. Household speakers asked why people without
shares in the captured boats should ransom them. Craftspeople asked whether
work offered in place of coin would be counted. The market officers could
name a price and collect a customary due, but four hundred marks was beyond
any due custom allowed them.

The first achievement of the Reckoning was not finding money. It was deciding
that there existed an **Ombreval answer** to a demand addressed to no single
master.

## What a mark meant

The **F.54 mark was not a coin**. It was a weight and money of account: a
contractual quantity of silver against which mixed coin, bullion, plate, and
other value could be assessed. No one laid four hundred round pieces in rows.
Coins from several mints circulated, many clipped or worn, and valuable metal
had to be weighed and sometimes assayed.

Nor should the F.54 mark be converted neatly into F.437 sparks.
The silver standard, coin fineness, local pounds, mint charges, and price of
goods all changed in the intervening centuries. Clerks can reconstruct
ranges, and every reconstruction serves an argument. The only safe economic
conclusions are that four hundred marks was far beyond any normal private
purchase in Ombreval and still within the combined capacity of a trading
town willing to mortgage its future.

There is a further dispute over **whose mark** set the weight. Harne copies
name the castle weight. Ombreval copies name a Serle merchants' mark. One
later civic roll says the parties proved both weights and split the
difference, an answer so reasonable that careful clerks distrust it. The
Tallage stone and Gaudry weights did not yet stand beside the Cut; the Cut
itself would not be made until F.83–90.

In later speech the title acquired a useful triple meaning. There were marks
of silver, marks cut into tallies, and the personal marks made by contributors
who could not or would not write a name. Founding recitals exploit the pun.
It is not evidence that people in F.54 did so.

## Summoning the First Common Reckoning

No source securely identifies the summoner. This is the first and most
politically important disagreement.

The Chapter says a canon called the town to shelter under the unfinished
Lanthorn. The Bench says household elders demanded an open reckoning after
private negotiations failed. Cloth accounts say the principal creditors
refused another advance until all wards stood behind it. Boat tradition says
women of the detained families rang the old watch-bell without leave and
would not stop until the market gathered. Harne's version says its lord
ordered Ombreval to send persons capable of binding the town, making the
enemy—absurdly—the father of the Bench.

The meeting place is no more certain. Three locations are shown to visitors:

- an old mason's bench kept at the Bench House, said to have stood in an
  open-sided Fabric shed;
- the site of an early toll shed near the old ford-market; and
- a Chapter room later absorbed into the Lanthorn precinct.

The surviving descriptions are compatible with several meetings in several
places: a first crowd at the bell, smaller bargaining sessions under a roof,
and a final public reading at the market. Later tradition compressed them
into one room because a founding needs a door through which everyone can be
imagined entering.

### Who came

The First Reckoning was broad for its century and narrow by any claim of
universal government. It probably included:

- delegates of resident households, selected by lane, kin group, landlord,
  or neighbourhood custom;
- owners, part-owners, and kin of the detained boats;
- salt and cloth traders, money dealers, warehouse keepers, and carriers;
- masters or trusted speakers from craft fellowships;
- market and ford officers;
- clergy and officers of the Chapter and Fabric; and
- a smaller number of servants, porters, apprentices, labourers, and poor
  householders who came without a formal seat but could not simply be kept
  outside once the market had gathered.

Women who headed households, owned shares, ran shops, or represented absent
kin could speak and pledge. That is consistent with later Ombreval custom and
does not make the Reckoning a modern equal franchise. Many adults had no
recognised independent voice. Lodgers, dependent servants, hired labourers,
and the very poor were more likely to be spoken for than listened to. People
outside the town whose property was trapped at Harne had money at stake but
no obvious place in an Ombreval assembly.

The modern number of sixteen benchers should not be projected backward. Late
pageants seat sixteen founders, two for each present ward, beneath a First
Seat. Early lists, where not plainly reconstructed, suggest a changing crowd
and a smaller group of counters. The eight current tax wards did not spring
fully named from one winter meeting.

### Why it was called a reckoning

The gathering was not first called a parliament, commune, or council. It was
remembered as a **reckoning** because its authority came from making burdens
visible: what was asked, what existed, what each party promised, who would
hold it, and how repayment would be heard.

The distinction still matters in Ombreval. A ruler announces what is owed. A
reckoning is supposed to say why, by what measure, from whom, and where it
went. Practice often falls short. The claim remains.

## How the four hundred were made

The great error in most factional histories is to imagine that the
contributions must add in one clean column. They do not, because the same
value passed through several hands and appeared in several kinds of record.

A cloth merchant might pledge finished cloth. A money dealer might then
advance silver against that pledge. The Reckoning might promise future market
or river revenue to repay the money dealer. Households might later pay the
assessment that supplied that revenue. The cloth merchant, money dealer, and
households could all say truthfully that **their** property paid the same
mark. A boat family might also have stood surety for it with a freight share.

The contradictory totals are therefore not proof that every roll is false.
They show different layers of one financing. Ombreval did not discover four
hundred marks in a forgotten coffer. It made hundreds of obligations agree
long enough to pass as one payment.

### Ready silver and merchants' advances

The fastest value came from people who already held coin, bullion, or credit
outside their own trade. Money dealers and larger merchants advanced silver
in return for written acknowledgements, security over goods, priority in
future toll income, or some combination of the three.

Their descendants describe this as rescue at grave private risk. Their
critics answer that no other creditors in Ombreval ever acquired such secure
claims on public revenue. Both statements can be true. The risk was real;
the bargain made under emergency was also favourable to those able to supply
ready metal.

No reliable rate of interest survives. Some deeds forbid **increase** while
granting a yearly **courtesy**, a share of toll, favourable warehouse terms,
or repayment above nominal weight to cover clipping and assay. Later lawyers
argue endlessly over whether these were interest, expenses, or gifts. The
money did not become less costly because the cost had a polite name.

### Cloth and bonded goods

Ombreval's cloth interests had wealth that could be counted even when it was
not coin. Finished cloth, wool stocks, dyestuffs, and claims on future
production were pledged or sold below their expected value. Some may have
gone to Harne as part of the settlement; more likely, much of it secured
silver advanced by merchants who could make the immediate payment.

The cloth version of the founding says the town was freed by bolts laid end
to end across the Reckoning floor. This is good pageantry and poor finance.
Cloth mattered because it was trusted, measurable, movable, and saleable—not
because Harne accepted a picturesque mountain of it without valuation.

The later Draper's Reach did not exist in F.54, and its guild histories are
retrospective. Cloth workers and merchants nevertheless had organised
interests long before the gallery received that name. Their claim cannot be
dismissed merely because later institutions polished it.

### The Chapter's silver

Some Church silver entered the financing. This is fixed by the Chapter's own
mocking admission that the town lies, **but not by much**, when it says the
Church gave nothing. The amount and form are not fixed.

Chapter histories tell of sacred plate freely laid down when secular purses
failed. Town histories say a small quantity of broken, unconsecrated, or
already pledged silver arrived late and was loudly remembered. An inventory
gap around F.54 proves that objects left the Chapter's custody; it does not say
whether they were melted, pawned, exchanged, hidden during the crisis, or
lost in unrelated Fabric accounts.

The most plausible reconstruction is that the Church's share was modest in
the total but useful because at least part of it was hard silver available at
the exact moment a balance had to be made good. This permits both civic mockery
and clerical pride. A small final weight can turn a scale. It does not become
the whole load.

Stories that the high altar was stripped bare, that a saint's reliquary was
broken under a hammer, or that the unfinished Lanthorn surrendered its future
Great Rose are later inventions. The Great Rose would not be made until
F.198–203.

### Boat shares, hulls, and future freight

Boat ownership along the Serle was divided. A household might own an eighth
of a hull, part of a seasonal cargo, the use of a tow animal, or a claim on
certain freight journeys. The Reckoning could therefore take security in
river property without taking one boat from one owner whole.

Boat-family accounts say they pledged shares in the very craft Harne held,
as well as future freight after release. Some say crews promised a number of
free common journeys: salt, stone, timber, or grain carried for the town
without freight charge. Such service would leave weaker written traces than
silver but could have been economically substantial.

Later exemptions complicate the claim. Several old river households enjoyed
reduced dues or preferred landing rights and said these repaid F.54 shares.
The Common Chest sometimes called the same privileges revocable customs.
Whether the town honoured a debt or tolerated a powerful family depended on
which book was open.

### Household pennies

Small payments were collected by street, lane, hearth, landlord, parish, or
some untidy mixture of them. They included copper pennies, fragments of
silver, rings, buckles, and promises payable after market. Poorer households
gave far less in absolute value than great traders. Their contribution became
the moral centre of the story because it was broad, visible, and painful.

The popular account says the final deficiency was met one penny at a time
after every great purse had refused more. The Chapter says the pennies came
first and proved inadequate. Merchant rolls often omit them or enter only the
silver for which a money dealer exchanged them. Civic copies group them into
large anonymous sums such as **the east lanes** or **the ford hearths**, which
is efficient bookkeeping and excellent erasure.

It is arithmetically implausible that household pennies supplied most of four
hundred marks. It is equally implausible that they supplied nothing. More
important, many households paid again through the later assessments used to
repay advances. This is the origin of the enduring poor-household claim:
**the rich paid once in front of the clerk and were repaid; the poor paid
twice and were thanked.**

### Work that no roll valued

Porters carried boxes and bales. Smiths altered locks and bound coffers.
Clerks copied pledges. Bakers fed a meeting that outlasted the food brought to
it. Watchmen guarded collections. Messengers travelled the Harne road. Kin of
the detained crews lost wages while the boats lay still. None of this fits
cleanly into the four-hundred-mark sum.

Craft accounts sometimes convert the labour into money and claim it as part
of the ransom. Official accounts usually classify it as expense, service, or
duty. That difference matters. What is excluded from the celebrated number
is excluded from the founding honour.

## Gift, loan, levy, or pledge

The Reckoning's hardest argument was not how much each group could produce.
It was what the town promised in return.

Every contribution needed a category:

- A **gift** ended the giver's claim.
- A **loan** required repayment, but the source and order of repayment had to
  be named.
- A **pledge** put property at risk if another promise failed.
- An **assessment** asserted a common power to compel payment.
- A **service** might discharge money owed or might simply be demanded as a
  customary duty.

The categories blurred under pressure. A household mark that one clerk read
as assent to an assessment was copied by a descendant as acknowledgement of
a loan. A merchant's gift became a secured advance in the family book. A boat
share described as temporary surety remained burdened after the peace. The
same ambiguity that let agreement happen in F.54 produced litigation for
generations.

Later Bench recitals praise the founders for giving without distinction. The
surviving fragments show people making distinctions with great care.

## Tallies, names, and personal marks

Promises were recorded in several ways. Larger obligations received written
instruments where a clerk and available parchment could be found. Running
accounts grouped smaller payments. Split wooden tallies let a contributor
and collector retain matching evidence. Seals authenticated institutions and
wealthy households. Other people made witnessed personal marks.

This was not one perfect ledger. Collections occurred in different places,
were converted between goods and silver, and passed through intermediaries.
One clerk counted what entered a ward coffer; another counted what a merchant
advanced against it; a third counted what left for Harne. Later copyists tried
to make the stages look like one list and often counted the same value twice
or removed a name that seemed duplicated.

The founding's documentary confusion is therefore structural. Fraud came
later and probably came at the time as well, but honest accounting under
emergency would still have left conflicting records.

### The woman with the last penny

Every ward has, at one time, claimed **Ede Pennyhand**, also called Ede
Threepence, the Salt Widow, or simply Old Ede. In the common story she stood
when the Reckoning was short, emptied three household pennies onto the board,
and shamed the wealthy into completing the sum.

No early roll names her. The oldest versions disagree over whether her kin
were on a detained boat, whether she sold onions, mended nets, kept a cookshop,
or begged at the ford. Some versions make her young and carrying a child;
others old enough to rebuke grandsons. Three wards point to a different house
site. Harne plays turn her into a comic fishwife who cannot count.

Ede should not be unmasked as the forgotten true founder. She is more useful
as the person the story needed when anonymous household sums became morally
intolerable. She gives a face to real small contributors whose names the
great rolls did not preserve.

## The first Common Chest

The collected value required neutral custody. No party would place everything
under one rival's key. The Reckoning therefore appointed several keepers and
used a coffer or set of coffers opened under joint witness. Later tradition
gives the principal chest three locks: one key for the market, one for the
river, and one for the Church. Other versions give four keys, eight, or two.
The number always resembles the constitution preferred by the storyteller.

It is likely that joint custody was real. It is not likely that the large
coffer displayed at the Bench House is the same timber. Repair accounts name
its ironwork centuries after F.54, and parts have plainly been replaced. The
physical claim matters less than the institutional one. Money called common
could not remain in a private strongroom and still command trust.

The name **Common Chest** may also be later. Early copies use several phrases:
the peace coffer, the town holding, the salt account, and the common keeping.
By the time the Cut and Tallage were established, the concept of revenue held
for shared works was no longer temporary even when individual taxes still
were.

The modern Chest-keeper, the requirement for warrants from two benchers of
different wards, and the public reading of expenditure are not unchanged
F.54 procedures. They express the same inherited suspicion: no single hand
should be able to turn common need into private money.

## The road to Harne

Once enough immediate value and acceptable security had been assembled, a
delegation went downriver or by the winter road to Harne. Every institutional
history places its own predecessor at the delegation's head. No list is
secure.

The public version gives the delegates one chest containing exactly four
hundred marks. The likely reality was less satisfying: weighed silver and
coin, documents securing later delivery or payment, certified goods, and
several accounts that Harne's clerks had to accept at negotiated values. Some
portion may already have been in the detained cargoes or in hands able to pay
at Harne.

The lord accepted the settlement, released the boats and salt, and reopened
passage. Whether all four hundred marks changed hands before the chain came
down is unknown. Ombreval copies say release followed the proof of the whole
sum. Harne copies speak of an immediate composition with the residue secured.
Those can describe the same arrangement while assigning victory differently.

No battle liberated the boats. No saint broke the iron. No king granted
Ombreval freedom as a reward. The town paid a price it could not refuse.
That humiliation is essential. The civic achievement lies in what Ombreval
made from being cornered, not in pretending it was never cornered.

### The acquittance

A later copy of Harne's receipt addresses **the common at Ombreval** rather
than one lord, canon, or merchant. The Common Bench treats this as the first
external recognition of the city as a body capable of owing, paying, and
receiving peace.

The original seal is gone. The earliest surviving copy is later than the
event, and Harne antiquaries say its wording was modernised. Even if exact,
the phrase need not have granted political independence; a creditor may name
a group simply to collect from it. Even if modernised, it may preserve the
substance of an older instrument.

This is the right degree of uncertainty. Harne did not create Ombreval by
charter. By accepting one payment backed by many unrelated people, however,
Harne helped establish that the town could act outwardly as one party. The
enemy's receipt became more useful to civic liberty than any speech made at
the Reckoning.

## The return of the salt

The boats came upstream after release. Later songs have every household at
the bank, though many people would have been working, sleeping, or unable to
leave the meeting and collections. Salt sacks were landed, counted, and sold
or distributed under rules that immediately caused another dispute.

Who received the first salt?

Boat accounts say crews and detained households. The Chapter says the sick,
its alms, and the poor. Merchant books say buyers with prior contracts.
Household tradition says each contributing hearth received a measured share.
All may have happened in some degree. Scarcity does not end when a cargo is
released; it changes into an argument over allocation.

The clean civic ending—one pinch to every household—is a ritual memory, not a
credible distribution plan. Wealth still bought access. Connected people
still knew when a boat would arrive. Some speculators profited. The founding
story does not require the founders to become better people before the last
page.

## The city owed itself

The peace emptied the emergency coffers and left Ombreval full of internal
claims. This is the event's most important aftermath.

Harne had been paid, but merchants expected repayment, pledged cloth had to be
released, boat shares had to be restored, Church objects redeemed, and
household assessments collected or forgiven. Costs of assay, carrying,
guarding, copying, and negotiation sat beside the nominal ransom. Competing
priority clauses promised first payment from the same future revenue.

For years, perhaps decades, the town argued over a debt larger on paper than
the four hundred marks because several records represented different stages
of the same payment and because costs accumulated. A generation born after
the chain still paid dues said to arise from it. That is why descendants can
claim both that an ancestor was repaid and that the founding debt was never
settled: one instrument might have ended while a toll, privilege, or grievance
continued.

Temporary collections became customary. Clerks retained their books because
someone had to know which promise was next. Joint keepers retained a coffer
because revenue continued to pass through it. Hearings were needed because a
private creditor could not be allowed to decide a common priority alone.
People who had accepted assessment demanded to hear the account.

The iron chain at Harne was lifted. Ombreval spent the following centuries
arguing about the paper chain it had forged in reply.

## What the Reckoning founded

The First Common Reckoning did not create the present government in one
winter. It established several habits and claims from which that government
could later grow.

### A common liability

The town accepted that some threats could be answered from resources wider
than any one estate. This was not a blank authority to tax. Every later levy
still needed a named purpose, a measure, collectors, and an account. The
Bench's power grew through repeated emergencies and works, not one unlimited
grant.

### A common custody

Assets raised from many groups required a holding no contributor owned alone.
This is the conceptual origin of the Common Chest even if no physical coffer
or uninterrupted office survives from F.54.

### A right to a reckoning

Those made liable claimed a corresponding right to hear what had been
received and spent. Modern expenditure is read aloud at quarter reckonings
and proved at Gaudry's Audit. Civic tradition traces that publicity to F.54.
Whether the first reading was genuinely open is unknowable; the belief that it
should have been is politically real.

### Representation by household and place

The emergency taught the town to gather obligations through local groups and
send people able to speak for them. Over time, unstable lanes, parishes,
interests, and neighbourhoods became tax wards with regular reckonings. The
current eight wards and two elected benchers from each are later settlements,
not frozen founder law.

### The town as a party

Ombreval negotiated, contracted, paid, and received an acquittance in a common
capacity. Its later status as a free city rests on many acts of self-rule,
agreements, defences, tolls, judgments, and absences of an effective external
master. F.54 is remembered because it makes that long process legible in one
story.

### A secular authority beside the Church

The Chapter participated but could not command the whole response. Merchants
could finance but not lawfully assess everyone. Boat families could obstruct
or negotiate on the river but not bind inland households. The shared body
that emerged was therefore neither a Chapter office nor a private company.

This remains the constitutional lesson in F.437. The Candor governs doctrine,
clergy, and consecrated property. The Common Bench governs civic money,
markets, streets, walls, and secular law. They overlap and bargain. Neither is
the other's department.

## What it did not found

Later pageants routinely place the modern Common Bench in an F.54 room. That
should not be taken literally.

The First Reckoning did **not** establish, in their present form:

- sixteen benchers serving one-year terms;
- the eight current tax wards;
- a First Seat without veto;
- the permanent offices of Common Clerk, Chest-keeper, or Line-keeper;
- the Civic Measure Court or Full Measure;
- the present rules for warrants, quorum, recusal, or open ward choice; or
- a fully articulated free-city constitution.

The first unambiguous records of several of these arrangements are much later.
Names recur after gaps. Offices change duties. Ward boundaries move. A clerk
who says the Common Bench has sat continuously since F.54 is speaking civic
truth more confidently than documentary truth.

The strongest responsible formulation is: **the modern Bench descends from a
tradition of common reckoning that claims F.54 as its first necessary act**.
That is enough to make the event foundational without turning a desperate
collection into a miraculous constitution.

## The Cut, the Tallage, and the lesson of the chain

The Cut was made in F.83–90, nearly thirty years after the seizure. The Serle
was straightened through the town, the Tallage established, and the first
Tally Bridge raised. Later civic histories present all three as the founders'
answer to Harne: control the urban reach, standardise toll and weight, and keep
money ready so no downstream lord can surprise the town again.

This is partly retrospective. The Cut served trade, drainage, landing, and
political interests beyond fear of Harne. It also created new toll powers and
new winners. Still, people who remembered F.54 would have understood the
value of a navigable channel, known weights, bonded storage, reliable revenue,
and a body able to negotiate for the whole town.

The modern **Chain Bridge** does not commemorate Harne's chain. Its name comes
from the later harbour chain-house on the former Cut. Guides and children
often conflate the two, and civic pageants sometimes encourage the mistake.
Author-facing canon should keep them distinct.

## Eight accounts of who saved Ombreval

No account below is the hidden correct version. Each preserves evidence,
omits an inconvenience, and serves a present interest.

### 1. The Bench account: everyone, by measure

The official civic version says no estate could free the river alone. Each
household and trade gave according to capacity; trusted keepers counted the
whole; public consent made the payment lawful; and from that consent came the
Common Chest and Bench.

Its strength is that only a layered, collective financing explains the
event. Its dishonesty is retrospective neatness. Contributions were not equal,
consent was not universal, bargaining was coercive, and the people whose
labour went uncounted do not fit the polished roll.

The Bench tells this version whenever it wants a levy accepted or an account
closed.

### 2. The Chapter account: the silver that turned the scale

The Chapter says secular bargaining exhausted itself until Church silver made
good the final weight. It emphasises shelter under the unfinished Lanthorn,
clerks supplied by the Fabric, oaths kept under the light that does not lie,
and mercy shown in the later distribution of salt.

Its strength is the inventory gap and the likelihood that some immediately
acceptable silver mattered at the end. Its dishonesty is expansion: a timely
minor share becomes the decisive sacrifice, then the moral cause of every
other gift.

The town's answer remains the chronicle's own line: the Church did not give
nothing; the town lies, but not by much.

### 3. The cloth account: credit opened the river

Cloth merchants and their successors say goods, not speeches, made the ransom
credible. They advanced saleable wealth, found silver, accepted long risk,
and taught the town how future revenue could answer present danger.

Its strength is financial realism. Household pennies and boat pledges could
not be turned instantly into a form Harne would accept without intermediaries.
Its dishonesty is to describe security and priority as disinterested courage.
Some fortunes and toll privileges grew from the bargain.

The cloth account is told most loudly when the Bench seeks cheap credit.

### 4. The river account: no peace without the boats

Boat families say crews resisted, delayed unloading, hid manifests, or made a
night attempt against the chain, forcing Harne to bargain rather than simply
keep cargo and hulls. They pledged vessel shares and future freight, then
carried the salt home.

Its strength is that Harne needed cooperation to turn seizure into revenue and
that detained families bore immediate danger and lost work. Its dishonesty is
heroic enlargement. The chain was not broken in battle; the boats were
released because payment was accepted.

The river account is invoked against toll increases and whenever an inland
bencher speaks as if freight moves itself.

### 5. The household account: the last pennies

The popular version says great institutions argued until ordinary households
closed the deficiency. Ede Pennyhand's three pennies stand for thousands of
small sacrifices. The moral is not that the poor supplied the greatest weight
but that no common authority was legitimate until their money was counted.

Its strength is the evidence for small collections and later household
assessments. Its dishonesty is the comforting idea that being charged created
equal political power. Many contributors remained dependent and unnamed.

The household account is told at ward reckonings and in opposition to closed
books.

### 6. The clerkly account: the town was saved by distinctions

Clerks, notaries, and measure officers say the essential act was separating
gift from loan, weight from face value, ready payment from security, and
private claim from common obligation. Without credible records, nobody would
have advanced anything and no later payer would have trusted the Chest.

Its strength is that collective credit depends on legible promises. Its
dishonesty is assuming the surviving categories were neutral. A clerk's
choice determined whose labour disappeared, whose penny became a gift, and
whose advance earned priority.

The clerkly account is told whenever a fee is charged for copying old paper.

### 7. The Harne account: a debt dressed as liberty

Harne says its lord enforced a lawful toll claim, accepted a negotiated
composition, and released attached vessels. Ombreval later turned payment of
a debt into a tale of chains and freedom because a town of merchants could
not bear admitting that another market kept the stronger books.

Its strength is that a toll dispute probably lay beneath the seizure and that
Ombreval sources use the event for civic self-praise. Its dishonesty is the
pretence that winter timing, armed detention, and a demand backed by a castle
were ordinary accounting.

Harne tells this version because being remembered as a robber is bad for
trade, and because puncturing Ombreval pride is a local pleasure.

### 8. The debtor's account: the rich bought the town from itself

The harshest Ombreval version says the crisis socialised private shipping
losses, gave wealthy lenders claims on public revenue, converted emergency
collection into permanent taxation, and called the result freedom. In this
account the Common Chest was born not when everyone contributed, but when the
powerful discovered that common need could repay private silver.

Its strength is the long trail of privileges, toll shares, and household
resentment. Its dishonesty is reduction. The salt threatened the whole town,
the lenders assumed genuine risk, and shared institutions later paid for
walls, wells, watches, drains, and other things no private cargo owner could
provide alone.

This account is rarely read at the Bench House and often heard just outside
it.

## The surviving things

F.54 is unusually rich in claimed relics and poor in securely contemporary
objects. Every item below can matter without becoming a key that unlocks one
final answer.

### The Harne link

A black iron link, longer than a hand and too heavy for ceremony to make
graceful, is kept by the civic officers and displayed on some Weighdays. It is
said to be a link of Harne's river chain, surrendered with the peace or cut
loose by an Ombreval crew.

Its secure custody does not reach back to F.54. Its form is consistent with
heavy river or harbour work over a wide span of centuries. Harne says it came
from an Ombreval boom. Boat families say Harne replaced the cut section and
then denied it. An authentic link would prove a chain of the right general
kind, not who cut it or why peace was made. A later link would prove the
power of the story, not that the seizure never happened.

### The long tally

A split tally in civic custody bears grouped notches traditionally read as
payments toward the four hundred. Its matching half is lost, unless one of
three privately held fragments is genuine. The notation has been interpreted
as marks, half-marks, instalments, cargo lots, or a later memorial count.

The tally is early enough to command respect and damaged enough to support
almost any patient expert. A newly surfaced matching half should settle one
transaction, not the whole ransom.

### The Great Roll

The Common Clerk keeps a formal list of contributors commonly called the
**Great Roll of the Four Hundred**. It is a later copy of a copy, organised by
wards that had not yet taken their present form. Its defenders say a careful
copyist regularised old lane groups for use. Its critics call it a civic
genealogy written backward.

The roll contains prestigious names, anonymous collective entries, damaged
lines, and sums that do not reconcile without treating some entries as
security rather than payment. Families have sued over a single cramped word.
No serious clerk treats it as a transparent account; no politician can afford
to call it worthless.

### The White Inventory

Chapter books before and after F.54 differ in their silver. One leaf is
missing, several objects change description, and a later hand writes
**for peace** beside an entry whose original purpose cannot be recovered.

The inventory establishes movement of value, not heroic scale. It is the best
evidence that the Church gave something and the worst evidence for every
sermon describing exactly what.

### The cloth books

Several merchant families preserve or claim fragments listing cloth, coin,
and toll income associated with the peace. The most famous totals more than
the entire ransom if every line is read as a separate payment. Cloth interests
say this includes cost and later advances. Their enemies say it reveals
double collection. A third possibility is that it records security, advance,
repayment, and reassignment of the same value at different dates.

Authenticity does not make the reader's preferred arithmetic correct.

### The river shares

Boat families keep copied share deeds, service traditions, and oral lists of
free journeys supposedly owed against F.54 pledges. Some claims explain
privileges attested much later; others appear only when a toll is raised.

A family may sincerely preserve a false interpretation for twelve
generations. It may also preserve the substance of an agreement that civic
clerks omitted because it was performed rather than paid.

### The acquittance of Harne

Only copies survive of the receipt or peace instrument. The phrase **the
common at Ombreval** is central to the Bench account and absent from one Harne
copy. Seals described in later endorsements do not match one another. The
instrument certainly existed in some form, because both towns acted upon the
settlement. Its exact constitutional wording remains unavailable.

### The first Chest

The Bench House coffer called the first Common Chest is a civic relic, not an
archaeological certainty. Timber, hasps, feet, and internal trays belong to
different repairs. Claims that an ancient salt crust remains beneath its
lining are pageant talk.

Its continued use is the more interesting authenticity. A chest repaired into
a different object over centuries resembles the institution claiming descent
from F.54: materially discontinuous, socially treated as the same.

### Pennyhand tokens

Wood, lead, and base-metal tokens marked with a hand, three dots, a salt sack,
or a crude chain are sold and inherited as receipts for household payments.
Most are later Weighday badges, ward tokens, devotional substitutes, or plain
forgeries. A few could descend from collection tallies. None bears Ede's
verifiable hand.

Old paper and old metal both acquire ancestors in Ombreval when a lawsuit
needs them.

## The debtor rolls

The rolls matter because they do not merely say who contributed. They imply
who was owed.

At least four incompatible families of account survive:

- a civic roll that favours completed repayment and gifts to the common;
- Chapter copies that separate redeemable silver from alms;
- merchant books that preserve principal, courtesy, expenses, and toll
  assignments; and
- household and river lists that treat assessments or service as loans never
  properly discharged.

No version can be totalled without decisions about duplicated value,
conversion, damaged notation, and the difference between security and final
payment. Those decisions are political as well as technical.

By F.437, an ordinary unsecured claim to repayment from F.54 has little hope
in the Civic Measure Court. A claimant would need more than an old name and a
family story. A plausible case might concern a current toll share, a property
burden renewed in later deeds, an object still held as pledge, or proof that a
supposedly temporary privilege was never lawfully extinguished.

That is how an ancient debt can return without requiring a court to award
three hundred and eighty-three years of imagined interest.

## Memory in F.437

The event lies **383 years** in the past. People call it four centuries when
speaking loosely, but its four-hundredth anniversary will not fall until
F.454. Clerks and pedants correct this; tavern speakers do not care.

Every citizen knows the simple account: Harne chained the river, held the
salt-boats, demanded four hundred marks, and Ombreval paid together. Most also
know that the Church and town accuse one another of lying about the silver.
Detailed knowledge follows occupation and allegiance.

### At Gaudry's Audit

The Four Hundred Marks has no separate great feast in the shared calendar.
Its civic memory attaches chiefly to **Gaudry's Audit**, or Weighday, when
standards are proved and ledgers closed.

Custom varies with the year and the First Seat. Common elements include a
bowl of coarse salt beside the open account, display of the alleged Harne
link, reading a short founding preamble before current expenditure, and one
penny entered from each ward for poor relief. Critics call the penny an insult
when ward arrears are being collected. Benchers call it remembrance that no
sum is too small to require an account.

The best-known later preamble reads:

> The water was bound and the salt stood below us. No purse was the town's.
> Therefore the households reckoned, the trades pledged, the boats stood
> surety, and the Church opened silver. Four hundred marks went downriver.
> The salt came home. What remained was common.

Every sentence after the first two is disputed. That is why the preamble
works.

### At ward reckonings

Candidates invoke F.54 when demanding open books, opposing a levy, defending
one, or accusing creditors of buying policy. A bencher who hides expenditure
is said to be **putting the chain inside the Chest**. A speaker who demands
benefit without sharing cost is asked **where is your mark?** Neither phrase
is formal law.

The founding gives opposite sides a common vocabulary. The Bench says shared
danger permits shared assessment. The wards answer that assessment without a
public reckoning is Harne's demand in civic clothing.

### In the Chapter

The Chapter remembers reluctant cooperation followed by enduring civic
ingratitude. On suitable anniversaries a sermon notes that silver used in
peace served more souls than silver standing on a shelf. The town answers
that an institution which gave little has preached greatly on the return.

The old quarrel remains affectionate until money or jurisdiction is actually
at stake.

### On the river

Boat families remember that the town needs them most when pretending not to.
Some touch iron at a mooring before a disputed toll passage. Others keep a
link-shaped knot in a working line. No supernatural protection is claimed;
it is a reminder not to approach a chain without knowing who holds the winch.

Harne crews answer Ombreval boasts with **you paid, we opened**. Ombreval
crews answer **you closed, we remained**. Trade usually resumes before the
argument ends.

## Sayings, rhymes, and small customs

The event supplies ordinary language as well as constitutional rhetoric.

- **“To buy peace of Harne.”** To pay an extortionate price one cannot refuse.
- **“A Harne sum.”** A round demand whose arithmetic belongs entirely to the
  person making it.
- **“Where is your mark?”** Who bears the cost, or what gives you standing to
  decide?
- **“Put it to the common.”** Make a shared expense explicit and require a
  reckoning; sometimes sincere, sometimes a way to move a private cost onto
  everyone.
- **“The chain is in the paper.”** The immediate danger has passed but its
  debt or restriction remains.
- **“Paid in penny, remembered in plate.”** Said when a great institution
  receives credit for other people's small work.
- **“Four hundred exactly.”** A dry answer to a suspiciously convenient total.

Children use several versions of a counting rhyme:

> Link by link and mark by mark,
> salt came home before the dark;
> plate for priest and cloth for hall,
> penny, oar, and debt for all.

Harne children reverse the last line to **coin for Harne, debt for all**.
Ombreval children insist this does not scan and shout it anyway.

At some household tables, the first pinch taken from a newly opened winter
salt sack is placed beside the account book or rent tally before it goes into
the pot. People explain this as thrift, remembrance, ward custom, or something
their grandmother did. It is not a religious rite and does not need to be
universal.

## The dispute with Harne in the present

Harne is close enough to know Ombreval's story and near enough not to be
impressed by it. Its merchants accept the city's weights, trade in its
markets, and mock its founding between bargains. Ombreval does the reverse:
it curses the castle and relies on the downstream road.

The present lord takes a lawful, published toll. Closing the river again would
breach later peace and threaten every interest along the Serle. This makes a
literal repetition of F.54 unlikely. It does not make the memory harmless.
A delayed salt convoy, disputed toll schedule, damaged seal, or careless
Harne visitor can make an old insult immediate.

A visitor need only say one of the following in the wrong square:

- the chain was a lawful boom;
- the boats were attached for smuggling;
- Ombreval paid only after Harne reduced an honest account;
- the famous link was forged in Ombreval;
- the Common Bench descends from Harne's collection order; or
- the city celebrates being the only debtor vain enough to call a receipt a
  constitution.

None requires the speaker to believe it. Mockery is inexpensive until a crowd
forms.

## Present-day conflicts and story uses

The Four Hundred Marks works best when evidence changes who has standing,
money, honour, or influence now. It should not become a hunt for the one true
ledger.

### A revived river share

A boat family produces a copied pledge and a newly found tally half. It claims
that an ancestor's share bought a perpetual exemption from a class of river
dues. The Chest says the exemption ended when the Cut was made. A present
freight broker has already bought the family's expected right at a discount.

The questions are current even if the F.54 instrument is genuine: Was the
promise perpetual? Did a later settlement replace it? Can a common body bind
all future residents forever under emergency pressure?

### The thirty missing household names

A damaged leaf appears to expand an anonymous ward contribution into thirty
named households, several ancestral to living poor families. The names may be
an honest copy from a lost tally, a later memorial, or a forgery intended to
support relief claims. Publishing it would restore people to the story even
if no legal debt follows. Suppressing it protects the Great Roll and several
prestigious genealogies.

### Harne's counter-roll

A Harne clerk or merchant brings a copy that itemises the settlement as toll
arrears, damages, and costs. Its total is four hundred marks. Some entries
correspond disturbingly well to Ombreval merchant books; others use terms or
weights that may be later. The visitor offers inspection only under conditions
that guarantee a public audience.

The document need not be wholly true or false. Its best use is to force
Ombreval factions to explain why their own records share its inconvenient
details.

### The link is replaced

Before Weighday, the displayed Harne link is stolen and a visually convincing
substitute left in its place. Was the thief trying to expose lax custody,
prevent examination, create a relic for sale, or swap a false object for a
genuine one? Finding either link does not establish which first touched the
Serle.

### The Church asks redemption

A Chapter inventory is read as showing that silver was pledged, not given,
and never redeemed. The Chapter does not demand four centuries of money; it
asks the Common Chest to fund a visible work or poor endowment in recognition.
The Bench calls it a disguised appropriation. Household speakers ask why
their own unrepaid claims receive no altar or alms fund.

### A new salt delay

Flood, grounding, labour dispute, or a quarrel over the published Harne toll
delays winter salt. Prices rise. The Bench calls an emergency common
reckoning, and every speaker uses F.54 to support an opposite plan. The city
can repeat the founding procedure without repeating the founding event.

This is a strong way to make the lore active: old language shapes a practical
decision about stores, credit, price controls, convoy protection, and who is
heard.

### Public insult

A Harne visitor mocks Ede Pennyhand or calls the Common Chest a ransom box.
The immediate issue may be assault, apology, safe conduct, or a market ban.
The deeper issue is who is allowed to mock a story Ombreval residents mock
among themselves every day.

### A bencher's ancestry

A candidate claims descent from a named founder in the Great Roll. A rival
finds evidence that the name belonged to a creditor repaid at exceptional
profit, or to a later copyist's patron. The interesting choice is not whether
ancestry grants a rightful seat—it does not—but what the candidate's use of
the story reveals about present alliances.

## Evidence principles for plots

Stories using F.54 should follow these rules:

1. **Authentic does not mean complete.** A genuine merchant entry records the
   merchant's transaction, not the whole town's contribution.
2. **False does not mean useless.** A later household list may preserve names
   omitted by official copies even if its totals were improved for a lawsuit.
3. **The same value can appear in several accounts.** Security, advance,
   payment, assessment, and repayment are different stages.
4. **Expertise is interested.** Clerks, smiths, boat workers, money dealers,
   and Chapter archivists can identify different features and have different
   reasons to emphasise them.
5. **A verdict settles a present claim, not history.** The Civic Measure Court
   can decide who receives a toll share without declaring one faction the true
   founder.
6. **No relic should prove a hero.** A chain link, signature, tally half, or
   inventory gap can move probabilities and politics, never reveal that one
   hidden person saved everyone.
7. **Material stakes come first.** Salt, freight, winter stores, credit,
   silver weight, labour, and toll access make the dispute matter.

## Knowledge by social position

For smart-actor context, knowledge should be distributed rather than giving
every citizen the full author-facing history.

### What nearly everyone knows

- Harne chained the Serle and held Ombreval's salt-boats in F.54.
- Ombreval paid four hundred marks and still says the price with resentment.
- The first Common Reckoning and Common Chest are said to come from the
  collection.
- Every group claims it paid most, especially the Church, merchants, boat
  families, and ordinary households.
- “To buy peace of Harne” means paying an extortionate price one cannot refuse.

### What a civic officer or experienced clerk knows

- A mark was a weight of account, not one coin.
- The Great Roll is a later, internally inconsistent copy.
- Modern Bench procedure cannot be documented continuously back to F.54.
- Duplicate totals may represent layers of financing rather than simple fraud.
- Ancient unsecured repayment claims have little force without later deeds or
  a present burden attached to them.

### What a boat-family member is likely to say

- The detained crews and their kin faced the danger while inland people
  debated.
- Boat shares and free freight are undervalued in official accounts.
- Harne lowered the chain because river people made keeping the boats costly.
- The civic link is probably genuine, and the clerks would say otherwise even
  if rust from Harne still stained it.

### What a cloth merchant or money dealer is likely to say

- Pennies cannot ransom cargo until somebody converts and guarantees them.
- Ready silver and trusted cloth credit made the agreement possible.
- Later privilege was repayment for real risk, not theft from the common.
- People who condemn lenders in peace look for them first in winter.

### What a poorer householder is likely to say

- Great contributors had their names copied; small contributors became an
  anonymous ward sum.
- Households paid once into the collection and again through tolls and
  assessments.
- The right to hear common accounts belongs to payers, not as a favour from
  benchers.
- Ede Pennyhand may not have existed exactly as told, but someone like her
  certainly paid and was not named.

### What a Chapter officer is likely to say

- Church silver supplied trustworthy weight when promises were plentiful and
  metal scarce.
- Chapter rooms, clerks, oaths, and custody made an agreement possible.
- Civic memory minimises the Church because the Bench needs a secular birth.
- The chronicle's joke about giving little proves candour, not guilt.

### What a person from Harne is likely to say

- Ombreval calls every toll extortion when another town collects it.
- The four hundred settled a claim; Harne did not invent Ombreval's debts.
- The link and Ede Pennyhand are civic theatre.
- If paying made Ombreval free, Harne is pleased to have sold freedom at a fair
  price.

## Tone and continuity guardrails

- Keep the event secular and material. Do not connect the chain, salt, marks,
  or founding documents to the impossible light.
- Keep the humiliation. Ombreval paid; it did not secretly win a battle and
  disguise the victory.
- Keep collective action unequal. The Reckoning was unusually broad, not a
  modern democracy or universal franchise.
- Keep the Church contribution real but relatively small. Its timing may have
  mattered more than its total.
- Keep the mark an accounting weight, never a purse-sized denomination of four
  hundred ordinary coins.
- Keep Harne politically human. Its lord exploited leverage; its present
  inhabitants are neighbours and trading partners, not hereditary monsters.
- Keep institutional descent plausible but unproved. F.54 is an origin claimed
  through function and memory, not a perfectly preserved constitution.
- Let several factions be right about the same value at different stages.
- Do not resolve the documentary mystery into one forged roll and one wholly
  true roll.
- Make present disputes cost something: revenue, standing, relief, toll access,
  credit, office, or public honour.

## The founding paradox

Ombreval's favourite secular story begins with failure. The town could not
guard its necessary road, could not agree who owed the danger, and could not
pay without borrowing against people not yet born. It submitted to an
extortionate demand.

What it made from that failure was durable. People who had no common purse
created one because a rival had priced them as one community. People assessed
for common need demanded a common account. Creditors forced the town to
continue meeting after the boats came home. Local delegates became habitual
representatives. A receipt given by an enemy became evidence that Ombreval
could contract in its own name.

The city was not founded because four hundred marks were paid. The settlement
at the ford was older, the Lanthorn's first stone already fifty-three years
laid, and every household had a history before the Chest. Nor was the modern
Common Bench born complete on a mason's plank.

The founding claim is narrower and better: in F.54, Ombreval first discovered
that if a burden was truly common, somebody had to count it in public—and that
whoever kept the account afterwards would help decide what the city was.
