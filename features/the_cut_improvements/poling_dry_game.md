# Poling dry — the Cut game

**Status:** design proposal, 2026-07-28. **Nothing here is canon yet.** This resolves
`features/design_the_cut_game.md`, which has been parked since the phrase "enforcement of the Cut
game" turned up in `features/lore_ward_politics.md` with no definition anywhere in the corpus.

Everything in §3–§7 that is not marked *(canon)* was invented for this document and for the
explainer video that goes with it. The lore update follows once the design is accepted — see §10.

Depends on `features/the_cut_improvements/the_cut_kerb.md`: the game is played on the crown of the
cartway, and the kerb doc deliberately left the camber out for this feature to specify.

---

## 1. What it is, in one paragraph

An iron barrel hoop — **the band** — is set on the crown of the Cut's cartway, aimed with a
boatman's pole, and struck. Nobody may touch it after the strike. The camber holds it on the centre
line, and a true band runs well past a hundred metres down the only straight street in Ombreval,
in full view of everybody standing on the margin. Then people bet on it.

It is the children's game their fathers played, played by the fathers, for money, in the bed of the
river those fathers used to work.

---

## 2. Why this and not the other three seeds

`design_the_cut_game.md` offered hoop-poling, kerb-pitch, wet-or-dry, and something
eel-market-adjacent. The argument that settles it is a gameplay argument, not a lore one:

**Poling dry is the only one of the four that could not be played anywhere else in the city.**

Every other street in Ombreval doglegs, pinches and changes width between one house and the next —
`major` tier tops out at 8.5 m and bends; the Cut is 20 m of road ribbon and runs 748 m without a
turn. A hoop needs a straight run and a crown. Kerb-pitch could happen in any yard. Wet-or-dry
could be chased down any lane. Poling dry is *made of* the thing that makes the Cut strange, which
means it is also the thing that makes the Cut worth walking down.

It also satisfies the parked doc's own three tests: it is played in the open street, it is marginal
enough to crack down on, and it is loved enough that cracking down costs votes.

And it is emphatically **not the Dry Race** — that is once a year, on a cradle, at Marenstide, and
it means something else. Poling dry is a Tuesday.

## 3. The equipment

**The band.** An iron barrel hoop off **Sibbe Tarn**'s bench on Tanners' Slip *(canon: Sibbe Tarn,
hoop-setter; the Tarns are coopers and tanners in the Reed Ward)*. Bands are not made for the game;
they are made for barrels, and a hoop that failed its cask is a hoop that is free. A good band is
true, heavy at the rim, and worth keeping. The best are named.

**The pole.** Here is the whole feature in one object: the pole is **a boatman's**, shod at the tip
for a riverbed, and **it has no boat**.

The Serle was moved outside the south wall in F.362 and the bed filled between F.363 and F.369
*(canon)*. The boat-families did not lose the trade — Ewart Alder still poles three days up to
Brede and comes home with wool to the gunwales *(canon)* — they lost the neighbourhood. The men
poling dry are holding the correct tool, on the correct line, over sixty-eight years of rubble.

Nobody in the fiction says any of this out loud. It is in the objects.

## 4. The course, and the vocabulary

The kerb feature divides the Cut's 20 m into a 10 m cartway and 6.7 m of margin either side. The
game lives on that division:

- **The crown** — the camber down the centre of the cartway. It is what holds a band on line, and
  it is the reason the game exists.
- **The margin** — flat. A band put down on the margin travels about four metres and lies over.
  The crowd stands here; the game cannot.
- **The kerb** — where a leaning band dies.

**The wagering vocabulary is river vocabulary, and that is the point.** Bets are called on the
**north bank** or the **south bank** — which side of the crown the band dies on. There has been no
water in the Cut since before anyone playing was born, and they still call the banks. That single
word is the whole emotional argument of the feature, and it costs nothing to ship.

*(The Cut runs west to east — Chain Bridge quarter at `z = +325` to the Old Sluice at `z = -422` —
so its banks are north and south. The compass here is left-handed: north is `+x`, east is `−z`.)*

### A run

1. **Set** the band on the crown.
2. **Call the bank** — north or south — out loud, before the strike. This is a commitment, not a
   prediction of the winner; it is what makes the second market playable.
3. **Strike** with the pole.
4. **Hands off.** Any touch after the strike voids the run. The camber does the rest.

A true band runs 100 m+. A band struck off the crown leans, finds the kerb, and is down in single
figures.

### The three markets

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **The length** | How far before it lies down. | Settled by pacing it out — which is why a run has an audience walking beside it. |
| **The bank** | Which side of the crown it dies on, called before the strike. | The caller's market. Skill, mostly. |
| **The crossing** | Whether it survives a side lane. | Where the money is. |

**The crossing** deserves its own note, because it is the best thing in the design. Every lane that
meets the Cut has worn a shallow trough across the cartway, and a trough is a crown upside down.
A band arrives running true, loses its crown for half a metre, and picks a side — and in
sixty-eight years nobody on that street has learned to predict which. It is an honest coin flip
produced entirely by the geometry of cart traffic. No dice, no dealer, nothing anyone can rig.

## 5. The law, which is the actual mechanic

This is where the feature earns its place, and canon does almost all of the work.

`lore/core_lore/secular_government.md` *(canon)*: *"Public gaming for small stakes is legal in
licensed taverns and gaming rooms. Marked pieces, rigged play, unlicensed large-stake rooms and
lending against a player's person are offences. Informal children's games and household wagers need
no licence."*

Read that against poling dry:

- It is **not in a room**, so the tavern licence does not reach it.
- It is **not a room**, so "unlicensed large-stake room" does not reach it either.
- It **is** the children's game, which needs no licence at all — and the men playing it will say so,
  and they are not lying.

**So the Bench cannot easily prosecute the play. It can only prosecute the place.** The offence
actually available is *obstruction of the cartway* — the oldest rule on the Cut, and the one the
Bench already enforces there with a notice.

That is why "enforcement of the Cut game" is a discretionary act rather than a statute, and why it
can swing with a ward election. Nobody has to change the law. A bencher only has to decide how
literally the cartway rule is read this year.

And the game **cannot dodge it**, because the crown is the cartway. Move the play to the margin and
the band dies in four metres. The thing that makes it possible is the thing that makes it illegal.
There is no version of poling dry that is lawful in the open street.

### The line it can cross

One offence *does* reach it cleanly: **lending against a player's person**. A book that stays in
sparks is winkable; a book that starts lending is not. That is the escalation seam — the point at
which a sergeant who has spent a year looking the other way stops being able to, and it puts a hard
edge on an otherwise soft, political offence.

## 6. Who owns it

The parked doc asked whether the game belongs to one ward or travels three. Answer: **it is played
the whole length and belongs to the Reed Ward by blood.**

The pole is a boat-family object, the bands come off a Reed Ward bench, and the play runs west into
the Weigh Ward at the Tallage and past the Cinder end at the Chain Bridge. Three wards' benchers
therefore get asked about it every reckoning, and only one of them has family in it.

That one is a gift, because all three names are already canon:

- **Sibbe Tarn**, hoop-setter — makes the bands.
- **Gude Tarn**, who *"runs an illegal game"* — runs the play and holds the book.
- **Idonea Tarn**, 54, ledger-keeper, and **one of the Reed Ward's two benchers** — votes on
  prosecuting it.

The family that supplies the sport also holds a vote on whether it is prosecuted. That was sitting
in `lore/families/family_tarn.md` the whole time; this feature just names what those three lines add
up to. (The Tarns also have **Tobin**, a smuggler, which is already the reason the ward's postern
vote is *"never quite about smuggling out loud"* — the same shape, twice.)

## 7. Two names for one afternoon

The strongest legible thing the feature produces is a **vocabulary tell**.

- A neighbour says: *"they were poling dry down by the Tally Bridge."*
- A notice says: *"unlicensed poling on the cartway."*

Same men, same band, same hour. Which phrase a character reaches for tells the player where that
character stands, in one word, without anyone explaining a political position. For LLM actors this
is close to free: give each character the phrase their household would use, and let the player
learn to hear it.

At a ward reckoning, somebody stands up and asks which of the two it was. That is the vote.

## 8. What the player does

Gameplay first — every one of these works with verbs that already ship (`say`, `offer_item`,
`go_to`, `make_sound`, `gesture`, `raise_notice`, `settle_notice`, `seize`):

- **Watch one.** The Cut is the only place in the city where you can see something happen 100 m
  away and follow it the whole way. That is the appeal, and it needs no interaction at all.
- **Bet.** Put a spark into Gude Tarn's book and call a market. The crossing pays; the bank rewards
  reading the crown.
- **Roll.** Set, call the bank, strike. A player who learns where the crown is true gets better at
  this by *looking at the street*, not by levelling anything.
- **Pace it out.** Settling the length is a walk beside the band with everyone else — the most
  sociable thirty seconds in the game.
- **Be caught.** The play is an obstruction. Arguing with a sergeant about it is how most players
  will first meet `raise_notice`, and it is a much better introduction to the Bench than a theft.
- **Keep the watch.** Segwin Mott's south beat is *(canon)* Maren's Green at Dayspring, the Tallage
  at High Wick, Maren's Green again at Lamplight. The play has a rhythm around a known patrol, and a
  player who learns the beat is playing the real game.
- **Ask about it.** See §7.

### The fixture list

Play runs on the quiet days — **Second, Fourth, Seventh**. On **Lowmarket** the markets open at the
Tallage and at Maren's Green *(canon)* and the street fills with people, money, and a bench
sergeant: the biggest crowd, the biggest stakes, and the worst possible afternoon to be caught.
That risk calculation is the schedule, and it needs no new systems — it falls out of the clock the
sim already runs.

## 9. Milestones (sketch only)

- **M0 — the object and the words.** Bands exist as items off the Tarn bench; the cast knows the
  two names and uses the one their household would. Nothing rolls yet. This alone makes the street
  talk about itself.
- **M1 — a run you can watch.** A band that rolls the crown, an audience on the margin, a length
  paced out at the end. Needs the camber (kerb doc, deferred here).
- **M2 — the book.** Wagers in sparks across the three markets, held by Gude Tarn.
- **M3 — the law.** The play registers as an obstruction; the beat can end it; the notice settles or
  escalates.
- **M4 — the reckoning.** Enforcement becomes a ward-level posture that can change, and the
  vocabulary tell becomes a readable poll.

M0 and M1 are the feature. M2–M4 are what make it matter.
