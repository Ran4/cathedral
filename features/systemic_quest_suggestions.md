# Systemic quest suggestions

These are possible authored situations for Cathedralbevy, not approved canon, a release plan, or twenty-four
features that should all be built. Names, places and particulars must be reconciled with the lore before any
suggestion becomes a quest spec.

The intended design shape is:

> The designer authors the people, conflict, stakes, legal facts and durable outcomes. The player supplies the
> method using shared systems: schedules, routes, speech, appointments, witnesses, items, custody, favors,
> weather, law and crime.

A useful quest leaves behind more than completed dialogue. It changes a route, schedule, price, access right,
occupation, relationship, public fact, law or physical place that later play can use.

## Selection rules

A suggestion is worth promoting into a full quest only when it can answer all of these:

1. What forces the player to move through the city rather than solve it in one conversation?
2. Which existing general-purpose systems support at least three materially different approaches?
3. What can go wrong without producing a reload-shaped failure?
4. Who can witness, misunderstand, exploit or publicize what the player does?
5. What changes in ordinary city life after resolution?
6. What new capability or complication can matter in a later quest?

Do not promote an idea merely because its lore premise is interesting. A quest needs playable verbs, pressure
and aftermath.

## 01 — The condemned bakehouse

**Authored situation.** A viewer intends to close a popular bakehouse before a feast because its firebreak and
buckets are inadequate.

**Systemic approaches.** Recruit a mason, borrow equipment, assemble a Cinder witness and stage a fire test;
negotiate a temporary licence; bribe the viewer; forge an inspection mark; discredit the complaint; or move the
baking elsewhere before the office closes.

**Persistent aftermath.** A lawful repair creates a safer oven, jobs, cheap bread and perhaps a kitchen key or
sleeping place. A forgery preserves those benefits immediately, but a later fire can expose it and make both
the baker and player materially responsible.

## 02 — The bale that gained forty pounds

> **Promoted 2026-08-30** → `features/quest_the_bale_that_gained_forty_pounds/` (spec, ten worked playthroughs with storyboards, and a four-page implementation summary). The text below is the original idea, kept as written.

**Playable hook.** At High Wick, Hugh Crake's road party reaches the Wool Gate with a corded bale of broadcloth
that the Tallage weighed and sealed only hours earlier. The gate beam says it is forty pounds heavier than the
manifest. Officers call it smuggling, turn the cart around before a gathering crowd and impound the load until a
public opening at the next Dayspring. Six loom households have lawful bolts inside and will not be paid while it
remains evidence; Hugh will also owe for a missed Brede departure. The player meets a blocked cart, frightened
oxen and porters pulling at the same rope — forty pounds that plainly came from somewhere — rather than a
merchant explaining a fee rule.

**The authored truth.** Clemence Hobbe has woven one forty-pound bolt from fleece bought outside Ewart Skell's
putting-out system: her first chance to sell work in her own name. Renn Crake promised to put it on Hugh's
returning cart, then found the Tallage had already sealed the bale. Desperate to honor the promise and cover his
own freight debts, Renn diverted the cart, opened the bale with warehouse access, inserted Clemence's bolt and
imitated the seal. Hugh did not authorize it. The accusation against him is false, but a crime did occur; clearing
him can expose both the weaver's bid for independence and the broker whose family keys are already in debt. These
are authored facts, not a randomized culprit, but the player may reveal, conceal, legalize or exploit them.

**Systemic play.** The cart's route is the investigation: Tallage, Tally Bridge stores, Wickmarket, the Draper's
Reach and Wool Gate. Walk its custody chain, catch a porter before he goes home, compare cut cord and copied wax,
count Ewart's remaining bolts, enter the warehouse with a borrowed or stolen key, accompany the impounded bale,
or put Clemence, Renn and Hugh in one room where their stories cannot all stand. A lawful player can stage a
neutral reweigh with Bertran Hobbe's proved weights, pay the omitted duty and amend the manifest before witnesses.
A social player can make Renn confess without naming Clemence, persuade Ewart to buy the independent bolt, or
turn the six unpaid households into a public crowd. A covert player can remove the bolt, replace the false seal,
alter the custody copy or route the cart through another gate. A predatory player can sell the truth to Ewart,
take Renn's warehouse key as the price of silence, or leave Hugh carrying the crime in return for Clemence's
future loyalty. Evidence remains with objects, places and people; it never becomes a glowing clue list.

**Pressure and recoverable failure.** The public opening occurs whether the player is ready or not. Witnesses
keep their schedules, Renn may run when accused, and Hugh must choose at the Waning between abandoning the cargo
and missing the road. Arriving late changes the problem rather than ending it: once the bolt is found, play can
continue through a surety hearing, escape, debt bargain or attempt to prove who broke custody. A forged seal may
fool an officer now and fail later before the porter who saw the player borrow wax.

**Persistent aftermath.** A lawful amendment may let innocent owners recover goods and give Clemence a direct
customer; Ewart can retaliate by cutting prices, denying wool or trying to absorb her workshop. Full exposure
clears Hugh but may cost Renn his trade, keys or freedom, changing who brokers cargo each week. A cover-up creates
a real smuggling route and possibly a copied warehouse key, while leaving everyone vulnerable to an audit.
Discrediting the Tallage makes freight slower and dearer; sacrificing Hugh can stop the Brede cart and constrict
raw-wool supply. The answer remains visible in carts, stock, wages, access, relationships and who stands at the
weigh-beam, not merely in a completed-quest entry.

## 03 — The keeper who stopped coming

**Authored situation.** A cistern keeper has vanished and the queue is deteriorating into bribery and fights.

**Systemic approaches.** Find the keeper and learn why they fled; recruit a temporary replacement; operate the
fixture personally; obtain wages from the Common Chest; reorganize the queue with lawful marks; or let a local
strongman control access.

**Persistent aftermath.** Hundreds of daily routes change with the keeper and queue. A replacement becomes a
real employed citizen with a new round, while an informal controller creates favoritism, debt and future
conflict.

## 04 — A funeral at a closed postern

**Authored situation.** A poor family must carry a body through the postern after its legal closing hour.

**Systemic approaches.** Obtain an exemption, persuade the gatekeeper to remain, pay a toll, recruit enough
bearers for another route, use a larger procession as cover, borrow a key, or open the gate illegally.

**Persistent aftermath.** The city may recognize a funeral exemption, lengthen gate hours or increase patrols.
The family may owe the player; the gatekeeper may lose their post; the postern may become harder to exploit.

## 05 — The procession and the failing bridge

**Authored situation.** A major procession is scheduled to cross a bridge that a carpenter believes unsafe.

**Systemic approaches.** Repair or brace it, obtain a closure, redirect the procession, reduce the crowd, stage
a public demonstration of the danger, or suppress the warning to avoid civic embarrassment.

**Persistent aftermath.** The bridge remains a shortcut, closes for weeks, becomes guarded or fails under a
later load. The route chosen for the procession determines which wards witnessed it and which markets lost
their trade.

## 06 — The apprentice accused of stealing silver

**Authored situation.** Physical evidence implicates an apprentice, but its custody history has gaps.

**Systemic approaches.** Trace everyone who handled it, intercept a witness on their daily round, pay surety,
pressure the master, plant replacement silver, arrange a confession, expose a family interest, or help the
apprentice escape.

**Persistent aftermath.** Acquittal can earn workshop access and a loyal contact. Escape creates a fugitive ally,
an angry guild and a watch that associates the player with the crime.

## 07 — The unlicensed upper rooms

**Authored situation.** Pilgrims are sleeping in unsafe rooms above workshops and undercutting licensed lodging.

**Systemic approaches.** Conduct honest inspections, warn hosts in advance, relocate pilgrims, forge a compliant
register, negotiate temporary feast licences, expose only a rival's rooms, or secure a voluntary contribution
to public wear and fire safety.

**Persistent aftermath.** Enforcement changes where visitors sleep, which taverns fill, lodging prices, fire
risk and the routes of temporary residents. Selective enforcement creates witnesses and enemies who know why
they were chosen.

## 08 — The widow's eviction

**Authored situation.** A landlord possesses a valid debt judgment and intends to remove a household before
winter.

**Systemic approaches.** Pay the debt, find an omitted payment witness, challenge the property boundary, obtain
a guild surety, pressure the landlord, relocate the family first, or prove that another party owns part of the
building.

**Persistent aftermath.** The house remains occupied, becomes empty, gains a new tenant or becomes available to
the player. The household, landlord, neighbors and court remember the method, not only the result.

## 09 — The false peal

**Authored situation.** A bell rang at the wrong office, moving workers, carriers and watch patrols at the wrong
time.

**Systemic approaches.** Investigate the tower, compare hearing positions, inspect the rope, protect or expose
the ringer, prove political sabotage—or exploit the confusion to complete a different obligation.

**Persistent aftermath.** Control of the tower, trust in particular bells and the speed of future alarms can all
change. A false alarm may also establish a repeatable but increasingly risky way to move crowds.

## 10 — The guild election

**Authored situation.** Two candidates contest an office controlling licences, apprenticeships and employment.

**Systemic approaches.** Bring members to the meeting, protect or expose records, arrange testimony, solve an
immediate trade problem, call in precise debts, or keep a candidate occupied somewhere else when the count
begins.

**Persistent aftermath.** The winner changes prices, access, inspections and daily work assignments. The loser
remains in the city with allies and a grievance rather than vanishing after an ending scene.

## 11 — The watch's private toll

**Authored situation.** A watch patrol is informally charging carriers to pass a lane.

**Systemic approaches.** Gather testimony, catch the patrol before respected witnesses, bait them with marked
coins, report them through the law ladder, blackmail them, organize carriers to use another route, or negotiate
an unofficial exemption.

**Persistent aftermath.** Exposure changes the patrol roster and restores the route. Blackmail leaves the patrol
in place but gives the player a dangerous favor—and an enemy who knows exactly why.

## 12 — The prisoner transfer

**Authored situation.** A prisoner must be moved from the Stone House to a public hearing.

**Systemic approaches.** Escort them lawfully, recruit sureties, alter the route, conceal a tool, cause a crowd
obstruction, exchange clothing, challenge the notice, borrow a service key, or arrange an ambush made of words
rather than weapons.

**Persistent aftermath.** The prisoner becomes a lawful contact, a fugitive, a debtor or an enemy. Security,
routes and key custody around later transfers adapt to what happened.

## 13 — The fish cart before Lamplight

**Authored situation.** A load of fish is spoiling while a gate dispute prevents entry.

**Systemic approaches.** Secure an exemption, prove the load's origin, divide it into lawful handbarrows, redirect
it to another market, hide it inside a procession, borrow a gate key, or bribe the gate staff.

**Persistent aftermath.** Success lowers food prices at one market and may raise them elsewhere. Failure creates
waste, hunger, debt and an immediate smuggling opportunity.

## 14 — The locked grain store

**Authored situation.** Grain prices are rising and somebody claims a merchant is withholding stock.

**Systemic approaches.** Inspect the warehouse lawfully, find the owner or keyholder, recruit porters, expose
false accounts, spread a rumor that forces an early sale, steal and distribute sacks, copy the key, or discover
that the alleged hoard does not exist.

**Persistent aftermath.** Bread prices, cart traffic, public order and the merchant's behavior change. A forced
release helps people immediately but may leave no reserve for the next shortage.

## 15 — The colored water

**Authored situation.** Dye appears in a public cistern and an entire trade is being blamed.

**Systemic approaches.** Trace the water physically, inspect outfalls, follow dye carts, operate sluices, compare
work schedules, bring ward representatives together, enter a locked work yard, or conceal the actual source.

**Persistent aftermath.** The responsible workshop is fined, closed, repaired or protected. A false accusation
can become established public memory even while the sim retains the underlying truth.

## 16 — The disputed standard weight

**Authored situation.** Traders suspect a public standard is wrong, but proving it could invalidate decades of
transactions.

**Systemic approaches.** Arrange a neutral procedure, transport comparison weights, recruit credible viewers,
sabotage the test, cause a lawful recusal, secure the standards-room key, or adopt a replacement standard
without declaring the old one fraudulent.

**Persistent aftermath.** The quest need never reveal an ultimate truth. Its outcome changes public trust,
future inspections, access to standards and which witnesses institutions consider credible.

## 17 — The passage someone wants demolished

**Authored situation.** A covered passage is a vital shortcut, but one owner claims it is dangerous while another
claims a right of access.

**Systemic approaches.** Locate deeds, arrange an inspection, brace it temporarily, organize users to protest,
sabotage it, purchase the disputed right, obtain its gate key, or demonstrate that closing it breaks a public
round.

**Persistent aftermath.** The physical map changes: the passage remains open, becomes restricted, collapses or
is replaced by a longer route. NPC schedules and market traffic respond.

## 18 — Fire at Snuffing

**Authored situation.** Trapped residents, a threatened workshop, public records, a sealed reserve and too few
ladders all demand attention at once.

**Systemic approaches.** Wake workers, break or unlock the reserve, redirect crowds, recruit bucket lines, use a
writ, sacrifice property, rescue records or people, and decide which lane receives the limited water.

**Persistent aftermath.** Buildings, homes and occupations may permanently disappear. Survivors remember what
the player prioritized, while destroyed records create new legal uncertainty.

## 19 — The flooding lane

**Authored situation.** Opening one channel during a downpour protects a market but directs water toward poorer
cellars.

**Systemic approaches.** Recruit labor, clear another route, warn households, sacrifice stored goods, misuse a
writ, unlock an emergency fitting, divert the market, or accept one district's loss.

**Persistent aftermath.** The next days contain altered paths, displaced residents, closed businesses,
emergency work and concrete blame. There is no abstract good flood ending.

## 20 — The dangerous pamphlet

**Authored situation.** A text about the impossible light is circulating before a major sermon.

**Systemic approaches.** Find the printer, copy it, destroy it, replace pages, protect its carriers, deliver it to
the Chapter, enter a locked archive, or arrange a public demonstration of one narrow claim.

**Persistent aftermath.** Sermons, patrol attention, clandestine meetings and the people seeking the player all
change. Suppression can make the text more desirable rather than removing it.

## 21 — A signal made from impossible light

**Authored situation.** Someone must send a message across the city without using a runner.

**Systemic approaches.** Learn when a cathedral beam reaches a roof, acquire mirrors or polished shields, open
specific shutters, obtain roof access and arrange for the recipient to be watching at the correct office.

**Persistent aftermath.** Completing the operation reveals a reusable optical route. Later quests can use that
knowledge for warnings, conspiracies, navigation or false signals.

## 22 — The missing child who is not stationary

**Authored situation.** A child disappeared during a market office, but witnesses saw them following different
familiar workers.

**Systemic approaches.** Reconstruct population movement, follow a baker's cart, ask people who genuinely know
nearby passages, seed a carefully worded search message, use a roof vantage, or ring an alarm that may frighten
the child into hiding.

**Persistent aftermath.** The search changes family trust and market routines and may reveal a shortcut known
only to local children. The player gains usable geographical knowledge rather than detective XP.

## 23 — The marriage that joins two hostile trades

**Authored situation.** A proposed marriage would settle one feud while threatening several commercial
alliances.

**Systemic approaches.** Carry terms faithfully or alter them, protect the dowry, arrange a private meeting,
invite witnesses, expose another attachment, obtain a private-room key, settle a debt, or ensure one party
misses the ceremony.

**Persistent aftermath.** A new household and relationship network forms—or two families blame the player.
Homes, shops, debts, schedules and invitations can all change.

## 24 — The unidentified corpse

**Authored situation.** Identifying a body determines inheritance, debt and ownership of a workshop.

**Systemic approaches.** Trace clothing and objects, compare work schedules, locate witnesses, enter the
workshop, suppress an identity, substitute a plausible name, alter custody records or insist that the inquest
remain unresolved.

**Persistent aftermath.** The chosen identity determines who inherits the building, assumes the debt, becomes
homeless and controls the workshop. Later courts also remember which evidence and witnesses were accepted.

## Cross-quest progression these suggestions can create

The reward is not a generic relationship or charisma score. Examples include:

- a bakehouse key that grants food, shelter and a route between two lanes;
- a bell-tower key that creates a roof shortcut and access to civic signals;
- knowledge of one patrol's shift-change gap;
- knowledge that a drain connects two places whose owners publicly deny it;
- a Line-keeper willing to authenticate water work;
- a porter owing exactly one cart journey;
- a clerk owing a private introduction but not a favorable ruling;
- a fugitive who can guide the player through the night city;
- an enemy courier who now reaches shared contacts first;
- a guild that discourages members from sheltering the player;
- a room whose position changes storage, visitors, dawn position and access;
- a new toll, patrol, queue, opening hour or route that alters later plans.

The strongest test is:

> Does resolving this quest leave behind a new verb, route, pressure, access right or human relationship that
> materially changes how another quest can be solved?

If not, it may still be a good story, but it is not doing enough work for the game's systemic campaign.
