## The broad history

Before F.362, Ombreval had around 15,000 inhabitants.

The Great Rains of F.362 wrecked the Cut, flooded lower districts, spoiled stores, damaged foundations, and ultimately forced the Serle outside the walls. The city survived, but part of its commercial geography never recovered. Warehouses emptied, river families
relocated, and the population began gradually declining.

Then came the Great Hail of F.415.

For perhaps ten or fifteen minutes, extraordinarily large hailstones fell over Ombreval and its nearest fields. These were not merely fist-sized stones: some were heavy enough to smash tile, punch through timber roofs, break scaffolding, and collapse already weakened
upper floors. Thousands died almost at once—people caught in streets and squares, but also families crushed when roofs and party walls failed.

It need never be established whether this was supernatural. It was physically real, extraordinarily localized, and so improbable that nobody agrees what it meant.

## A possible population trajectory

- Before F.362: approximately 15,000
- After the Great Rains and river diversion: approximately 11,000–12,000
- Killed during or immediately after the F.415 hail: approximately 2,000–3,000
- Dead later from injuries, exposure, ruined food stores, fires, and collapsing buildings: perhaps another 1,000
- Departed during the following twenty years: approximately 3,000–4,000
- Present population in F.437: approximately 5,000

This makes the Long Departure a consequence rather than a magical compulsion. People leave because Ombreval has become expensive, damaged, half-empty, economically weaker, and widely believed to be accursed.

## Why the hail was so destructive

Several factors compound it:

- Dense streets gave people little warning or view of the approaching storm.
- Jettied houses created cascades of broken tile and timber.
- The hail shattered cathedral glass, market roofs, chimneys, and water conduits.
- Damaged chimneys started fires while streets were blocked by ice and wreckage.
- Warehouses lost grain, wool, salt, and winter stores.
- The surrounding crops and orchards were beaten flat.
- Old flood-damaged foundations failed under the impacts and sudden weight.
- Ice accumulated in alleys, then meltwater flooded cellars and crypts.

That makes the event more than “hailstones hit people.” For a few minutes, the whole built city becomes a weapon.

## What remains in F.437

Twenty-two years later, the catastrophe can still shape the visible city:

- Roofs are patched with mismatched slate, tile, timber, and scavenged lead.
- Some upper storeys remain abandoned while their ground floors are occupied.
- Hail scars survive on soft limestone, statues, bells, and metal shutters.
- Certain courts are called “widow courts” because nearly every household there lost someone.
- Residents instinctively seek arches and covered passages when skies darken.
- Market awnings are built unusually heavy.
- Property ownership remains tangled because entire households died together.
- The cloth halls never fully recovered from the destruction already mentioned in the chronology.
- Outsiders call Ombreval unlucky; locals resent them while privately agreeing.

The existing F.415 entry—“Hail damages the cloth halls”—could later be revealed as an almost absurd official euphemism. Perhaps the Chapter’s chronicle deliberately minimizes the disaster because its own decisions worsened the death toll.

I’d use these names:

- The Great Rains — F.362 and their aftermath.
- The Great Hail or the Hammering — the immediate F.415 catastrophe.
- The Long Departure — the decades-long demographic decline encompassing both.

“The Hammering” is especially good in ordinary speech:

> “That roof has stood since before the Hammering.”

> “Her mother was killed under slate in the Hammering.”

> “They went downriver in the Long Departure and left us their empty house.”

This preserves the realistic-ish world while giving it one spectacularly strange disaster that nobody can comfortably explain.
